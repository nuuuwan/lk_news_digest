Colombo tipped for bigger role in Asia’s trade and investment flows

Standard Chartered Bank’s India and South Asia CEO sees strong potential for Colombo to become regional centre for trade, treasury and investment

Says India’s expanding manufacturing and investment ecosystem presents significant opportunities for Sri Lanka

Urges fewer non-tariff barriers, simpler procedures and greater focus on high-value exports

Standard Chartered Bank yesterday made a strong case for Colombo to emerge as South Asia’s trade finance hub, with its India and South Asia Chief Executive Officer P. D. Singh identifying a significant opportunity for Sri Lanka to leverage its strategic location and India’s expanding economic footprint to become a key centre for regional commerce.

Singh said Sri Lanka is well placed to capitalise on the growing integration of manufacturing, services, investment and supply chains across Asia, with Colombo having the potential to play a much bigger role in facilitating trade and financial flows within the region.

“I, for one, want to see Colombo emerge as South Asia’s hub for trade finance, so that I can spend more time here. I really do enjoy visiting Sri Lanka and Colombo,” Singh said, underscoring his optimism over the country’s prospects.

Delivering the keynote address at the session titled ‘Linking Sri Lanka to Asia’s Growth Network’ at the Sri Lanka Economic Summit 2026, organised by the Ceylon Chamber of Commerce (CCC), Singh said Sri Lanka had an opportunity to position itself as a regional gateway by building on its existing strengths and tapping into the economic transformation taking place in neighbouring India.

“Looking ahead, four areas stand out, and I think this is very well appreciated already. One is on trade and treasury, where Colombo can become a genuine regional hub at scale, at size, and something that is in the right time zone as well,” he said.

Singh identified global capability centres, high-value analytics and technology services, specialised manufacturing, and port logistics and connectivity as other areas offering considerable potential for Sri Lanka.

He stressed that the opportunities were particularly relevant given the scale and sophistication of India’s rapidly expanding industrial and services ecosystem, which has been built through decades of international investment.

Describing India’s transformation, Singh said the country had emerged as a major manufacturing base, talent pool and destination for global capital, supported by investments from Japan, Korea and other international partners over the past two to three decades.

He pointed out India’s growing presence in global automobile production, smartphone assembly, pharmaceuticals and global capability centres as examples of how deeply the country had become integrated into international production networks.

“These are just small examples of what I’m trying to say is the ecosystem that has developed, with components, engineers, logistics and, of course, financiers that already exist. And this is all in your backyard,” Singh said.

According to him, the significance for Sri Lanka was that companies operating in India were increasingly looking beyond their existing markets for reliable partners, resilient supply chains and opportunities for regional expansion.

“Domestic and international companies operating in India are now looking outward. They’re looking for resilient supply chains. They are looking for partners nearby, and it is almost obvious that when you have a precise, reliable and fast partner close by, it will be easy to make that connection,” he said.

“Proximity is a meaningful advantage, and we are building on that to make sure, as far as we can, as a bank, that we do what is best for the future of business on both sides.”

Singh noted Sri Lanka could also benefit from the growing international ambitions of Indian corporates, many of which required regional bases to support their overseas operations.

“Indian corporates that are looking overseas also need regional bases, and why not Sri Lanka?” he asked.

However, he pointed out that companies seeking opportunities in India needed to appreciate the diversity of the market rather than approach it as a single economic entity.

“As you all probably know, India is not one market. It is an ensemble of different consumption patterns, different markets, and therefore, for each industry, it is best to choose what works best. A strong local partner can probably support,” Singh said.

On the manufacturing front, Singh emphasised that Sri Lanka’s ability to participate in regional and global value chains would depend not only on maintaining product quality but also on meeting internationally recognised certification requirements.

He also highlighted the opportunities in high-value analytics, technology services and global capability centres, where Sri Lanka could build on its skilled workforce to attract international business operations.

Turning to trade policy, Singh called for greater attention to removing non-tariff barriers and simplifying procedures, noting that the nature of international commerce had evolved considerably since the bilateral trade framework between India and Sri Lanka was established in 2000.

“So, looking at fewer non-tariff barriers and simpler procedures is probably something that we would all do well to talk about and see if we can influence in any way.”

Singh said the effectiveness of efforts to strengthen regional trade should ultimately be measured by the ability to bring more businesses into export markets and increase the share of high-value exports.

He suggested that closer commercial integration would require practical measures that made it easier for businesses to access markets, establish partnerships and participate in cross-border trade.

Singh went on to express confidence in Sri Lanka having the foundations and entrepreneurial capabilities to strengthen its position within Asia’s growth network, although the choices made in the coming years would be critical.

“Let me say that the choices we make over the next few years can build on the resilience and the progress we already have. Many of those seated in this room today will actually be shaping the next chapter of what we are likely to see,” he told the fully packed audience.

He reiterated that positioning Colombo as a regional centre for trade finance and using the city as a springboard for growth across South Asia were ambitions he strongly supported.

“Using Colombo as a springboard for growth across the region is another theme that I think is very close to my heart,” Singh said.
