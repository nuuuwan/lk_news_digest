Globally adored hippo Fiona dies after eating a visitor’s hat at Cincinnati Zoo

CNN - Fiona, the universally loved and internationally famous hippopotamus at Cincinnati Zoo, has died at age 9 after ingesting a knit hat that accidentally landed in her habitat, the zoo announced.

The social media darling had received round-the-clock care from the zoo’s veterinary team and care staff, receiving various treatments, but did not survive. Her death has struck a chord with fans throughout Cincinnati and beyond, many of whom watched the hippo grow up.

“We are in shock at the moment,” said Cincinnati Zoo director Dave Jenike in a news release Thursday. “We did not expect this outcome. She fought until she couldn’t anymore.”

Surgery had not been an option for the more than 2,000-pound animal, according to the Cincinnati Zoo, which had consulted with both “veterinary and human specialists.”

“Despite multiple attempts to move the hat out of her system, it was ultimately too much to overcome,” the zoo said. “The team performed necessary supportive care today in an attempt to save her, but it was not successful and she did not recover.”

The knit cap was tossed into the air in front of the habitat more than a week ago and caught by a ceiling fan that catapulted it through a 1-foot opening above the 12-foot-tall viewing windows, zoo communications director Michelle Curley said Tuesday.

Zoo Miami associate veterinarian and hippo expert Dr. Gabriella Flacke, who consulted on Fiona’s case, said intestinal obstructions can occur when animals eat foreign objects.

Fiona previously underwent a colonoscopy, according to the zoo. An ultrasound showed her intestines were bunching, a sign that something was passing through her small intestine.

When an object becomes stuck, “the intestines around that object will start to die, basically, and devitalize because of the pressure of the object in the blockage stretching the intestine to the point that the blood supply goes away,” said Flacke.

“Your gut is a very dynamic thing, and if it gets plugged up like that, it doesn’t take very long for things to go bad,” she added.

Fiona’s care team may have decided against surgery partially because common hippos’ weight and size can make it difficult for surgical incisions to heal, Flacke said. They also have unusually thick skin designed to insulate their bodies and offer protection.

“They just are very specialized, which is great for them in their environment where they need all those adaptations to thrive and survive, but are not really geared toward a major surgery,” Flacke said.

Fiona became an international celebrity when she was born six weeks prematurely in 2017, at a record-low weight of only 29 pounds. Hippos are typically born closer to 100 pounds, but Fiona managed to survive against all odds.

So small she could barely walk and unable to stand long enough to nurse from her mother Bibi, the baby animal had several zoo staff pressed into service to care for her, forming a care team they began to call “Team Fiona.” They were joined by nurses from the nearby Cincinnati Children’s Hospital, experts at finding veins and caring for tiny beings.

Before long, Team Fiona grew beyond a team and into a hashtag.

As Fiona put on weight and her health improved, the zoo’s social media team began putting pictures and videos of the baby hippo online to let people follow her journey. The #TeamFiona hashtag began filling up social media with official updates from the zoo and requests and well wishes from fans all over the world.

The sporadic updates became daily as Fiona’s fandom grew. Videos shared on social media showing keepers reading Fiona some of her fan mail or of her falling asleep in the shower went viral.

#TeamFiona became a full-on brand for Cincinnati Zoo, filling its shop with accompanying merchandise, from magnets to T-shirts. The city rallied behind their new four-legged mascot too, a local brewery creating a special beer in honor of the hippo and local clothing company Cincy Shirts making a special Fiona T-shirt, with proceeds going to the zoo.

The hippopotamus drew thousands of people from around the world to the zoo, with travelers from as far away as Australia, Germany, Brazil and Japan visiting Cincinnati, Ohio, to see the globally adored ungulate.

At 9 years old, Fiona’s death was considered early for a common hippopotamus, whose lifespan in captivity usually ranges from about 40 to 50 years, according to Flacke.

It is not the Cincinnati Zoo’s first time experiencing the death of an animal celebrity. Harambe, a 17-year-old western lowland gorilla, was killed there in 2016 in an attempt by the zoo to save a boy who slipped into the animal’s enclosure. The internet erupted with outrage and cries of “justice for Harambe” after the incident.

Fiona’s tragic death has sparked a similar outpouring of grief across social media, uniting everyone from Cincinnati sports teams to city and state lawmakers.

“Fiona was a symbol of perseverance and joy” in the city, said Cincinnati Mayor Aftab Pureval in a Thursday afternoon X post.

Ohio Gov. Mike DeWine and Gov.-elect Amy Acton offered similar sentiments, with Acton calling the hippo “one of Cincinnati’s most beloved icons.”

The community left pink and white flower bouquets and cards honoring Fiona leaning against a statue of the beloved hippo and her mother outside the zoo, video from CNN affiliate WKRC shows. A card — with a drawing of Fiona, a heart and childlike handwriting — read, “Hope that you get well soon friend.”

A man wearing a grey sweatshirt with a graphic of Fiona wearing a crown and a purple robe told WKRC he would walk through the zoo while recovering from a knee replacement and a heart surgery.

“Fiona was always my destination on those walks,” he said. “Her grit, how she went and would make herself available to her fans, her ear wiggle … Just the way that she held herself. She was the face of Cincinnati Zoo.”

“Fiona was such a gift,” said Cincinnati Zoo Executive Chair of the Board Thane Maynard. “I have never known an animal so beloved in all my life. From the day she was born, she was like lightning in a bottle. And, she will be remembered and beloved many years from now.”

Video posted by the zoo Wednesday showed Fiona grunting and walking around her pen as she stuck her nose into the camera and wiggled her ears. This was an apparent improvement from video posted a day earlier showing the hippo sedated with a tube inserted in her nose and a towel covering her eyes.

Although this brought hope to many, “recovery isn’t always a straight line,” American Veterinary Medical Association president Dr. Jennifer Quammen told CNN.

Animals can show encouraging signs while still facing complications, she said. “Even with careful monitoring, appropriate treatment, and the dedicated efforts of veterinary teams, some animals develop complications they cannot overcome.”

Fiona’s death, while seemingly the result of an accident, is a reminder for zoo visitors to secure their personal belongings, respect safety barriers and be mindful of animals’ spaces, Quammen said. “Something that seems harmless to us can have devastating consequences for an animal.”
