Dengue breeding grounds differ across areas

Colombo, Oct. 3 (Daily Mirror) - Breeding grounds for dengue mosquitoes vary from one area to another, with local conditions determining the types of sites where mosquitoes predominantly breed, according to Dr Prashila Samaraweera, Consultant Specialist at the National Dengue Control Unit.

Speaking to Daily Mirror, Dr Samaraweera said dengue mosquitoes commonly breed in water accumulated in discarded items, broadly categorised as degradable waste, non-degradable waste and items temporarily set aside before disposal.

“Water can accumulate in and around homes, including in refrigerator trays, while broken and discarded material can collect water and become mosquito breeding sites. Items, such as paint buckets, are kept for possible reuse and can become breeding sites when they collect water,” Dr Samaraweera said.

Dr Samaraweera also highlighted how prominent mosquito breeding grounds differ from one area to another, depending on local conditions and water management practices. In Batticaloa and Kalmunai, for instance, shallow wells can serve as potential breeding sites, while in Gampaha and Kalutara Districts, pineapple plantations and coconut shells used to collect latex in rubber plantations provide suitable conditions for mosquito breeding. She also noted that certain water management systems in some areas can create conditions conducive to mosquito breeding.

“Mosquitoes carrying the dengue virus breed in clean water. By clean water, we mean clear water and not necessarily potable water,” Dr Samaraweera further said.

In terms of dengue management, Dr Samaraweera said Sri Lanka has adopted aspects of Thailand’s model, noting that Thailand has a strong dengue management system. She said both countries have strong dengue management systems in the South Asian and Southeast Asian regions.

On case classification, she said that in Sri Lanka, a case can be classified as suspected dengue when two symptoms are present, while some countries use different criteria and place greater emphasis on confirmed cases. She said these differences in surveillance and case classification should be taken into account when comparing dengue figures between countries, as reported case numbers can reflect not only disease transmission but also the criteria used to identify and record suspected and confirmed cases.
