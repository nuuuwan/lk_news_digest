AKD’s two years and beyond

President Anura Kumara Dissanayake completes two years in office today, seemingly with satisfaction of his thus far performance and perhaps with uncertainty over the future.

Although former Presidents Ranasinghe Premadasa and Maithripala Sirisena also hailed from ordinary backgrounds, they had been elevated to the elite class when they assumed power. However, it was Dissanayake who will go down in history as the first leader to come from both a working-class family and a remarkably humble background even when assuming power.

In fact, the rise of the National People’s Power (NPP), Dissanayake’s party,  is in a way accidental and it was former President Gotabaya Rajapaksa and his political clan that created a conducive environment for it.

Marxism, Dissanayake’s original political doctrine,  observes that revolutions cannot be won without a revolutionary environment being created by the history. Gotabaya created it, while his brother Mahinda Rajapaksa — who boasted about his experience in governance — praised it during an interview with the Daily Mirror, calling it a fantastic job.

Pressured by the miles-long and weeks-long queues for fuel and cooking gas and a thirteen-hour power cut as a result of  mismanagement by the Gotabaya Rajapaksa government, millions of people marched to the capital on April, 2022 and camped in front of President’s office, ultimately forcing him to flee  the country.

Although International Monetary Fund (IMF) guided the country towards a relative recovery during the Ranil Wickremesinghe administration, people’s anger towards the highly corrupt Rajapaksa regime, and later towards Wickremesinghe for collaborating with the Rajapaksas,  created the “revolutionary environment” favourabe to the NPP, at the next Presidential and general elections in 2024.

Despite Opposition parties’ criticism over the inexperience of the NPP in governance, Dissanayake through his pragmatism has overcome many challenges so far. Shedding his party’s long standing dogmatic stance on the IMF, he brought forward the latter’s Extended Fund Facility (EFF) programme initiated by Gotabaya Rajapaksa and continued by Ranil Wickremesinghe.

He swiftly grasped that  macroeconomic issues are the prime concerns that have to be addressed first instead of hurriedly implementing his election pledges. Hence, disregarding constant attacks by the Opposition, certain NGOs and  social media over going back on its promises, the NPP government broadened the tax base and increased certain indirect and withholding tax rates while providing income tax relief by raising the tax-free threshold.

The finance ministry under  Dissanayake’s close watch kept a close tab on Sri Lanka Customs, the Department of Excise, and the Inland Revenue Department (IRD), the three principal agencies responsible for state tax and revenue collection, which resulted in the increase of state revenue considerably. This helped the government to face  Cyclone Ditwah, the unprecedented natural disaster that hit the whole country in November last year and the West Asian crisis.

Conversely, disappointing  Opposition political parties which predicted an economic collapse under the “inexperienced” NPP administration,  Sri Lanka earned a record US$ 11.6 billion in 2025 from tourism, foreign remittances and consular services,  and there are signs that those revenues taking a further upward trend this year. Export revenue in 2026 is also tracking a strong growth. These achievements are encouraging against the backdrop of the government’s attempts to eradicate corruption and waste.

On the political front, however, the NPP’s record remains dismal and highly questionable; its core pledges—namely enacting a new Constitution and holding provincial council elections—appear far from seeing the light of day. It may be not a matter of insincerity, but rather an attempt to avoid a threat to the survival of its government.

NPP is acquainted with the fact that those who voted for it the last time did so on impulse and not with political awareness. Therefore, fearing that constitution-making could hit the rocks — since any solution to the ethnic problem incorporated in it would inevitably divide his vote bank — Dissanayake seems to want to put off the issue until his second term, or even indefinitely. Similarly,  last year’s local government election results would compel the government to further defer the Provincial council elections.

However, the political trajectory of the country beyond the next Presidential election totally depends on whether the masses have realised the difference between political objectives of the NPP and the other parties.
