Budget offers no plan to lift working people out of poverty- Communist Party

Colombo, Nov. 25 (Daily Mirror) - The Budget for 2027, presented to Parliament on 12 November 2026, is a status-quo budget which meant to manage the economy within the IMF framework, yet without any plan to lift people out of poverty, the Communist Party of Sri Lanka said.

The party said the budget manages the economy within the IMF framework and continues the liberal reform agenda, but it offers no plan to lift working people out of poverty or to transform the economy. It was attempted to draw a beautiful picture by presenting macro-economic indicators, yet ordinary people of the country are hard hit and unable to meet the minimum daily living.

The party in a statement signed by its General Secretary Dr. G. Weerasinghe, said, “The economy has stabilised even from the previous government’s time. Growth reached 5 per cent in 2025 and the deficit has narrowed. But these gains were paid for by workers, farmers and the poor. The budget does not say who will now share in the recovery.

The Budget Speech offers almost no analysis of the world in which Sri Lanka must make its way. It mentions the war in West Asia mainly as a cause of higher fuel prices.

Yet the world is in upheaval. The wars in Ukraine, Palestine, Iran and Sudan are killing hundreds of thousands and disrupting the global trade in food, fuel and fertiliser. By the government’s own account, the closure of the Strait of Hormuz alone cost Sri Lanka more than Rs. 147 billion.

The Speech is silent on the rise of unilateralism and protectionism in the Global North, where tariffs, sanctions and export controls are now used as weapons. It is equally silent on the shift of the world economy’s centre of gravity to Asia. Trade agreements with China and India and an application to join RCEP are listed as separate items, not as a strategy.

South–South cooperation, through BRICS, the New Development Bank and regional partnerships, offers developing countries alternatives to IMF conditionality and dependence on Western markets. The budget does not even raise the question.

A large section of our people lives in poverty and cannot afford food or medicine. Inflation rose from 2.1 per cent in December 2025 to 8.3 per cent in October 2026, eating into wages and pensions. Yet:

-Social protection, including Aswesuma, receives Rs. 230 billion, against Rs. 2,643 billion for interest payments.

Cuts in the levy on sprats and Maldive fish are positive, but insignificant. What is missing is a real poverty alleviation programme, with targets, a timeline and a clear account of who is poor and how they will be lifted out.

The budget continues the path of IMF stabilisation and liberal reform. Its central targets, a primary surplus of 2.3 per cent of GDP and a cap on primary spending, are the targets of austerity. Interest still takes about 43 per cent of government revenue and together with principal payments, it adds up to 78.5% of the same.

The reform agenda is familiar: a Public-Private Partnership Act, a holding company for state enterprises, an Investment Protection Bill, labour law reform, private partners for the Sapugaskanda refinery and Mattala airport, and tariff cuts on 800 product lines.

Stability is necessary, but it is not a development strategy. The Speech admits that the external sector has not been strengthened. The current account swung from a surplus of USD 1,853 million in the first nine months of 2025 to a deficit of USD 420 million in the same period of 2026. Trade deficit for the first nine months of 2026 was 7.9 billion USD compared to 5.2 billion USD for the same period in 2025. Opening the economy further, before building domestic production, will widen the gaps.

The 7 percent growth target cannot be met without an industrial policy that selects strategic industries and commits the state to building them. The budget offers only small schemes: industrial estates, concessional loans and tax allowances for large investors. For graphite, mineral sands, silica and quartz, the plan is simply to “identify a suitable investor”, which risks yet more exports of raw materials.

We welcome the rise in public investment to Rs. 2,000 billion, including about Rs. 100 billion for rural roads and Rs. 80 billion for railway electrification. But roads and railways alone will not industrialise the country. Public investment must be tied to domestic production through local content rules, local manufacture of buses, rolling stock and energy equipment, and development finance for chosen sectors. The Asian economies that industrialised did so through planning, directed credit and the protection of new industries, not by cutting tariffs and waiting for investors.

Indirect taxes make up about 76 per cent of tax revenue. Every family pays VAT and SSCL on what it buys, whatever its income. Yet more than half of the budget’s Rs. 28.4 billion in tax concessions is income tax relief for those earning over Rs. 150,000 a month, from which the poor gain nothing. Business gains the abolition of the Tourism Development Levy and new tax allowances.

Indirect taxes on the poor should be reduced. The tax net should be widened to reach the wealthiest, especially in the informal sector, where many pay little or nothing. With revenue set to fall to 15.8 percent of GDP, below the government’s own floor of 16 percent, the money for relief must come from taxing wealth.

1.Adopt a foreign economic policy for a multipolar world, based on non-alignment and South–South cooperation.

2.Launch a national poverty alleviation programme and bring down the prices of food and medicine.

3.Adopt an industrial policy that names strategic industries and backs them with public investment and directed credit.

4.Tie public investment in infrastructure to domestic production and employment.

5.Reduce indirect taxes on essentials and widen the tax net to reach the wealthiest.

The people gave this government a mandate for change. Stability on the IMF’s terms is not the change they voted for. The CPSL will support every measure that benefits working people, and oppose every measure that places the burden of recovery on their shoulders,”
