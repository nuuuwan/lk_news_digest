Current  global crisis a testing time for Sri Lanka –Dr. Saman Weerasinghe

The war in the Middle East has unleashed unbearable consequences for the world in terms of high fuel costs, disruption to navigation, etc.

It is high time now for the development of new energy projects to enhance security in that perspective, as otherwise the country is susceptible to volatility in the Middle East

To achieve long-term economic stability, Sri Lanka must urgently focus on boosting exports and expanding product diversification beyond traditional apparel and tea

Dr. Saman Weerasinghe, former Ambassador of Sri Lanka to the Russian Federation and Chairman of the board of Lay Trustees of Kelaniya Rajamaha Viharaya, shares his views Daily Mirror regarding the current global conflict situation and how Sri Lanka should navigate complexities in the advancement of its interests. Following are the excerpts of the interview -

QAs a former ambassador, how do you see the unfolding circumstances of the world?

World history has been replete with conflicts. Power struggle has been the norm of the international order. It is natural that every country operates with the assumption that conflicts may occur at any time. What has happened now in the Middle East, Ukraine-Russia or elsewhere in the world amply describes the conflictual nature of the world. In human history, conflicts have stood in the way of progress or the realisation of the total potential of global economic development for the well-being of the entire mankind. The tools developed in the wake of World War II are not deterrent enough to prevent war. Today, the war in the Middle East has unleashed unbearable consequences for the world in terms of high fuel costs, disruption to navigation, etc. Global security is at stake. The prolonged Ukraine-Russia war is bound to trigger a food crisis. The two countries are responsible for the supply of a bulk of wheat products to the world market. The current petroleum crisis will also result in hampering fertiliser production. It is then a hit on agriculture.

I, as a former diplomat, see new security dynamics being formed as the available arrangements have failed to prevent conflicts. Saudi Arabia has teamed up with Türkiye and Pakistan, and formed the Mecca Agreement since it realised that overall reliance on the U.S. does not bode well for security in the region, as evidenced in today’s context.

When two elephants fight, the grass gets crushed. As such, small and medium powers such as Sri Lanka should read the scenario carefully and strategise accordingly for their security in a broader sense.

It is not only security from a military perspective, but also from energy, food, digital and other aspects. Neutrality is fundamental in the exercise of foreign policy. Yet, it is not that the current government should follow neutrality by its dictionary meaning. It is strategic, dynamic neutrality that matters in the current context.

It is high time now for the development of new energy projects to enhance security in that perspective, as otherwise the country is susceptible to volatility in the Middle East. Sri Lanka has high potential for solar and wind energy. That potential should be harnessed forthwith, and the country should advance e-mobility instead of depending on fossil fuels. It is a valuable lesson taught by the current conflicts in the world.

Again, geopolitically, it is a testing time for Sri Lanka. In a global crisis situation, the parties test who their friends or enemies are. For example, both the United States and Iran test Sri Lanka’s foreign policy. Obviously, the United States, in this instance, has more levers in prevailing upon Sri Lanka because of economic ties. Around 20 Iranian vessels that are stranded off Sri Lankan waters have reportedly sought assistance. According to the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), it may require Sri Lanka to extend assistance, but practically it cannot risk American sanctions.

Sri Lanka should diversify its economy. Today, the United States is responsible for 23% of Sri Lankan exports. Overreliance on a single market is not advisable for any country in the world. It is regrettable that the present government has not prioritised trade negotiations with India and ASEAN. Also, Sri Lanka should join the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) - the world’s largest free trade zone covering 15 countries, including China, Japan and Australia. If signed, it is a godsend for Sri Lanka. The need is amply felt in the current context.

To achieve long-term economic stability, Sri Lanka must urgently focus on boosting exports and expanding product diversification beyond traditional apparel and tea. Generating sustained foreign currency inflows is vital to strengthen the Sri Lankan rupee against external shocks and reduce reliance on emergency borrowing. Expanding high-value service exports such as IT, logistics, and tourism alongside higher value - added goods will directly bolster the current account balance within Sri Lanka’s Balance of Payments (BoP). A resilient current account surplus provides the ultimate shield against global energy spikes, debt pressures, and geopolitical volatility.

QSri Lanka is a strategically located country. How should Sri Lanka position itself in terms of foreign policy?

First and foremost, Sri Lanka should read its geography properly. I wonder whether it has been done by any government or leader. In global security, geography matters more than anything. Today, despite its military might, the United States is unable to overpower Iran, which wields control over the Strait of Hormuz. It is a key chokepoint for global fuel transportation. Nearly 18 million barrels per day (bpd) pass through this strait.

Missile attacks by Yemen’s Houthi militants on maritime vessels in the Red Sea have roiled international shipping and altered the security landscape for this vital waterway connecting Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. Sri Lanka sits along one of the world’s busiest East-West maritime shipping corridors. Over 60 per cent of container traffic takes place through this route. It is in close proximity to the world’s key markets like India, China and some emerging countries in the ASEAN region like Viet Nam, Thailand and Indonesia. The country should realise the economic benefits on offer from this positioning. Then, it is a better lever for Sri Lanka to deal with the rest of the world for its benefits. India is critically important for Sri Lanka because of its close proximity and civilisational and cultural linkages. While maintaining its ties with India in the proper manner, Sri Lanka should move with other countries. China stands out as a special partner for trade and economic cooperation. With Russia, the total potential of economic cooperation is yet to be realised. The country should move closer to ASEAN. Sri Lanka should further its relations with middle powers such as Australia, South Africa, Kenya, Türkiye and others. Then, it has more space to bargain in dealing with major powers having competing interests in the world. As a maritime nation, Sri Lanka should strengthen its naval power to protect these sea-lanes and for disaster response. Once it is in possession of a powerful navy with air surveillance capacity, the country will have strength in the exercise of its neutrality.

QWhy has Sri Lanka been unable to realise its full potential for economic development?

In the realisation of the fullest possible benefits from foreign relations for economic development, Sri Lanka should get its act together domestically. Lack or absence of policy consistency right throughout its post-independence history has prevented Sri Lanka from becoming a developed nation. At times, the foreign policies of the political parties remained poles apart. What one government does is completely undone by the other. Domestically, the parties can do politics, but foreign policy should not vary too widely from each other. It is important because foreign policy is no longer traditional diplomacy. It is always linked to trade, investment, technology, tourism, energy, logistics and, finally, access to global value chains. Also, Sri Lanka has so far failed to realise its areas of strength. Its relevance remains today as a maritime hub and a service hub.

Sri Lanka is blessed with an educated workforce. The International Monetary Fund has identified information technology, business-process outsourcing and tourism as sectors with potential to become important service exports. Instead of viewing these sectors separately, Sri Lanka could position itself as a regional services and connectivity hub. Sri Lanka has failed to discern its areas of strength and make proper use of its foreign policy. Domestic politics should not bar the implementation of a rightly identified foreign policy.

Non-alignment has been the trademark of Sri Lanka’s foreign policy. It is a concept developed in the wake of World War II. The developing countries took initial steps to advance their interests without being aligned with either of the two power blocs – one led by the United States of America and the other by the Soviet Union. Sri Lanka played a leading role in the Non-Aligned Movement, particularly under the leadership of Mrs Sirimavo Bandaranaike. Its conceptual meaning stays relevant even today. Today, there are multiple alliances in the world. Sri Lanka, as a country, should choose what is best for it. It is an issue-based approach. Given its positioning, it has to deal with all. India, China, Japan, the United States, Europe and Russia are equally important for Sri Lanka. Also, Sri Lanka should look at Africa as an emerging region with a lot on offer in terms of trade and investment. Accordingly, it is nothing but friendly with all, inimical with none. It can have friendly relations with all concerned but in different equations, though.

QYou served in Russia as the ambassador. Where should Russia feature in Sri Lanka’s foreign policy equation?

Russia should have a place in Sri Lanka’s foreign-policy equation, given its critical support at challenging times. When the Western powers stood against Sri Lanka during the wartime, Russia came forward for Sri Lanka. Sri Lanka has had excellent ties with Russia. Now, the relationship has to be viewed within the broader context of Sri Lanka’s national interests. Foreign relations, anyway, keep evolving. Today, the relationship continues in tourism, trade, education and other areas. There is no reason for Sri Lanka to abandon this relationship with a country simply because the geopolitical environment has changed.

At the same time, Sri Lanka should strike the necessary balance. We have to maintain good relations with all major powers, particularly India, China, the United States, the European Union and Japan.

Unfortunately, the Russia-Ukraine conflict has complicated the international environment. Strategic balance and open dialogue will pave the way for better ties with all concerned. Sri Lankan interests should be realised without compromising relations with any.

QWhat is your view on the importance of Sri Lanka’s improved connectivity with the region?

Connectivity is key in today’s world. The more deeply countries are connected, in areas like tourism, energy and people-to-people links, the greater the potential economic benefits. Regional connectivity is best. It is proposed that the two power grids of India and Sri Lanka should be connected for bilateral trading of electricity. It is an economically viable idea that should be realised. Yet, Sri Lanka should not lower its guard over its control of domestic energy security.

India is a major power today. It is one of the key economic powerhouses in the world. Naturally, connectivity with India through trade is beneficial for Sri Lanka, for sure. It is important to further enhance people-to-people ties with the region.

While maintaining better ties with India, Sri Lanka should also look at Pakistan, a traditional friend of Sri Lanka. With the presence of a large middle class, Sri Lanka can do a lot more in trading with Pakistan. There is a current Free Trade Agreement in operation. It should be expanded now in the current circumstances. In Bangladesh, a lot of Sri Lankan companies have invested. With Nepal, Bhutan and the Maldives, relations remain robust. But enhanced regional connectivity can bring further benefits and offer better levers on the global stage. SAARC is virtually a dead letter over differences between Pakistan and India. BIMSTEC is in operation sans Pakistan. I believe Sri Lanka is the only neutral country. As such, in regional integration, Sri Lanka can even play a stabilising, mediatory role. But things look complicated because of animosity between India and Pakistan.

Regional cooperation is needed in countering terrorism and extremism. Sri Lanka is better positioned to be a unifying force.

QWhat are the biggest challenges facing diplomacy in the 21st century?

Today, diplomacy finds its areas of coverage vastly expanded. It cannot keep pace with the speed of change. With the development of AI, fresh opportunities and challenges have emerged. Existing tools look exhausted in the prevention of conflicts. The rise of conservative forces in the world and the change in the aspirations of new generations have challenged politics in many countries. Diplomacy has to cope with all these developments.

Actually speaking, we are living in a much more contested world. Diplomacy has to deal with economic security, trade, technology, cyber security, climate change, energy, food security and migration. Countering harmful information (misinformation and disinformation) has become part of the diplomatic agenda. Today’s diplomats should be multi-skilled. It is high time that Sri Lanka establishes a proper institute that produces world-class diplomats who can steer Sri Lanka through these challenges successfully. It is an area with a lacuna today. Sri Lanka needs a highly skilled diplomatic corps.

QHow do economic interests, trade, and investment influence relationships between countries?

Naturally, relations in trade and investment bring countries closer together. At times, nations even set aside deep political differences because economic interests take precedence over purely geopolitical conflicts. The best example is U.S.- China relations: despite being major geopolitical rivals, the two economic powerhouses are deeply interdependent, as seen when Chinese President Xi Jinping was welcomed by U.S. President Donald Trump in Washington, D.C. Similarly, a thaw in ties occurred between India and China when Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi and President Xi met at the BRICS summit and decided to move forward on economic cooperation while managing border tensions.

In high-stakes geopolitics, economic and security imperatives regularly override ideological divisions. This was further demonstrated during the August 2025 Russia - United States summit at Joint Base Elmendorf-Richardson in Anchorage, Alaska, where U.S. President Donald Trump hosted Russian President Vladimir Putin to pursue peace negotiations over the Ukraine war, underscoring a pragmatic willingness to engage directly despite intense global opposition.  Simultaneously, historical memory and economic-strategic ties continue to steer foreign policy over rigid alliances. Speaking at the 62nd Munich Security Conference meeting in New Delhi, India’s External Affairs Minister S. Jaishankar stressed that at a time when much of the world preferred backing a military regime next to India, Russia was a country with which India’s relations were very good. He noted that India-Russia relations are shaped by that legacy in many ways, while highlighting that the bilateral relationship has widened into a strong economic partnership as well. All in all, economic diplomacy and national interest remain the true driving forces of international relations.

Sri Lanka should regain its proactive role in the Global South. It should be connected with like-minded countries more and more. I wonder why the current government, despite its positive rhetoric, did not attend the 18th BRICS Summit, which was held on 12–13 September 2026 in New Delhi, India, at Bharat Mandapam. It is a platform with China, India and Russia on board, in addition to many other small and medium powers. China and India have competing interests in Sri Lanka. Then, BRICS will be a better place to reconcile interests. Countries such as South Africa and Brazil are also its members.

At the same time, Sri Lanka must expand its engagement beyond traditional frameworks by tapping into new economic initiatives across the Indo-Pacific. Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney has rolled out an aggressive trade push targeting Southeast Asia as part of Ottawa’s broader strategy to diversify trade away from single-market reliance. With Canada negotiating new trade frameworks with ASEAN and committing multi-billion-dollar investments in regional infrastructure, energy, and digital technology, Sri Lanka should actively position itself to participate in these Southeast Asian economic expansion programs. Engaging with partners across both the Global South and middle-power initiatives allows Sri Lanka to maximise its strategic leverage and tap into fresh capital and trade networks.
