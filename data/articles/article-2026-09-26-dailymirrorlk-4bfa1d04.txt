Regulation or control? NGO Bill puts civic space under scrutiny: TPA’s Barath

Colombo, Sep 26 (Daily Mirror) - The proposed NGO law could weaken organisations that help citizens challenge government decisions, defend their rights and obtain essential assistance, Democratic People’s Front Vice President for International Affairs and TPA Politburo Member Barath Arullsamy warned.

Arullsamy said the circulated draft Non-Governmental Organisations (Registration and Supervision) law would extend compulsory registration across a broad range of civil society organisations, require renewal every three years and give a minister-appointed authority powers to inspect, suspend or deregister organisations. It also requires NGOs to “align with the policies of the Government”.

“Consider an organisation helping families challenge a government land decision. If it must align with government policy, can it defend those families without fearing consequences? That uncertainty alone can discourage people from speaking out,” he said.

“Repeated compliance costs could also leave small organisations with fewer resources for legal assistance, women’s protection or disaster relief. Ultimately, the public could lose both practical support and an independent voice.”

The NGO Secretariat’s official July 24 statement says the Bill aims to strengthen regulation and reduce money-laundering, terrorist-financing and proliferation-financing risks. It also records consultations and an invitation for written civil society submissions.

Arullsamy said preventing financial crime and terrorist financing were legitimate regulatory objectives, but did not explain why independent organisations should be required to align with government policy.

Sri Lanka already has the 1980 legislation governing voluntary social service organisations, the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, the Financial Transactions Reporting Act and the Convention on the Suppression of Terrorist Financing Act. The three financial laws were also amended in 2026.

“The Government must identify what these existing laws cannot address and justify each additional power. If financial crime is the problem, why make agreement with government policy part of the solution?”

He pointed out that the Financial Action Task Force’s Recommendation 8 calls for proportionate measures based on identified terrorist-financing risks while protecting legitimate nonprofit activity.

At the Sri Lanka CSO FATF Network’s September 17 event, UN Resident Coordinator Marc-André Franche warned that blanket restrictions could undermine security and development. A Global NPO Coalition co-chair also warned that excessive banking caution could delay humanitarian funding.

“We should not confuse effective financial regulation with blanket control of civil society. FATF itself has moved towards a targeted and proportionate approach. Sri Lanka should be careful not to go further than what genuine financial-security risks require,” Arullsamy said.

He recalled the Defence Ministry’s 2014 restrictions on NGO media activities, including press conferences, workshops, journalist training and the dissemination of press releases, on the basis that such activities were considered beyond their mandate, when Gotabaya Rajapaksa was Defence Secretary.

During the good governance era, Mano Ganesan withdrew contested NGO amendments during his ministerial tenure.

“During Leader Mano Ganesan’s ministerial tenure, many organisations, including Amnesty International, secured NGO registration after previously being refused under the earlier Rajapaksa administration. That was an opening of democratic space. Regulation must not become a mechanism through which governments decide what civil society may say, advocate or question,” Arullsamy said.

He urged scrutiny of the NGO proposal alongside the 22nd Amendment and proposed counterterrorism legislation, warning against the cumulative weakening of democratic safeguards.

He called for the removal of the policy-alignment requirement, simpler obligations for small organisations, meaningful public consultation and effective judicial safeguards against arbitrary interference.

“The two-thirds mandate carried an expectation of democratic change. Gotabaya Rajapaksa also had a supermajority, yet public protests during the 2022 economic crisis forced him from office. An iron fist proved a poor substitute for public trust,” he said.
