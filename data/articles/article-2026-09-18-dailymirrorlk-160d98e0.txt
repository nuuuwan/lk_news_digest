Unpaid care work drives women out of jobs

The Government has identified unpaid and unequal 'care responsibilities' as a major reason why women leave the workforce, saying families and the economy cannot reach their full potential unless care work is recognised as a shared responsibility.

Deputy Minister of Women and Child Affairs said the issue was particularly important in Sri Lanka, where women continue to face difficulties balancing employment with childcare, caring for elderly family members, household duties and other unpaid care work.

“When we ask why women leave the workforce, why families struggle to balance work and home, or why opportunities remain unequal, we often arrive at the same answer: care,” the Deputy Minister said.

Addressing a regional gathering in Seoul on transforming care systems for women's economic empowerment, she said care work was often ignored and undervalued, despite being essential to families, workplaces and economies.

“Every success story is also a care story,” she said, noting that behind every child who learns, every worker who contributes and every leader who serves, there is often someone providing care.

The Deputy Minister said care should no longer be seen mainly as a responsibility for women, but as a shared responsibility of families, communities, employers and the State.

She said Sri Lanka was taking steps to develop a more inclusive and effective social care system, with the National Social Protection Strategy identifying social care as a priority.

A National Action Plan on Social Care has also been developed and is awaiting Ministerial approval, she said.

The Government's long-term goal is to make care an important part of national plans, programmes and budgets, while reducing the burden on families and improving women's access to employment and economic opportunities.

The Deputy Minister said changes in social attitudes were also needed, along with greater investment in childcare, eldercare and services for persons with disabilities.

Sri Lanka has strengthened its commitment through regional cooperation, including participation earlier this year in the SAARC Transform Care South Asia Dialogue in Nepal, organised by SAARC and UN Women with the support of the Republic of Korea.

The dialogue contributed to a regional Action Plan on transforming care systems.

The Deputy Minister said countries in the region could learn from each other's experiences in areas such as childcare, eldercare, disability inclusion, public investment, data systems and social attitudes.

“No country has all the answers,” she said, stressing the importance of regional cooperation in finding practical solutions.

She said Sri Lanka was ready to work with the Republic of Korea, UN Women and regional partners to strengthen care systems.

“If care makes our economies possible, sustains our families, and advances women’s empowerment, then the question before us is not whether we can afford to invest in care. It is whether we can afford not to,” she said.
