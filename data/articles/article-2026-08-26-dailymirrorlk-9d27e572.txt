Let’s Not Bring Back The Death Penalty By  Any Means

The prison cells shown here house prisoners who are on death row at Welikada Prison which also contains the gallows used when death sentences were carried out

But, seen in the modern context, hanging (or the firing squad, lethal injection or the electric chair) can be just as barbaric

Talk of reviving the death penalty is back again, and this concerns not just the 800 plus people on ‘death row,’ with sentences commuted to life imprisonment. It concerns all of us as citizens of a civilised nation.

Eight hundred is a high figure if we compare with the United States. The US with its population of 343 million has close to 2000 people under death sentence, only two and half times as much,  though its population is nearly fifteen times bigger than ours. Worldwide, the figure tops 25,000.

But most countries have now abolished the death penalty. The majority of the 193 UN member states have either fully abolished capital punishment or maintain a legal moratorium against executions, while around 55 UN member countries still practise legal executions.

No country can be called uncivilised for carrying out the death penalty. But there has to be a soul-searching debate regarding this much abhorred practise in any country which considers itself civilised.

Executing people for various crimes was standard practise all over the world until a few centuries ago, and people were killed in horrible ways. Venezuela is recognised as the first country to abolish the death penalty by presidential decree in 1863, the first country to do so. In 1786, Grand Duke Peter Leopold of Tuscany issued a penal reform ending the death penalty locally, but Tuscany is not an independent country. In 1849,  San Marino abolished the death penalty for common crimes, (fully in 1865), though it was actually  suspended there  since 1468, hence not carried out.

In  1865,  Portugal became the first European country to abolish capital punishment  for all crimes. Interestingly, in ancient times, financial restitution,  or banishment, were seen as alternatives to capital punishment. In ancient Israel, under strict Talmudic law, the legal hurdles required to execute someone were so high that executions became virtually impossible in practice. Modern Israel retains the death penalty for crimes like treason, but there have been only two executions since 1948. But Israel’s parliament passed a controversial law in March 2026 legalising death by hanging for Palestinians convicted in military courts.

Influenced by Buddhist teachings and imperial policy, ancient Japan officially suspended capital punishment in 810 AD,  and this lasted nearly 350 years. Unfortunately, modern Japan continues to exercise the death penalty, resuming executions last year after nearly three years. On June 27, 2025,  Takahiro Shiraishi, widely known as the “Twitter Killer,” was hanged for the 2017 murder, dismemberment, and sexual abuse of nine people he met online.

This marked the end of a 2-year, 11-month hiatus – the longest pause since Japan’s Ministry of Justice began publicly disclosing the names and crimes of death row inmates in 2007. This resumption is unfortunate,  since  89-year-old Hakamada Iwao – once the world’s longest-serving death row inmate who spent  nearly 50 years in prison --  was finally aquitted last year. After spending years in solitary confinement, Iawo had lost the power of speech.

Among wealthy, highly developed G7 nations, only the United States and Japan still retain and actively use the death penalty. Other  high-income democracies like Canada, Australia, and all of Western Europe have completely abolished it. China, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Iraq and Somalia are prominent among the small group of countries which still practise the death penalty.  Russia, though not considered a democracy,  has had an indefinite moratorium on the death penalty since the late 1990s, and courts do not sentence people to death.

In China, convicts are executed by firing squad or lethal injection. In Iran, Iraq and Somalia, it is by hanging or firing squad. In Saudi Arabia, the legal method of execution is hanging, though beheading is often carried out.  In Afghanistan under the Taleban, execution is by firing squad and is carried out publicly.

In the US. 23 states have abolished the death penalty, while 27 states have capital punishment laws. But four of these – California, Ohio, Oregon, and Pennsylvania – do not actively carry out death warrants. The common method of execution is lethal injection, using a traditional multi-drug cocktail or a single massive dose of a powerful sedative. But there is also Nitrogen Hypoxia, forcing the victim to breathe pure nitrogen through a mask, causing suffocation. Electrocution is the third method used.

Until a few centuries ago, barbaric methods of torture were used to execute people all over the world. It is hard to say just when death by hanging became widely accepted almost everywhere as a more ‘civilised’ method of execution. But we inherited this practise from the British (though the Dutch used this method before them). Ancient Sri Lanka used beheading and often barbaric methods of execution. As far as we know, death by hanging wasn’t standard practise under the kings.

But, seen in the modern context, hanging (or the firing squad, lethal injection or the electric chair) can be just as barbaric. There is no need to argue if being hanged is less barbaric, or painful, than being impaled on a stake, crucified,  or being beheaded (the British carried out beheadings too, in 19th century Sri Lanka, for e.g., the beheading of Keppetipola Disawa). It could be less painful than methods common in ancient times, but it is still very painful. Experts say that the victim feels acute pain for about two minutes during a hanging.

No one knows exactly. The victim is not there to recount his or her ordeal. Executions are justified given the heinous nature of crimes committed. Some are serial killers. But definitions of crime can be stretched in infinite ways, especially by authoritarian governments. Hence, millions  of political activists, opponents, prisoners of war, minority group members and those deemed enemies of the state for very biased reasons have been executed in the 20th century alone, and this has continued unabated to this day (by the Islamic State, and in some of the non-democratic countries listed above).

It is a very humane gesture on the part of governments  not to take away the life of even the most disgusting criminals. For such people, life imprisonment in less than ideal conditions would be a worse punishment than death. Mexico, for example, prefers to imprison drug traffickers instead of executing them. Mexico abolished the death penalty for civilian crime in 1935, and imposed a total ban in Article 22 of the Mexican constitution.

Then there are the miscarriages of justice. A statistical study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) determined that at least 4.1% of all defendants sentenced to death in the United States are actually innocent. This happens all over the world. What are the final thoughts of any person facing execution for a crime he or she didn’t commit like?

Finally, it is fitting to end this column with the words of Viscount Templewood: ““Executions are so much a part of British history that it is almost impossible for many excellent people to think of a future without them.” (In the Shadow of the Gallows -- 1951).

Looks like many Sri Lankans feel the same way now, given the lack of public debate on this issue.
