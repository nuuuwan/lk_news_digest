Supreme Court rules transit passengers subject to Customs jurisdiction

Colombo, Sep. 21 (Daily Mirror) - The Supreme Court has dismissed a long-running claim by an Indian national seeking the return of 50 slabs of gold seized at the Bandaranaike International Airport in 1991, holding that transit passengers are also subject to the jurisdiction of Sri Lanka Customs.

The Supreme Court held the view that a transit passenger carrying a restricted or prohibited item falls outside the jurisdiction of Sri Lanka Customs is contrary to the provisions of the Exchange Control Act and the Customs Ordinance.

The judgment was delivered by Justice Arjuna Obeyesekere, with Chief Justice Preethi Padman Surasena and Justice Mahinda Samayawardhena agreeing with him. The Court made the determination while allowing an appeal filed by the Attorney General.

The Supreme Court delivered this judgment brings to a close a legal battle that began 35 years ago when Angelo Sebastian Pereira, then 37 and based in Dubai, arrived on an Air Lanka flight from Dubai bound for Madras.

Pereira was carrying 5,850 grams of gold equivalent to more than 700 sovereigns concealed in a false bottom of a black briefcase. He was stopped at the entrance to the transit lounge by an Assistant Superintendent of Customs. The gold was forfeited under the Exchange Control Act, the Import and Export Control Act and the Customs Ordinance. A fine of three times the value was initially imposed; it was later mitigated to Rs. 100,000, which Pereira paid.

Pereira maintained he was only a transit passenger, that the gold was destined for India and that he had never “arrived” in Sri Lanka in the legal sense. He later claimed ownership after initially telling Customs officers the gold belonged to a third party in Dubai and that he was merely a courier for a fee.

The District Court of Colombo dismissed his action in 1999, finding the evidence of ownership weak and holding that the gold had been brought into Sri Lanka without a Central Bank permit. The Court of Appeal reversed that decision in 2019, accepting both ownership and the argument that a transit passenger does not “import” goods.

The Attorney General appealed. On the question of ownership, the Supreme Court held that Pereira had failed to establish title. His original statement to Customs that the gold had been given to him by a man named Mohammed for delivery in Madras against a fee was never satisfactorily explained. The Court rejected the later assertion of ownership as an afterthought required only to meet the statutory requirement that a claim under the Customs Ordinance must be made by the owner. Section 110 of the Evidence Ordinance, which creates a presumption of ownership from possession, was held inapplicable because possession of the gold without a permit was unlawful.

On the second issue, the Court ruled that the bringing of gold into Sri Lanka constitutes importation under Section 21 of the then Exchange Control Act, even when the passenger remains in transit and does not clear immigration. Section 21(2) expressly deems the bringing of gold into a port or airport, even if it is intended to leave Sri Lanka without being removed from the vessel or aircraft to be importation. The Court noted that gold was a restricted item requiring prior Central Bank permission. Once imported contrary to that restriction, the gold became liable to forfeiture under Section 43 of the Customs Ordinance.

The case, which began with a plaint filed in the District Court of Colombo in September 1991, has spanned more than three decades, successive appeals and the substitution of a new plaintiff after the original claimant’s death.
