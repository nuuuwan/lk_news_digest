Political revenge or long overdue justice?

For years, Sri Lankans watched politicians accused of corruption walk through the corridors of power while ordinary people struggled to survive. Now that investigations are reaching some of those who once enjoyed political power, the Opposition is crying foul.

When allegations were made, where was this outrage? When questions were raised about public money, government contracts, questionable deals and the misuse of state resources, where was the concern for the people? Governments changed, politicians crossed the floor, and cases dragged on for years.

President Anura Kumara Dissanayake has taken a direct swipe at those who complain that the Government is targeting them politically. He has pointed to cases that remained unresolved for years and said that if they had been dealt with earlier, there would not be so many arrests taking place now. The President said fathers should have advised their sons not to steal. If a father had stopped his son when he first took a bribe, there would be no reason later to ask what could be done when that son was caught.

Sri Lanka has spent too many years normalising the idea that politicians can become extraordinarily wealthy while ordinary citizens are asked to tighten their belts. People stood in fuel queues. Families struggled with food prices. Businesses closed. Young people left the country looking for jobs. The country eventually went bankrupt. This is why the public has little sympathy when politicians being investigated suddenly discover the language of victimisation.

If you are innocent, go before the courts. If the evidence does not support the allegations, you will be cleared. An arrest is not a conviction. An investigation is not proof of guilt. Every suspect is entitled to due process and a fair hearing. But the opposite is also true. Calling every investigation “political revenge” does not make an allegation disappear.

The Government must therefore be held to the same standard it is demanding from its opponents.

If a former minister is accused of wrongdoing, investigate. If a former official is accused, investigate. If a businessman is involved, investigate. And if someone within the Government is accused of corruption, investigate them,  too. The law cannot have one face for the Opposition and another for those in power. That is where the real test for President Dissanayake begins.

It is easy to promise a corruption-free country while sitting in Opposition. It is much harder to build institutions capable of investigating corruption without political interference. The question before the country is not whether politicians should be arrested. It is whether the institutions of the State are strong enough to investigate wrongdoing without fear or favour. Those entrusted with public money must be able to explain how it was used, and answer when questions arise.

The Opposition, instead of immediately claiming political victimisation whenever one of its members is investigated,  should allow the legal process to take its course. If there is evidence, let the courts decide. If not, let the courts clear the accused. That is democracy. The people want one rule, the same rule for everyone.

And perhaps that is the simplest lesson behind the President’s comment about fathers and sons. Parents teach their children not to steal because stealing is wrong. A country must teach its politicians exactly the same lesson. Public money belongs to the public. A minister’s office is not a personal bank account.

So, to those crying “political revenge”, there is a simple answer. If you are innocent, prove it through the justice system. If you are guilty, face the consequences. And if the Government is serious about ending corruption, it must ensure the same rule applies to its own people when the time comes. Sri Lanka does not need political revenge. It needs political accountability.

The law should not ask which party you belong to. It should only ask one question: what did you do?
