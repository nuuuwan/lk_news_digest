Investment climate: Sri Lanka cannot afford mixed signals

Sri Lanka has spent the past few years trying to rebuild an economy battered by the worst crisis in its post-independence history. The country has stabilised, growth has returned and the Government has remained committed to the International Monetary Fund programme. Yet, the latest U.S. assessment of Sri Lanka’s investment climate carries an important message: Economic recovery alone will not be enough to convince investors that Sri Lanka has become a predictable place to do business.

The U.S. Department of State, in its 2026 Investment Climate Statement on Sri Lanka, recognises the political stability that followed the sweeping electoral victories of President Anura Kumara Dissanayake and the National People’s Power Government in 2024.

Foreign investment is ultimately about confidence. Investors commit billions of dollars not merely because a country has attractive locations, a skilled workforce or strategic geography. They need to know that the rules under which they make an investment today will not be substantially altered tomorrow, that taxes will be predictable and government agencies will operate according to transparent procedures.

Sri Lanka has struggled with these issues for decades. The problem, therefore, predates the present administration. The 2024 U.S. assessment similarly identified inconsistent foreign investment policies, governance problems, bureaucratic delays and weak transparency as impediments to investment.

The report puts foreign direct investment in 2025 at US$1.06 billion, equivalent to about one per cent of GDP. It also identifies manufacturing, port development, tourism, information technology, business-process outsourcing and real estate among the sectors that attracted foreign investment. At the same time, investment experts cited in the report point to policy stability, regulatory reform, skilled labour, industrial land, logistics and streamlined trade procedures as areas requiring greater attention.

The country cannot expect to transform itself into a competitive investment destination while investors have to navigate layers of approvals across multiple institutions. Nor can Sri Lanka aspire to become a regional production and logistics hub if access to land, customs procedures, taxation and regulatory approvals remain difficult or unpredictable.

The Government has a particularly important opportunity here. Its political mandate was built partly around the promise of cleaner governance and an end to the practices associated with the old political culture. The administration has also publicly promoted foreign investment while maintaining 100 per cent foreign ownership in most sectors.

The report’s reference to major projects that have stalled or been withdrawn should therefore not be viewed merely through the prism of individual investment decisions. The cases involving the proposed Sinopec refinery and the Adani wind-power project have become examples of the broader question of whether Sri Lanka can provide investors with sufficient certainty once a project enters the complicated stages of negotiation, approval and implementation.

At the same time, Sri Lanka should not interpret every failed negotiation as proof that the investor must always be accommodated. A sovereign government has every right to protect the national interest, renegotiate terms where necessary and ensure that public resources are not committed under unfavourable conditions. The real test is whether such decisions are taken through transparent, consistent and clearly understood procedures.

But attracting investment cannot mean abandoning the Government’s responsibility to regulate strategic sectors or protect public interests. If the Government wants a greater role for State-owned enterprises, it should articulate that policy clearly. If it wants private and foreign capital to drive growth, it should create the conditions for such capital to compete on a level playing field. What creates uncertainty is not necessarily either policy choice, but the perception that the rules can change depending on the circumstances.

Sri Lanka is therefore facing a credibility test. The next phase of economic recovery must be about building institutions that make investment decisions easier, faster and more predictable. Cutting unnecessary approvals, improving inter-agency coordination, ensuring transparent procurement, strengthening contract enforcement and maintaining policy continuity would do more for the investment climate than more  investment promotion campaigns.

The Government should consequently treat the U.S. report as an opportunity for introspection rather than confrontation. Sri Lanka has repeatedly announced ambitious FDI targets. The harder task is creating an environment in which investors voluntarily choose to put their money here and, more importantly, reinvest it.
