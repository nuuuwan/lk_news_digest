A Buddhist Perspective on the Death Penalty

Today, the death penalty is one of the most debated issues in the criminal justice system

The death penalty, also known as capital punishment, is the practice of executing a person as punishment for certain offences. It has existed since ancient times and continues to be enforced in some countries today. When the subject of the death penalty is raised, it often generates strong emotions and controversy.

Today, the death penalty is one of the most debated issues in the criminal justice system. It is discussed not only within criminal justice systems but also among religious groups and human rights organisations.

In this discussion, I am not going to focus on how the death penalty is justified by criminal justice systems or other organisations. Instead, I will concentrate on the Buddhist perspective on the death penalty. From a Buddhist perspective, the death penalty can simply be described as unacceptable.

Buddhism is concerned with balancing wisdom and compassion. One of the most important teachings in Buddhism is the Five Precepts. The first precept is to abstain from taking the life of any living being. Therefore, the death penalty is contrary to this fundamental Buddhist teaching. Buddhists believe in rebirth: beings die and are born again, continuing through the cycle of birth and death known as Samsara until they attain Nirvana. Rebirth is understood to be influenced by karma. Good karma produces good results, while bad karma produces bad results.

From this Buddhist perspective, the death penalty is unacceptable because killing can produce negative karmic consequences. According to Buddhism, a person’s present circumstances may be connected to actions performed in past existences.

Therefore, people must be very careful about their actions because even seemingly small unwholesome actions can contribute to further negative karma. In the Culajammavibhanga Sutta, the Buddha explained the importance of karma to a young man named Subha. The Buddha taught: “Young man, beings have Karma as their possession, as their inheritance, Karma as their cause, Karma as their relative, as their refuge, it is only Karma that conditions beings either to be inferior or superior.”

The Buddha then explained that a person who takes the lives of living beings, is cruel, has blood-stained hands, is inclined towards killing or destruction, and lacks compassion may, because of those actions, be reborn in miserable existences. Even if such a person is reborn as a human being, the Buddha explained that taking life and lacking compassion can lead to a short life

An example given in Buddhist tradition is the Venerable Moggalana. Venerable  Moggalana was one of the Buddha’s foremost disciples and was known for possessing great supernatural powers. Despite this, he was killed by a group of robbers because of an action committed in one of his previous existences, when he had killed his parents.

According to the traditional account, the consequences of this past action remained with him even though he had become an Arahant. According to the Buddha’s teaching, killing and lacking compassion produce bad karma and negative results. Therefore, from this perspective, there is no justification for deliberately taking another person’s life. Punishment, however, is important for maintaining order in human society. There is no argument against the need to protect the public from criminals and to rehabilitate offenders. Deterrence is also an important purpose of punishment. There are many other reasons why societies punish people who commit crimes.

However, the important question is how we can justify the methods of punishment as human beings. The Buddha accepted that certain forms of punishment could be necessary. Some forms of punishment may be strict, but they should not be harmful. Their purpose should be rehabilitation rather than vengeance. In doctrinal terms, the death penalty is inconsistent with Buddhist teaching because the first precept requires compassion towards all living beings, including criminals, regardless of how serious their crimes may be.

The story of Angulimala provides an important example. Angulimala, whose name literally means “Garland of Fingers”, was a murderer and robber who had killed many people. The local people were terrified of him. Nevertheless, when the Buddha was staying in the area, he decided to travel along the road where Angulimala was believed to be hiding. Angulimala saw the Buddha and pursued him with the intention of killing him. However, the Buddha eventually managed to transform Angulimala, who became a monk. This story demonstrates the Buddhist belief that even a person who has committed serious crimes can change. It also illustrates the importance of rehabilitation rather than simply destroying the life of the offender. At the same time, the king and his army were searching for Angulimala. When the king met the Buddha, he explained the situation. The Buddha then showed him the reformed Angulimala, who was living peacefully as a monk. The king was amazed by the transformation. This clearly demonstrates the Buddhist notion of rehabilitation

Rehabilitation is therefore an important Buddhist principle. Buddhist concepts of rehabilitation and capital punishment are difficult to reconcile because the death penalty permanently removes the possibility of reform. Nirvana is attained. Reforming criminals can therefore mean returning productive members to society who can contribute to the welfare of others.

According to the Digha Nikaya Brahmajala Sutta, translated from Pali by T. W. Rhys  Davids in 1899: “Putting away the killing of living things, Gautama, the recluse, holds aloof from the destruction of life. He has laid the cudgel and sword aside, and, shunning roughness, is full of mercy, compassion and kindness towards all creatures that have life.” This teaching further emphasises the Buddhist commitment to non violence and compassion. Tenzin Gyatso, the Fourteenth Dalai Lama, has also expressed opposition to the death penalty. He argued that although capital punishment may serve a preventive function, it is also a form of revenge and is especially severe because it is final. The Dalai Lama has argued that, regardless of how horrible an individual’s actions may have been, everyone has the potential to improve and correct themselves.

Therefore, it may be possible to deter criminal activity and prevent harmful consequences without resorting to the death penalty. As capital punishment involves taking a life, it requires breaking the first precept. For this reason, it is incompatible with Buddhist ethics and with the Buddhist emphasis on compassion. Buddhists believe that all living beings are interconnected within Samsara, the cycle of birth and death. Therefore, compassion should naturally be important in our relationships with one another.

No person is perfect until attaining Nirvana or enlightenment. Until then, people remain influenced by ignorance, greed, hatred, jealousy and other negative mental states. From this perspective, wrongdoing can be understood as arising from harmful tendencies within a person. Instead of simply destroying the individual, it may be more appropriate to help that person overcome these negative vcharacteristics. Buddhist teaching therefore emphasises compassion and assistance in developing a calm and wholesome state of mind. According to the law of karma, no one can escape the consequences of his or her actions. If a person deliberately kills or injures another person, that person creates negative karma through the action. Therefore, if the state kills a murderer, the person carrying out the execution is still participating in an act of killing. Although the act may be legally permitted by society, it does not escape the consequences of karma from a Buddhist perspective.

The Buddhist understanding of death also provides another perspective on the death penalty. According to the Abhidhamma, there are four kinds of death

1. Death due to the expiration of the age limit (Ayukkhaya-marana).

2. Death due to the expiration of productive karmic force (Kammakkhaya marana).

3. Death due to the expiration of both the age limit and karma

4. Death caused by destructive karma (Upacchedaka-marana), such as sudden death resulting from an accident or suicide.

From a Buddhist perspective, the final moments of life are particularly important.

When a person is approaching death, it is important that wholesome mental states, or Javana Citta, arise. If wholesome thoughts prevail until the final breath, the being may be reborn in a happy existence. Conversely, if unwholesome mental states dominate before death, the person may be reborn in a woeful existence.

Everything is constantly changing, including human beings. Criminals are also subject to this nature of impermanence. A person who commits a crime may not remain the same person psychologically or morally throughout his or her life. If a person can change, then permanently terminating that person’s life removes the possibility of further transformation

The Buddha also explained the Dasa Raja Dhamma, or the Ten Royal Virtues, which are important qualities for leaders to develop to create a peaceful society based on happiness, security and the welfare of others. These qualities include

Avihimsa, meaning non-harm, and Akrodaya, meaning non-ill will, require leaders to put aside anger, hatred, jealousy, envy, fear and resentment. Kings and leaders should therefore act with kindness and compassion towards other beings. If leaders possess these qualities, they are less likely to promote violence and conflict. From this perspective, Buddhism does not provide legitimacy for the death penalty

One of the Buddha’s teachings in the Majjhima Nikaya gives a powerful example of the importance of maintaining compassion even towards those who cause harm. The teaching states that even if thieves were to violently attack a person, developing a hostile mind would still be inconsistent with the Buddha’s teaching. A similar idea is expressed in the story of a Vietnamese monk who was asked whether he would kill someone who had attempted to destroy Buddhism. The monk replied that it would be better to allow himself to be killed rather than kill another person, because killing would betray the very principles of the Dharma that he was trying to preserve. This illustrates the extreme importance that Buddhist ethics can place on avoiding the intentional taking of life.

According to Buddhism, no one becomes perfect until attaining Nirvana or enlightenment. Until that point, people remain influenced by ignorance, greed and hatred, which can produce jealousy, competition and other harmful behaviour. Human decisions and actions may therefore be imperfect. This raises questions about whether human judgments and legal decisions can ever be completely accurate or fair. Justice can also be affected by differences in wealth, power and social status. Wealthier people may have greater access to experienced lawyers and legal organisations and may therefore be better able to use the appeals system. In contrast, poorer people may have fewer resources and may have little choice but to accept decisions made by lower courts. The possibility of unequal access to justice is particularly important when considering an irreversible punishment such as the death penalty. According to Buddhism, a Buddhist should follow the Four Brahma Viharas: Metta, Karuna, Mudita and Upekkha. Upekkha, or equanimity, means maintaining an impartial mind and not giving people special treatment based on wealth, caste or social status. From this perspective, justice should not favour the rich over the poor.

Buddha also taught in the Kalama Sutta that people should not accept something simply because they have heard it from others, because it has been passed down through tradition, because it is found in scripture, or simply because it appears to be acceptable. This teaching encourages people to examine claims carefully rather than accepting them blindly. This principle can also raise questions about the legal system. Trials, judgments and conclusions are made by human beings, and human beings can make mistakes. Therefore, from a Buddhist perspective, it is difficult to justify an irreversible punishment such as execution when there is a possibility of human error.

Temporary punishment may cause suffering, but the death penalty is fundamentally different because it permanently separates a person from his or her family, children and loved ones. The consequences also affect people who have not committed the crime, but who may suffer emotionally because of the execution.

Although many Buddhist teachings emphasise non-violence and opposition to killing, it is important to recognise that Buddhist traditions have not always taken the same position on the death penalty. Historically, many states where Buddhism was the official religion-imposed capital punishment for certain offences. One notable exception was Japan, where Emperor Saga abolished the death penalty in 818. This abolition lasted until 1165, although executions continued privately in some areas.

Japan still retains the death penalty today, although some justice ministers have refused to sign death warrants, citing their Buddhist beliefs as a reason. Buddhist majority countries have also adopted different approaches. Bhutan has abolished the death penalty, while Thailand retains it, despite Buddhism being highly influential in both countries.

There are also traditional expressions within Japanese Buddhist traditions that connect the sword with both taking and preserving life. In these circumstances, it can be argued that the use of lethal force may be permissible when it is intended to prevent greater suffering.

However, Buddhist groups in different countries have reached different conclusions. In countries such as Japan, Korea and Taiwan, Buddhist groups have generally tended to oppose the death penalty, while in Thailand, Sri Lanka and Bhutan, where Buddhism has had significant political influence, attitudes have varied. Nevertheless, almost all Buddhist groups oppose the use of the death penalty purely as a form of retribution.

From the Buddhist perspective presented above, the death penalty is fundamentally difficult to justify because it involves the intentional taking of a human life. The first Buddhist precept calls upon people to abstain from killing, while Buddhist teachings on karma emphasise that intentional actions have consequences. Buddhism also places great importance on compassion, rehabilitation and the possibility of change.
