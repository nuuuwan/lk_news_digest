Houthis at wailing gate:  Iran war’s turning point

In 1991, when Saddam Hussein’s Iraq came under attack from United States‑led coalition forces, some 120 Iraqi fighter jets landed in Iran to avoid being hit. Iran did not return the aircraft. After all, it was Iraq that wronged Iran by launching the nine-year war that caused the deaths of nearly 600,000 Iranians.

As though the surprise gift was not enough, Iran saw pro‑Iranian Shiites capturing government power in elections held under 2003 US occupation and afterward. Call it divine reward or natural justice — Iran stood to gain. Iraq the enemy became Iraq the friend.

Fast forward to 2026. Iran was wronged once again when the US-Israeli combined force launched a surprise attack on Iran on February 28. And once again it stands to gain from a development in a direction it least expected to yield such a military advantage as it has now gained in the lightning battleground victories of Yemen’s pro‑Iranian Ansarallah group led by the Houthis. Iran’s position in its war against the far superior military might of the US has been reinforced by Houthi victories. The Houthis now control the Bab al‑Mandab waterway and the strategically crucial nearby islands in the Red Sea. They have captured the Red sea city of Mocha and are advancing in other directions.

In asymmetrical wars, the unexpected happens. Battlefield conditions can turn in favour of the underdog.

Such battlefield surprises neither the US nor Israel seemed to have taken into consideration when they rushed to attack Iran and triggered a war without giving much thought to adverse global ramifications that could ensue. Oil prices, particularly diesel, have been skyrocketing since the fall of Bab al‑Mandab, or the Gate of Tears. True to its meaning, the whole world wails over rising prices.

When the Iran war started on February 28, Yemen was relatively peaceful, with the Houthi‑led Ansarallah administering the fertile northwest provinces where about 80 percent of Yemenis live. Though the Houthis are Zaydi Shiites—who in some religious practices are closer to the Sunnis than the Shiites in Iran—sectarianism is hardly an issue in the Yemeni conflict. The Zaydi Shiites form 36 per cent of the population and had ruled much of Yemen under an imamate for centuries until the Ottomans occupied the country.

Amid the post‑World War I geopolitical shake‑up after the Ottomans lost their Middle Eastern territories, the newly established Wahhabi‑led Saudi Arabia, backed by Britain, launched a war on the revived Zaydi imamate in 1934. The brief war ended with the Yemeni imamate conceding three northern provinces, which today form part of Saudi Arabia’s southern region. In an interesting twist, Saudi Arabia backed the Zaydi imamate when a civil war broke out in 1962, after pro‑Nasserite military officers captured power and dissolved the thousand‑year‑old imamate. To support the new republic, Egyptian President Gamal Abdel Nasser sent 70,000 troops. Some 26,000 Egyptians died in the civil war. Egypt’s involvement in Yemen—dubbed Nasser’s Vietnam—was a key reason for its defeat against Israel in the 1967 war.

Meanwhile, Britain which was occupying Aden in the south left in 1967 following an uprising by left‑inclined youth. Yemen remained divided along the Cold War lines—North Yemen was supported by Saudi Arabia and the West, and the South by the Soviet Union. The end of the Cold War led to Yemeni unification, but the power‑sharing arrangement in favour of the densely populated North—with North’s president Ali Abdullah Saleh as head of state—gave rise to regular disputes and even civil wars. Al‑Qaeda elements found safe haven in the south and fished in Yemen’s troubled waters. In October 2000, they carried out a suicide attack on a US warship near Aden, 11 months before 9/11, killing 17 marines. In response, US carried out regular airstrikes on Al-Qaeda targets.

The winds of the Arab Spring flowed across Yemen in 2011. The Houthis backed the public uprising. Accused of amassing US$ 30 billion in ill-gotten wealth, Saleh, who ruled Yemen for more than three decades, was compelled to hand over power to his deputy, Abdrabbuh Mansur Hadi in 2012. The change of leadership did not address the Houthi grievances, and the civil war continued. The ousted Saleh teamed up with the Houthis. Together they seized the capital, Sanaa, and toppled President Hadi’s government, prompting a military intervention by Saudi Arabia and other Gulf countries in 2014. Hadi fled to Saudi Arabia, and his government in exile was recognised by the UN. Much to the chagrin of Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates broke ranks and backed the southern separatists known as the Southern Transitional Council (STC).

Saleh and the Houthis parted ways in 2017 when he signed a deal with Saudi Arabia and the UAE. Months later, Saleh was killed by the Houthis. The ensuing war plunged Yemen into what the UN described as the world’s worst humanitarian crisis, with thousands of children dying from hunger and diseases such as cholera. In 2022, a UN‑brokered ceasefire between Saudi Arabia and the Houthis took effect in Yemen.

In the meantime, in December last year, Saudi Arabia bombed two UAE ships supplying weapons to the southern separatists, which were on the verge of capturing two provinces from Saudi‑backed government forces. The UAE withdrew under Saudi threats.

The ceasefire collapsed in July when Saudi‑backed Yemeni government forces bombed the Sanaa airport, preventing an Iranian civilian aircraft from bringing home a top Houthi delegation, after they attended the funeral of Iranian leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. The Houthis hit back at Saudi Arabia. The war broke out. Nations panicked when the Houthis, in a lightning advance, captured Bab al‑Mandab and continued to gain battlefield victories.

Saudi Arabia sought US military support against the Houthis. The US refused. Instead, Washington held talks with the Houthis in Oman to elicit a pledge that shipping in the Bab al‑Mandab would not be disrupted. The Houthis agreed but did not extend the safe‑passage guarantee to Saudi ships. Saudi Crown Prince Muhammad bin Salman visited Cairo to hold urgent talks aimed at winning Egypt’s support. Israeli media reports claimed Riyadh had also sought help from Tel Aviv.

The Houthis matched each Saudi bombing campaign with attacks on Saudi oil and military facilities. Drones from Houthi allies in Iraq hit Saudi Arabia’s crucial East‑West pipeline. The attack halted 4 per cent of global oil supplies. Moreover, with the Bab al‑Mandab closed for the Saudi ships and the Strait of Hormuz remaining virtually closed, Saudi oil can reach the Asian market only through the Suez Canal or through tanker-to-tanker transfer in Oman.

Amidst these disruptions to global fuel supplies, the Saudis claimed they shot down a drone over the holy city of Mecca and blamed the Houthis. The news sent shockwaves across the Muslim world. The Houthis denied the reports, which they said were aimed at portraying them as enemies of Islam. Pakistan, which had recently signed a tripartite defence deal with Saudi Arabia and Türkiye, reacted to these reports by issuing a severe warning to the Houthis.

The reports sparked a social media war, with pro‑Saudi accounts likening the Houthis to Abraha’s elephant army, destroyed by God in the year the Prophet Muhammad was born when it sought to demolish the Ka’ba. Pro‑Houthi voices challenged the Saudi Wahhabis’ claim to Mecca, branding them invaders who erased its heritage and built toilets over the Prophet’s companions’ homes.

Saudi Arabia has left with only two options—escalate the Yemen war or ceasefire.

The new battlefield developments have put Iran in a more advantageous position. For the US, the situation cannot get any worse with oil prices soaring. The US understands the gravity of the crisis and is redoubling efforts to end its war with Iran. And that is good news.
