The Sri Lankan economy is not an island. We like to think that all will be fine as long as we stabilise our economy and ensure fiscal discipline. However, in reality, economic crises in Sri Lanka have more often been triggered by external shocks rather than internal problems. And now we are staring at economic and political developments that signal a dangerous world.

There are different external shocks that have been in the making, and it is indeed difficult to predict as to which particular source or combination of factors can rock the global order. The war in Iran has again intensified, global oil prices are hovering around US$ 100 per barrel, shipping around the Middle East is in trouble and the region itself could spiral into escalating wars.

The racist political right is on the rise in Europe, including in France and Germany that hold Europe together. Global trade is precarious with Trump’s arbitrary tariffs over the last year and a half. Indeed, with the trade system in disarray, the possibility of an export-led economic strategy has also become questionable, because even if there is increase in comparative productivity, tariffs will nullify the advances.

In such crisis prone times, this column focuses on the dangers ahead with global financial markets. With inflation rising due to commodity price increases including oil, the Federal Reserve and the Bank of Japan are increasing their interest rates. Furthermore, bond yields in the US, UK, France, and Japan are the highest in decades, signalling a shift towards higher cost of capital. This would in effect, lead to even higher interest costs for developing countries like ours.

There is an even more insidious danger with the possibility of a financial meltdown with the bursting of the AI bubble. It is the crisis tendencies generated by the global financial system, of which we know little about and hard to analyse, that we need to be particularly concerned.

The last five decades of neoliberal policies – what are commonly known in Sri Lanka as open economy reforms – have led to both increased financialisation of the economies and repeated financial crises. In other words, the rapid rise of financial institutions including the encroachment of finance into every sector has produced mounting crises caused by increased sovereign debt and capital flight. Notable are the Latin American and African debt crisis of the 1980s, the East Asian crisis of 1997, the North Atlantic financial crisis of 2008, the Greek sovereign debt crisis of the 2010s, and the Global South debt crisis of the early 2020s; the latter as we know crippled our country. These are symptomatic of a global financial system that creates tremendous wealth for global financiers but leads to dispossession for the rest of the population.

On a personal note, I experienced first-hand both the exuberance and the collapse of such financial crises in the 1990s and 2000s, when I worked as a telecommunications engineer in the heart of financial centres in Tokyo and New York. Even as I started working as a fresh graduate in 1994, the bond markets, particularly in the US, crashed. It took some time for the American financial sector to find its confidence, but capital found new pastures in East Asia. Three years later from the proximity of Tokyo, I observed the East Asian crisis in 1997. The crisis was propelled by the flight of capital that became possible with liberalisation of the capital account. However, global finance capital was not blamed, rather, the so-called East Asian miracles were labelled overnight by West as “crony capitalists”.

As an engineer building fiber optic networks that came of age with the expansion of the internet in the late 1990s, I heard fantastic stories of how with communication moving at the speed of light, the internet was going to completely change the global economy. Production of goods were apparently secondary, and it was information technology that would determine everything. Start-ups and massive investments, and so many computer scientists and engineers, were all lured by this so-called historic change that came to be known as the dot-com boom. But alas, in 2000 the bubble burst with so many companies going under.

Twenty-five years later, I sense similar sentiments and developments with the AI boom. The financial sector has been leveraged to the hilt for speculative investments in firms and data centres that for the most part are still loss making and can hardly show any real revenues. With every effort to hype up AI running out of steam, the time is ripe for a crash. But very few people seem to remember the past or learn from it. Indeed, finance moves quickly these days, but it also seems to make us forget quickly.

The world economy clearly is in trouble, and I would argue it is the heavily financialised character of our contemporary global order that makes it most vulnerable to severe crises. Karl Marx, close to a century and a half ago, called financial assets that now rule our world fictitious capital. Indeed, we may have to face a great crash and tremendous economic destruction when such large amassed speculative investments meet the reality of profits and losses that are the underlying logic of capitalism.

One may believe that this may just be another crisis for global capital that in a year or two will recover like all the other crises during the neoliberal era. However, I argue that this time it is different because neoliberalism itself is in crisis. Ideologically and materially, free markets are quickly crumbling as a polarised world not just in terms of political outlooks but also in terms of trade wars, if not physical wars, which are making any coordinated response by global capital to save itself unworkable.

In other words, I claim that the consequences of another bond market crash or the AI bubble bursting will be far worse than the previous financial crises. The confluence of crisis tendencies may create a downward cycle of unravelling that even the Central Banks and Finance Ministries of the most powerful countries will be unable to address; all the more so, as these states are at loggerheads with each other. This global political situation is more akin to the situation in the 1930s, when the resolution to the crisis came only after a prolonged depression, the rise of fascism and a devastating world war.

Amidst this tumultuous world, Sri Lanka needs to rapidly change its economic policies. We need to put up barriers to the devastating flows of capital rather than go along with the IMF and its recommendations for further financial opening. We need to focus on building our production economy rather than be deluded that the financial sector, whether it be the capital markets, finance companies or banks, will guide and strengthen our economy.

Most of all, we need to shore up our resources and secure the essential needs for our people starting with food security. In a dangerously shifting world, when amnesia of past crises is the norm, are we ready to change our thinking and the economic trajectory?
