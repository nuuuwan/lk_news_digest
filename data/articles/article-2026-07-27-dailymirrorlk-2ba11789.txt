Credit surge masked as auto dealers dodge inventory penalty

Dealers preempt 3% monthly inventory penalty by pre-registering vehicles, artificially depressing official registration-based financing statistics

Non-bank financial institutions rapidly expand their balance sheets through commercial bank borrowings to fund consumer and vehicle loans

Despite a 27.8% surge in private sector credit, strong government fiscal discipline anchors overall domestic credit growth

Official vehicle registration statistics are presenting a deceptive picture of auto-financing activity in Sri Lanka. Motor dealers are increasingly pre-registering unsold vehicles under their own names to avoid inventory penalties, while non-bank financial institutions aggressively expand their balance sheets to fund vehicle purchases, according to an analysis by JB Securities CEO/MD Murtaza Jafferjee.

Department of Motor Traffic data shows financing penetration hovering at a subdued 43 percent across passenger vehicles in June 2026. However, underlying financial system data reveals a sharp contrast. Private sector credit expanded by a robust 27.8 percent year-on-year in May 2026, driven largely by consumer finance, pawning, and non-bank financial institution lending.

This divergence stems from tactical moves by vehicle importers to bypass fiscal regulations. Authorities currently levy a monthly penalty equivalent to the percent of a vehicle’s CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight) value if it remains unregistered beyond 90 days after importation. Introduced to prevent overstocking and current account pressures, the policy has instead led dealers to register vehicles prior to retail sale. This effectively clears the statutory window while masking true leasing and mortgage volumes in official registration records.

“Although RMV registration statistics do not fully capture the extent of credit flowing into vehicle purchases—primarily because dealers have been pre-registering vehicles to avoid the 3% of CIF value per month penalty imposed on vehicles that remain unregistered for more than 90 days—it is evident that credit continues to be a key driver of both vehicle imports and purchases,” Jafferjee said.

He noted that lower recorded financing shares reflect dealers registering inventory before sales are finalized.

The accumulation of floor stock follows a sharp rebound in imports during May 2026, when traders rushed to open letters of credit ahead of fiscal tightening. “Vehicle imports peaked in November and December 2025 before moderating in the subsequent months.

However, imports rebounded noticeably in May 2026, likely reflecting a surge in letters of credit opened ahead of the 50 percent duty surcharge introduced by the fiscal authorities to cool demand,” Jafferjee explained.

The temporary surcharge, initially enacted for three months, expires in mid-August 2026 and is widely expected to be extended.

Crucially, Jafferjee’s analysis highlights that the rapid credit growth bankrolling vehicle purchases is being fuelled by a balance sheet expansion across non-bank financial institutions.

Sectoral credit data indicates that lending to financial services, consumer finance, and pawning has grown at rates well above the banking system average. Rather than matching loan growth with deposit mobilisation, many licensed finance companies have increasingly turned to commercial bank borrowings to fund their portfolios, according to him.

He highlighted that incremental lending has been concentrated heavily in gold-backed loans—accounting for between 25 percent and 50 percent of new credit for several firms—with much of the remainder channeled directly into vehicle financing.

Despite private sector credit surging by 27.8 percent, overall domestic credit growth remained anchored at 11.1 percent in May 2026. This macro-level stability was enabled by strict government fiscal discipline, which generated large primary surpluses, as well as cost-reflective pricing at state-owned enterprises that curtailed public sector borrowing requirements.

Vehicle imports accounted for 9.7 percent  of total current account outflows in May 2026, keeping the sector under close regulatory observation as dealer pre-registrations hide its footprint from official registration data.
