Is climate change responsible for Nepal’s disaster?

People hold  symbolic funeral ceremonies  meant to “liberate the souls” of those who died in the flood disaster (photo by Prakash Mathema).

Kunda Dixit, former editor and Publisher of Nepali Times  and author of ‘Dateline Earth: Journalism As If The Planet Mattered,”  thinks climate change is the main cause.

Global warming could be  the principal cause of the catastrophic floods which struck the Nepal-Tibet border on Aug 26. No one is quite sure yet what exactly caused it, though some are pointing fingers at construction work done by China in Tibet as a  contributing cause, a claim China has categorically rejected.

Arnab Goswami,  Indian journalist, television news anchor,  and editor-in-chief of Republic TV, directly accuses China of being responsible for the disaster, and blames China for ‘not doing anything when it could have done so much to prevent the disaster.’

The Nepalese government has not officially accused China, but the Nepalese journalists’ collective is putting the blame of Chinese construction work in the Girong County in Tibet (now in China). It further says China failed to share glacial flood data and offered no early warnings, claims rejected strongly by Beijing.

But Kunda Dixit, former editor and Publisher of Nepali Times  and author of ‘Dateline Earth: Journalism As If The Planet Mattered,”  thinks climate change is the main cause.

Tibetan-American politician-in-exile Dr. Lobsang Sangay, speaking on ANI News, India, claims that the glacier crashed at 8.31 am, but the avalanche swept away the immigration office on the Tibetan (Chinese) side twenty minutes later. He asks why Chinese authorities could not issue a warning during that crucial twenty minute period.

A full-scale diplomatic war may be coming in the near future.

What is certain that an avalanche struck the Lhende River in Tibet, twenty km north east of the China-Nepal border, and rolled down like a glacial tsunami on Rasuwa Gadhi in the Rasuwa district of Nepal.  Rasua Gadhi is a border crossing, and an important trade-pilrimage exchange traffic exchange point. The avalanche wiped it out, including the road leading to the border crossing. This is also the main economic corridor to China, Nepal’s second biggest trading partner.

The death toll, over 1000 at the time of this writing, was so high because the flood came on a full moon day, and the crossing was packed with vehicles carrying both foreign visitors and Nepali pilgrims heading towards the holy rivers in Nepal and in Tibet.

This would remind Sri Lankans of the 2004 tsunami disaster. The death toll was higher, and damage more extensive. But it was confined to the coastal areas, and Sri Lanka received an estimated $2 billion to $2.5 billion in total international aid pledges and contributions, making a quick recovery possible.

But Nepal’s case is different. It went through a major social upheaval six months ago, bringing in a new government, and has a GDP per capita of just $1,512 USD. Services make up roughly 62% of the economy, agriculture  about 25%, and industry comprises about 13%. Foreign aid so far has been paltry compared to what Sri Lanka received in 2004 – the United Arab Emirates (UAE) allocated $10 million, Australia pledged  approx. USD 5.7M,  the United Kingdom (UK) offered  a USD 6.79 million support package while Canada offered $5 million in urgent humanitarian aid. This will provide a measure of relief, but is hardly enough for the monumental repair and rehabilitation tasks ahead.

Finance Minister Swarnim Wagle has said the country will need USD4-5 million to rebuild, nearly a tenth of Nepal’s GDP. Ninety per cent of Nepal’s power grid (4300 megawatts) comes from hydropower. Nepal even exports electricity to India. Five power stations have been destroyed, with close to 390 megawatts of power damaged,  which is nearly 8% of total generation capacity. Nepal has asked the loss-and-damage fund for immediate support, saying the task at hand is beyond its resources. This fund was developed specifically for vulnerable developing countries during the world climate process

But the world is in turmoil,  Western Europe has economic woes and in no mood for largesse. The US is embroiled in a quagmire like war with Iran. While US President Donald Trump offered sympathy for Nepal, the US has offered only $3.6 million in aid so far, a paltry amount. Donald Trump  very conveniently forgot that he staunchly refutes the idea of climate change, which is seen as the most likely contributor to this disaster.

Sri Lanka expected a substantial amount of foreign aid after last November’s Cyclone Ditwah damage. We received  approximately USD 27.5 million USD in foreign contributions. This may sound like a lot. But it isn’t in this high inflationary period, the damage hasn’t been fully repaired yet, and we don’t know what to expect when heavy rains come later in the year.

This is why internal resources are so vital, and Nepal doesn’t lack them. For example, with its large limestone deposits, the country manufactures cement and is largely self-sufficient in that vital construction material.

What Nepal doesn’t lack is fortitude against heavy odds, and resilience.  After the devastating April 2015 earthquake, which killed 9,000 and injured   thousands more, while damaging or  destroying more than 600,000 structures in Kathmandu and other nearby towns, Nepal managed to recover.

Many foreign rescue teams arrived in the immediate aftermath of that earthquake. But it was hard to co-ordinate efforts. Having learnt from that experience, the Nepalese government declined foreign rescue teams this time, opting for emergency rations and other essential items.

The disaster highlighted the country’s strongpoints, hitherto unseen by those outsiders who only a small, landlocked, mainly agricultural country. Nepalese helicopters played a key role in the rescue missions. Priya Adhikari, the country’s first female helicopter pilot, flew repeatedly into the worst-hit areas, saving many lives.

Twenty air force helicopters flew extremely dangerous rescue missions, often flying in single formation through a narrow gorge in bad weather as mud, debris and water raced below.

Adhikari, 40, said she had to be on radio communication with several other helicopters all the time to avoid mistakes and collisions. They had to land on surfaces which looked like concrete, but very soft. Rescuers, unable to walk on such unstable surfaces, were seen crawling. The helicopters had to be on power while the rescues took place, or they would have sunk into the mud. Priya Adhikari flew 100 rescue missions in six days. She studied environmental science and worked as cabin crew before learning to fly helicopters. She has flown rescue missions to save stranded or injured climbers high up in the Himalayas.

The economic fallout of this disaster is huge. Kunda Dixit says there could be high levels of migration from the countryside to the cities in the coming months. Eighty bridges and forty kilometres of roads have been damaged by the destructive debris flow as the pulverised glacier crashed down from 5,200 metres above sea level to the valley floor 1,200 metres below, and was carried downstream with great force by the Trishuli and two other rivers; all three originate in Tibet, but flow into Nepal down below, and thence all the way to India joined by many other rivers. Bodies were found in India 250km away.

Arnab Goswami maintains that Girong Town and port in Tibet (now in China) are cut into the hills ‘on the rivers’ own course and confluence, and thus China can’t evade responsibility for the disaster.’

Some commentators point fingers at Nepal’s Upper Trishuli-1 hydropower project. Hundreds of workers and engineers remain trapped under the project’s tunnels and time is running out for them. One worker was found miraculously alive yesterday, but more than 900 remain trapped.

But Kunda Dixit offers another explanation for the disaster. Glaciers high up in the Himalayas have been melting and cracking due to global warming. Both India and China are high emitters of greenhouse gases, though in capital terms not as high as the industrialised countries (mainly the West but also including Eastern Europe). As Himalayan glaciers continue to melt, the dire warning is that Nepal could expect more avalanches of this kind in future.
