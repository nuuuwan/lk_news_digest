Animal Welfare Bill : A new ray of hope for voiceless beings

This Bill therefore brings a fresh ray of hope for voiceless animals

The passage of the long awaited Animal Welfare Bill marks a significant legislative milestone for Sri Lanka. For over a century, animal rights was  treated almost as an afterthought, governed by the obsolete Prevention of Cruelty to Animals Ordinance of 1907. Except for a handful of compassionate individuals who have been voicing for the rights of all animals, irrespective of whether it is an ailing stray dog on a street or an iconic elephant injured by consuming hakka patas, many others have chosen to cast a blind eye towards animal welfare.

This is evident when looking at the number of incidents of animal cruelty being reported on a daily basis. A recent video going viral on social media shows how a man and a woman obstructed an individual who usually bring food and water to feed stray dogs in his area. The woman is seen throwing away the food and water given to the dogs in protest while the man was seen chasing two dogs with sticks. Several stray dogs were also shot dead over the past few months, indicating deteriorating levels of compassion towards animals.

According to the 1907 Ordinance, the maximum fine for a first-time animal cruelty or neglect offence was a paltry 100 rupees, alongside a possible prison term of up to three months. But now, any individual who is found guilty of animal cruelty would be charged Rs. 250,000 with a jail term of two years. Any individual who kills an animal in a cruel manner would be liable to a fine of Rs. 500,000 and a jail term of three years.

This Bill therefore brings a fresh ray of hope for voiceless animals. It was initially drafted in 2006 and was subsequently presented to Parliament in 2010 by Ven. Athuraliye Rathana thera as a private member’s bill. But the Parliament was dissolved immediately thereafter. Animal welfare groups filed a Court of Appeal writ application to pressure authorities. In 2015, when the Yahapalana regime assumed power, the Bill was revived under the 100-day programme and was submitted to the Cabinet. However this triggered further reviews with the Treasury and public consultations.

After a long delay, the then cabinet approved the Animal Welfare Bill in January 2022. Even though the draft Bill was gazetted and presented to Parliament, it was later referred to the Sectoral Oversight Committee on Food and Agriculture which recommended key adjustments.

The 2006 draft proposed to include the concept of ‘duty of care’ towards animals in the statute. Duty of care imposes a positive legal obligation on owners to actively ensure that the welfare needs of the animal under their care are met. Countries such as UK and Australia have established a legal duty of care for people in charge of animals grounded in the Five Freedoms of animal welfare. These include freedom from hunger and thirst, freedom from discomfort, freedom from pain, injury and disease, freedom to express normal behaviour and freedom from fear and distress.

However, the Bill has now come under scrutiny for its provisions on chaining animals for instance. This concerns owners of domesticated elephants that have been chained for long years. Political groups have questioned provisions that restrict elephants to carry heavy loads. They argue that by indicating ‘heavy loads’, it could be interpreted in a way that restricts elephants from carrying the relics casket at processions.

This is why animal welfare has become a sensitive topic. One becomes emotional over not being able to continue archaic traditions while they seem to have absolutely no empathy towards animals being subject to cruelty while being taken on processions. There are numerous videos which expose the level of stress these animals undergo when wearing different costumes and parading streets to entertain crowds.

But now, despite certain gaps that still need to be addressed, finally there’s hope that this Bill would legally empower a culture of compassion towards animals.
