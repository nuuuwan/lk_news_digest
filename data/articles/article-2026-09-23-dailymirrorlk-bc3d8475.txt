Is Nature Finally Striking Back at Us?

As natural disasters strike with increasing frequency, humanity faces a critical question: is nature striking back? While scientifically passive, natural systems are behaving unpredictably due to human intervention. Decades of deforestation, wetland destruction, and climate disruption have dismantled nature’s defenses. Ultimately, nature is not seeking revenge, but simply presenting humanity with the inevitable bill for its arrogance.

Of late, one cannot help feeling that natural disasters are occurring around us with increasing frequency.

Almost every time we switch on the television or look at the news and social media, there seems to be another natural disaster somewhere in the world.

Sri Lanka has had its own share, with the most recent being the devastating effects of Ditwah and subsequent extreme weather. Nepal has experienced destructive floods and landslides, while the Philippines, Myanmar, Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Venezuela and Colombia have all faced different natural calamities in recent times.

Floods, cyclones, landslides, droughts, wildfires and extreme heat seem to be occurring with regularity. Earthquakes have added to the destruction in some countries, although they belong to a very different geological process.

Against this background, a serious question inevitably arises: is nature striking back at us?

It is a tempting thought, particularly when one remembers Conservation International’s Nature is Speaking campaign, where Julia Roberts, as the voice of Mother Nature, reminds us rather chillingly that nature has been here for billions of years, far longer than human beings, and will continue long after us (2014 Conservation International). The central message is brutally simple: nature does not need us, but we need nature.

There is something chilling about this because we humans have spent much of our relatively short existence behaving as though the opposite were true. Somewhere along the way we seem to have convinced ourselves that we are all-powerful and in full control.

Perhaps we can even be excused for this arrogance. After all, have we not sent men to the moon and brought them safely back? We have split the atom, changed DNA, cloned mammals and, more recently, grown human embryo-like models in laboratories using stem cells, without the normal process of fertilisation involving sperm and an egg. We can transplant hearts, communicate instantly across the world, look billions of years back into the universe. And most recently we have now built machines capable of forms of reasoning that, until very recently, we thought belonged only to us.   These are astonishing achievements by any measure, and perhaps it is understandable that somewhere along this extraordinary journey of human advancement, we began to believe that there was very little beyond our control. In fact, we may perhaps be excused for occasionally thinking that we are God.

But despite all this extraordinary knowledge and technological capability, our influence over the great forces of nature remains surprisingly limited.   We have become increasingly sophisticated in predicting natural events and protecting ourselves from some of their consequences, but the forces that create cyclones, earthquakes, monsoons, floods and rising seas remain essentially beyond our control. We may be able to predict some of these events increasingly well, protect ourselves against some of their effects and sometimes reduce the damage they cause, but we certainly cannot control nature itself (2016, Yuval Noah Harari).

Perhaps our scientific achievements have therefore given us a rather dangerous illusion. We have become very good at manipulating parts of nature and, somewhere along the way, begun confusing that ability with mastery over nature itself.

Scientifically, of course, the answer has to be no. Nature does not have feelings, become angry with us or decide that humans have misbehaved and therefore send a cyclone, flood or landslide as punishment.

Nor should we fall into the increasingly common habit, encouraged by some climate change doomsday prophets, of attributing every natural disaster to climate change. Take earthquakes, for example, which are primarily geological events arising from movements within the Earth’s crust. Floods, cyclones, droughts, landslides and wildfires also occurred for millions of years before humans started burning fossil fuels on an industrial scale. The Earth has always been dynamic and, at times, extremely violent as it goes through its cycles of evolution.   It would therefore be overly simplistic, and scientifically wrong, to say that all the disasters we are seeing today have been caused by humans.

But that certainly does not exonerate us by any means, because we have definitely altered the conditions in which many natural events now occur. Human-induced climate change has warmed the atmosphere, oceans and land, and the evidence is now very strong that this warming is influencing many weather and climate extremes.

Rising sea levels worsen coastal flooding and storm surges, while prolonged heat and drought can leave landscapes much more vulnerable to wildfires. (2021, IPCC AR6 Working Group I- “Weather and Climate Extreme Events in a Changing Climate”)   However, long before climate change became the great environmental issue of our time, we were already busy dismantling many of nature’s own defences.

For generations we have cut forests from mountain slopes, filled wetlands, destroyed mangroves, interfered with rivers, fragmented ecosystems and covered enormous areas of land with buildings, roads and concrete. When the rains come and mountainsides collapse, we then call it a natural disaster. Forests that once slowed runoff and helped hold soil together have disappeared from many areas, and we seem surprised when torrential rain sends water, mud and debris rushing down the mountains. The recent landslides after heavy rains in many of our upcountry areas are classic examples.

Similarly, we have removed mangroves and other coastal vegetation without sufficiently considering the protection these natural barriers can provide. The 2004 tsunami also demonstrated the importance of retaining natural coastal buffers.   So while the rain causes the flooding, our own actions may have helped turn that flood into a disaster. This distinction is sometimes lost in the climate change debate because a natural hazard and a human disaster are not quite the same thing. Nature may cause the hazard, but the scale of the eventual catastrophe is determined by how well prepared we are and how vulnerable the people in its path are.   Perhaps one of our more fundamental errors has been to treat forests, rivers, oceans, wetlands, wildlife, soils and climate as though they were separate entities, when in reality they form interconnected ecosystems in which disturbing one component can have consequences elsewhere.   A forest holds soil, influences water flows, stores carbon and provides habitat; wetlands hold and slowly release enormous quantities of water; mangroves reduce coastal erosion and provide breeding grounds for marine life; and oceans absorb vast quantities of heat and carbon. If we remove or seriously alter enough of these components, eventually the wider system begins to behave differently.

Yet we still talk rather confidently about “managing wildlife”, “managing forests” and “managing natural resources”, almost as though nature were some badly run corporation waiting for humans to take charge. Sometimes I wonder whether it is we who require more management.

We constantly hear appeals that we must “save the planet”, an expression I have always found rather strange, because the planet will almost certainly survive us.   Earth has endured massive volcanic eruptions, asteroid impacts, ice ages, enormous climatic changes and several mass extinction events in which species disappeared, others evolved, ecosystems collapsed and different ones eventually developed.   If human beings disappeared tomorrow, forests would gradually reclaim abandoned cities, rivers would find their own paths, and wildlife would return to places from which we drove it. Evolution would continue doing what it has done for billions of years and the Earth would carry on quite well without us.   So perhaps we have the problem the wrong way round. We are not really trying to save the planet but trying to maintain the relatively narrow range of environmental conditions under which human civilisation, settlements and economies have developed, and can continue to function.   Seen in this context, the Mother Nature message becomes much harder to dismiss because, however much we may dislike admitting it, nature does not need us nearly as much as we need nature.   There is also a disturbing unfairness in all this because many of those who contributed least to climate change are among those most exposed to its consequences.   A poor family beside a river in Bangladesh, a mountain community in Nepal and a farmer in a remote village in Sri Lanka, have contributed very little to the accumulation of greenhouse gases generated during two centuries of industrialisation.    Yet they can lose their homes and livelihoods in a few hours. Richer countries generally have stronger infrastructure, better forecasting and warning systems, insurance, emergency services and greater financial resources to rebuild, while poorer countries often do not have these in sufficient measure.

Coming back to where we started, I don’t really believe nature is striking back at us because nature is not a person and is inanimate. What we are experiencing is something less dramatic, but perhaps more concerning. We are beginning to live with the consequences of interfering with immensely complex natural systems, that we still only partly understand.

Perhaps our greatest mistake has therefore been to imagine that we are somehow masters of this planet, standing above nature and deciding how much of it we can use, modify or destroy. In reality we are very recent arrivals, living within a natural system that operated for billions of years before us, and will probably continue for billions of years after us.

So perhaps nature is not striking back at us after all. We are now beginning to experience some of those consequences and, perhaps, what we are seeing is simply nature presenting us with the bill.
