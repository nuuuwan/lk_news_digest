Justice and controversy over monks’ attire in prison

Over the past few weeks controversy in our country has been raging over the right of prison authority’s to disrobe a monk (in this particular case, a re-convicted person). However, whether ‘the man of the cloth’ be he/she, Buddhist, Catholic, Muslim, Jewish or Hindu, under secular legal systems, justice does not differentiate between religious figures and lay people once convicted.

The present controversy arose after prison authorities unrobed a controversial, monk who  over the past decade or so, has been convicted many times over. For instance,  he was found guilty of criminally intimidating Ms Sandhya Eknaligoda, the wife of missing journalist Prageeth Eknaligoda. This accused man of the cloth was also sentenced to a prison term for Contempt of Court. He was later granted a presidential pardon by past president Maithripala Sirisena in May 2019. Additionally, he has also in the past attacked or caused attacks on members of the Muslim and Christian communities.

The Media reported the monk claimed he had destroyed 22 churches. The first question which arises however, is why is there so much commotion over the dress of a man convicted by Court (convicted of Contempt of Court, for hate crimes and the equally despicable crime of intimidating a woman who was seeking justice for her missing husband) rousing such a hornets’ nest?

Another question -- probably the more serious one, which arises -- is whether all people in our country are equal before the law. Or, are men and women of the cloth above the reach of the law? Yet another question is whether or not we as a people believe, criminal law and sentencing guidelines are blind to a person’s religious status.

What is shocking about the ongoing controversy, is the attempt to politicise the issue of this individual being charged before Court and his unrobing. Today,  the law is finally catching up with a man who broke the law with impunity. Rather than applaud justice finally catching up with the individual, particular political-party affiliated leaders are attempting to rouse religious sentiments as a means of gaining short-term political benefit.

Our politicians never seem to learn. Way back in 1957,  our then Prime Minister (S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike) signed what came to be known as the  Bandaranaike–Chelvanayakam Pact (B-C Pact) signed in July 26, 1957 –granting regional autonomy to the Northern and Eastern provinces and addressing grievances of the Tamil people.

Opportunistic politicians of the day roused racial hatred and forced the author of the B-C Pact to tear up the document unilaterally. The victory of racist forces that day finally led to a complete breakdown of relations between the Sinhalese and Tamil communities. It led to the almost three decade long civil war beginning in 1983.

Today,  once again opportunist politicians are attempting to rouse religious bigotry and race hatred using the issue of prison authorities unrobing  a religious figure to gain regain popular support. It also comes at a time when the fight against corruption is leading to political giants of yester-year ending up behind bars. What is most surprising however, is the fact that this man of the cloth at the centre of the controversy is not a first time offender. He has been found guilty to committing nefarious crimes several times earlier. Yet, this individual is not the clergyman convicted. Some of the more infamous figures include Fr. Mathew Pieris, who murdered his wife and the husband of his lover.

Pieris initially appeared in court for his legal proceedings wearing religious robes (cassock). However following his conviction he was required to wear standard prison dress rather than his clerical garments. Of greater import was the trial of Mapitigama Buddharakitha Thero and Thalduwe Somarama Thero  accused in the assassination case of premier SWRD Bandaranaike.  At the trial Buddharakitha Thero insisted on wearing his robes. Somarama Thero however, stopped wearing his robes at trial proceedings. The presiding judge    Justice T.S. Fernando observed that Talduwe Somarama ‘had a streak of conscience as he did not attend court in saffron robes.’

We insist the culprits be brought to justice,  politicians stop rousing religious bigotry and  justice be allowed to take its course.
