UNHRC: A sword of Damocles

Ahead of its 63rd regular session scheduled to be held from September 7 to October 7, the United Nations Human Rights Council (UNHRC) has reminded the Sri Lankan government of a list of its past commitments.

Some of these commitments have gone unfulfilled for the past two decades. Successive governments have either bought time in the guise of fulfilling them or rejected them.

The UN human rights body has been adopting resolutions on Sri Lanka since 2012, pressing successive governments to implement a credible accountability process for the human rights violations which took place during the three-decades of separatist war, especially during the last few years of it. However, those governments have, one way or another, managed to mark time for the past 14 years, making little progress in this process.

The only concrete action that  past governments have taken with regard to accountability for human rights violations was the establishment of the Office on Missing Persons (OMP) and the Office for Reparations (OR). Even those institutions have not satisfied the victims of rights violations.

The result is obvious and clear. The UNHRC has toughened its stance towards Sri Lankan governments gradually. Its first resolution on the country adopted in 2012 merely requested the Mahinda Rajapaksa government to implement the recommendations of its own Lessons Learnt and Reconciliation Commission (LLRC) which recommended a domestic mechanism to address the accountability issues.

However, the Rajapaksa government stated that the government is not committed to implement all the recommendations of the LLRC. Then, the so-called Yahapalana Government pledged the UNHRC to be faithful in respect of addressing these issues. The then Foreign Minister Mangala Samaraweera while assuring the creation of a truth-seeking mechanism told the UNHRC in 2015: “Don’t judge us by the broken promises, experiences and U-turns of the past.”

And the government led by President Maithripala Sirisena and Prime Minister Ranil Wickremesinghe co-sponsored that year’s resolution on the country which recommended a hybrid judicial mechanism with local and Commonwealth judges and lawyers serving in it, to address human rights issues. Three months into the pledge, the government made a dramatic volte-face with President Sirisena declaring that he would not allow foreign judges to sit at such a court.

The resolutions passed later covered not only rights violations during the war, but also those allegedly committed after the war. And also, the Human Rights Council, in 2021, adopted a resolution that might affect many politicians and high-ranking military officials and sometimes a few former LTTE leaders in future. It provides for the establishment of a central database at the Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR) where information and evidence of human rights abuses in Sri Lanka can be stored and analysed.

This international mechanism, the UNHRC says, will serve to support future trials of Sri Lankans accused of rights abuses in foreign countries and support victims of atrocities in their quest for justice.

The question is not purely a matter of trustworthiness on the part of  Sri Lankan leaders, but rather a situation where the hands of the leaders are tied by the circumstances, even if they genuinely want to proceed with UNHRC’s accountability process. Unlike the situation in countries like Pakistan, the Sri Lankan armed forces are largely independent of politics, serving the government faithfully and loyally  irrespective of the political affiliations  of the leaders. However, the leaders fear that antagonising them by taking legal action against the members of those forces would be dangerous and would push them to take political stands.

On the other hand, even a legal or disciplinary action against an individual or a group of military personnel that would clear the Sri Lankan armed forces as a whole from allegations of human rights violations might politically be labelled as a “Ranaviru Dadayama” or a witch hunt against war heroes. Hence, such actions are deemed politically suicidal. This is not just a presumption, but a regular occurrence whenever action is taken against wrongdoings by the armed forces personnel.

Against this backdrop of even the UNHRC seemingly preferring retributive justice to restorative justice, successive governments remain in a precarious situation regarding human rights issues irrespective of their political leanings.
