Key economic reform bills stuck at understaffed Legal Draftsmen’s dept.

Colombo, Oct. 29 (Daily Mirror) - Many draft laws, including the new State-Owned Enterprises (SOE) Act, have got stuck at the Legal Draftsman's Department, casting doubt on the government's plan to pass the SOE legislation by the end of the year, according to Labour Minister and Deputy Minister of Finance and Planning Dr. Anil Jayantha Fernando.

Speaking at the Institutional Investor Forum, jointly organised by the Securities and Exchange Commission of Sri Lanka (SEC) and the Colombo Stock Exchange (CSE), Dr. Fernando noted in response to a question on the SOE Act's timeline that "lots of the drafts got stuck within the Legal Draftsman's Department". While the government hopes to have the bill approved by Parliament by the end of the year, he admitted he was unsure whether that would be possible, with the next parliamentary session due in the first week of November.

"First we need to get it cleared from the Legal Draftsman's Department. If that is the case, at least we can submit it to the Parliament," he remarked.

The bill would allow selected state enterprises to be reorganised under one umbrella and incorporated as limited liability companies, giving them the autonomy to take decisions based on market conditions. Earlier this month, President Anura Kumara Dissanayake stated that the SOE law would go to the Cabinet within about two weeks, and that the government's economic reform bills would most likely be presented to Parliament by February.

Amendments to the Customs Ordinance, part of the government's revenue administration reforms, are also with the department. The Cabinet directed the Legal Draftsman to prepare the bill in June, based on the recommendations of a review committee chaired by retired Additional Solicitor General Milin de Gunathilaka, PC. Four months later, the President said the proposed amendments had been submitted to the department and that he expected the bill to be finalised promptly.

Meanwhile, the State Assets Management Bill is back at the drafting stage. The bill was gazetted in August 2024 but lapsed when Parliament was dissolved two months later. Earlier this month, the Cabinet halted the 2024 version and instructed the Legal Draftsman to prepare a fresh bill based on a revised framework drawn up by an expert committee.

A unified labour code, which Dr. Fernando had targeted for the end of this year in his capacity as Labour Minister, is still being drafted by an expert committee and must go before the National Labour Advisory Committee before it can be finalised.

The Public-Private Partnership (PPP) bill, on the other hand, has moved faster. Drafted by the Legal Draftsman and cleared by the Attorney General, it was approved by the Cabinet last week for publication in the Gazette ahead of its presentation to Parliament.

The Legal Draftsman's Department completed and returned only 35 bills in all three languages in 2025, down 42 percent from 60 in 2024, while revisions of subordinate legislation fell to 104 from 124, as per the Justice Ministry's annual performance report for 2025. The ministry noted that the department continued to function with a severe shortage of staff, and acknowledged that progress on legal reforms had been affected as a result.

The department was operating with 62 of its 154 approved posts vacant at the end of 2024, a vacancy rate of 40 percent, according to its own latest performance report. At the senior level, 24 of 47 approved posts were unfilled, including three Deputy Legal Draftsman positions. The report also points out that most senior drafters have retired, and that new recruits often leave because pay is low relative to the workload.

However, the ministry's figures show that much of the backlog lies outside the department. At the end of 2025, 74 bills that had been sent out as preliminary or completed drafts were awaiting observations or instructions, 13 from the Attorney General and 61 from other ministries and departments. That is more than double the number of bills the department completed during the year.

The department attributes the repeated return of completed drafts mainly to instructing agencies changing their policies. As per its 2024 report, the National Building Research Institute Bill was sent back for amendments 24 times and the Animal Welfare Bill 21 times, with each round demanding the same time and attention as a fresh request. The report adds that the department has no structured work programme of its own, as its workload is set by ministry requests once the Cabinet approves policies.

Turnaround times are further stretched by the requirement that every piece of legislation be completed in Sinhala, Tamil and English before it is released to the requesting ministry or department. Under the Constitution, all laws and subordinate legislation must be enacted and published in Sinhala and Tamil, together with an English translation.
