Apparel’s US $ billions mask deeper industry vulnerabilities

US-bound apparel exports hit a 5-year low in July, even as sector’s overall export decline stays short of that mark

Hela Apparel Holdings’ liquidation points to deeper strain within industry beyond headline trade numbers

Soft, still-fragile US consumer sentiment adds to demand-side pressure on Sri Lanka’s largest export market

Sri Lanka’s textile and apparel export earnings declined 8.55 percent year-on-year (YoY) to US $ 416.25 million in July 2026, a fall driven largely by a five-year low in shipments to the US, the sector’s single largest market, according to the trade data compiled by the Joint Apparel Association Forum Sri Lanka (JAAFSL).

The US-bound exports fell 6.22 percent YoY to US $ 167.18 million in July, the weakest July figure recorded since at least 2022, while the cumulative US exports for the January-July period also slipped to a five-year low of US $ 1,057.61 million, narrowly undercutting the previous low of US $ 1,058.34 million set in 2024.

The overall figure, however, stops short of a five-year low for the sector as a whole: July exports in 2023 (US $ 401.18 million) and 2024 (US $ 414.38 million) were both weaker than this year’s tally, making July 2026 the third-lowest of the past five years rather than an outright low.

The same holds cumulatively, with the total exports for January-July 2026 at US $ 2,728.33 million, down 6.44 percent YoY but still above the equivalent 2023 and 2024 totals.

By contrast, the exports to the European Union (down 8.41 percent to US $ 133 million), United Kingdom (down 6.72 percent to US $ 52.35 million) and other markets (down 15.67 percent to US $ 63.72 million) all remained above their 2023 lows for July, even as they too posted YoY declines.

The industry officials have pointed to the prolonged uncertainty over the US tariffs as a key drag on the sector through the first half of 2026.

Sri Lanka spent much of the year negotiating a final tariff rate under a US Section 301 investigation into imports linked to forced labour, at one point facing the prospect of a 12.5 percent additional duty while competitors such as Bangladesh, Cambodia, Pakistan and Indonesia were assured the lower 10 percent rate.

A 10 percent rate for Sri Lanka was only locked in from July 24, alongside 16 other countries, well after most of July’s shipments would have already been produced and booked.

JAAFSL Secretary General Yohan Lawrence said as early as February that the industry expected the export volumes to decline from early 2026 because of the softer US demand tied to the tariff overhang and the comparative advantage some competitor countries secured in their own negotiations.

The demand-side pressure in the US is compounding the trade-policy overhang. The University of Michigan’s Consumer Sentiment Index climbed to a five-month high of 55.2 in July, recovering from a record low touched in May, when the fuel prices spiked amid the Iran conflict but the reading still sat around 10-11 percent below its year-ago level. The sentiment slipped again in August, easing to 51.7, as the inflation concerns resurfaced.

A separate EY-Parthenon survey of US consumers conducted in June found that more than half of Americans had saved nothing the previous month and 72 percent still regard apparel, alongside dining and beauty products, as a discretionary category they can cut back on, if the household budgets tighten further.

The sector representatives have also cited a broader global apparel oversupply, compounded by the prolonged disruption from the Russia-Ukraine war and instability in the Middle East. Sri Lanka’s own prohibition on imports linked to forced labour, which took effect on July 10, has added a further compliance layer for the exporters sourcing raw material inputs.

Rather than a straight-line decline, the sector’s trajectory over the past five years has swung sharply: the textile and apparel exports fell by around 18 percent in 2023, amid the economic crisis, before recovering to a post-pandemic high of over US $ 5.5 billion in full-year 2025. The renewed softening in 2026 – most visibly in the US market’s five-year low – suggests that recovery is now under fresh strain.

That strain is visible at company level too. Hela Apparel Holdings PLC, one of Sri Lanka’s largest listed garment exporters and a long-standing supplier to global brands including Tommy Hilfiger, Calvin Klein and Michael Kors, filed for court-ordered winding up of itself and two subsidiaries, Hela Clothing and Foundation Garments, on August 5, 2026, after its board concluded it could no longer meet its debt obligations, following the prolonged liquidity constraints.

Trading in Hela’s shares were halted on June 18, after an independent audit issued a disclaimer of opinion on the company’s financial stability.

The group employed more than 17,000 people across 10 manufacturing sites in four countries; in Sri Lanka, the Board of Investment has confirmed that 3,674 affected workers have already been absorbed into Emerald Clothing.

Hela’s collapse is a reminder that steady headline export figures can still mask acute distress within individual companies in the sector.

The industry’s employment numbers are also worth weighing against its net contribution to the economy. The JAAFSL cites the sector as directly employing more than 350,000 people, with livelihoods for over a million more across its wider value chain.

However, because Sri Lankan apparel manufacturing still relies heavily on imported fabric, yarn and trims, the gross export figures overstate the industry’s net foreign exchange contribution.

The industry estimates put domestic value addition at around 55 percent of export value today, up from just 20-30 percent decades ago, meaning the actual net foreign exchange earnings from apparel are considerably below the headline dollar figures reported each month.
