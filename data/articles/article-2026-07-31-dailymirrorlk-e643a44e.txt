Betel Chew Ban Raises Serious Questions

The sudden Gazette notification prohibiting the inclusion of tobacco in betel victuals  has left both vendors and consumers confused. A traditional betel chew consists of betel leaves, areca nut, chunnam (slaked lime), and tobacco. For many users, particularly among low-income communities, it serves as an inexpensive intoxicant and a substitute for alcohol.

While protecting public health is a legitimate objective, the Government appears to have acted without fully considering the wider economic and social consequences. Sri Lanka, like India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, has a long tradition of betel chewing, and thousands of livelihoods depend on this trade.

The ban is likely to affect more than 100,000 betel vendors and around 10,000 tobacco growers, many of whom rely on this industry for their daily income. Furthermore,  banning tobacco in beetle victuals could encourage consumers  to  shift to illicit liquor. Before introducing such a far-reaching regulation, the authorities should have conducted a comprehensive impact assessment and explored measures to help those whose livelihoods that will be affected.

The policy also raises an important question of consistency. If the objective is to reduce the health risks associated with tobacco, why has the Government targeted only tobacco used in betel victuals while cigarettes, which are equally harmful, continue to be sold legally? Is this because of the substantial tax revenue generated by cigarette sales, or is there a reluctance to confront the powerful multinational company that dominates the tobacco market?

Public health policies should be consistent, evidence-based and balanced. They should protect public health without placing an unfair burden on small vendors and farmers while leaving larger commercial interests untouched.
