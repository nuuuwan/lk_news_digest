A Pyrrhic Triumph on the Bench

In politics, there are victories, but not all of them carry positive consequences in the long run. In contrast, victories entail disastrous consequences to the winner. It means victory carries the bitter taste of defeat.

The government’s arrogance in pushing forward with constitutional amendments to extend the mandatory retirement age of superior court judges—raising Supreme Court tenure to 67 and the Court of Appeal to 65—falls squarely into the latter category. Even if this law successfully navigates the parliamentary process, it will stand as a classic Pyrrhic victory: a hard-fought battle won on paper, but at the catastrophic expense of public confidence, the morale of the wider legal fraternity, and the sacrosanct global principles of good governance.

At its core, statecraft requires not merely the raw exercise of parliamentary majority, but the preservation of moral authority. The use of numerical strength to bulldoze its way is not statecraft at all.  Judicial independence is sacrosanct, and the mere public mandate should not be used as a trump card to subvert it in the interests of the ruling side.

When the government  chooses to press ahead with structural tenure changes despite the unanimous resistance from   the Bar Association of Sri Lanka (BASL), regional bodies like the Commonwealth Lawyers Association, and mounting civil disquiet, it signals a dangerous detachment from democratic consensus.

The government attempts to frame this contentious intervention as a move to retain expertise in the judiciary for dispensing with a backlog of cases. Officials point to the staggering backlog of over a million pending cases across the nation’s court system, arguing that retaining experienced judicial minds is the need of the hour. No one denies the severity of the crisis plaguing our courts. Systemic backlogs degrade the fundamental right to speedy justice.

However, diagnosing a fever does not justify administering poison. To treat a massive structural backlog by altering the foundational retirement and tenure rules of sitting judges mid-stream is a superficial fix that ignores underlying realities. If court capacity is strained, the remedies prescribed by global best practices are clear: expand support infrastructure, expedite normal appointments to existing vacancies, introduce rigorous digital case-management, and streamline procedural laws.

Global principles of good governance, articulated across the Commonwealth Latimer House Principles and international jurisprudence, emphasise a cardinal truth: justice must not only be done, but must manifestly and undoubtedly be seen to be done.

Judicial independence is fragile. It rests not on brick and mortar, but entirely on public trust. When tenure rules for active superior court judges are modified by the political branch, it inevitably invites public suspicion.

This policy creates internal fractures within the judicial hierarchy itself. As associations representing district judges and magistrates have warned, artificially freezing the apex of the legal system creates a logistical and psychological bottleneck. Promotion pathways stall, career progression for younger and ambitious legal minds is choked, and institutional stagnation trickles down from the top. If the administration forces this law through parliament, it may successfully alter the numerical age limits written into our constitutional text. But what will it have gained? It will have alienated the legal profession, darkened the window of institutional transparency, rattled public faith in impartial adjudication, and violated the spirit of cooperative governance.

To press forward under such a heavy cloud of united opposition is governance divorced from wisdom. True leadership recognises when a policy, however well-intentioned in rhetoric, costs more than the nation can afford to pay. Stepping back from this contentious amendment is not a sign of weakness; it is an act of supreme statesmanship.

It is still not too late for the government to take a step back and shelve the enactment of the proposed piece of legislation. Otherwise, it will be a pyrrhic triumph. The alienation of legal fraternity, a powerful voice and a lobby, does not bode well for any government in a democratic set-up.   In Sri Lanka, there are examples from the past.  The impeachment of then Chief Justice Shirani Bandaranayake turned out to be a pyrrhic victory for the Mahinda Rajapaksa government.  The incident event create internal rifts in that government with its leftist allies having dissension over the impeachment.
