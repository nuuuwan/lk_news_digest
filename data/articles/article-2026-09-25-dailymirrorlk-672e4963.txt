Anti-rabies drive: 1.7 million dogs vaccinated last year

Colombo, Sep. 26 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka is stepping up efforts to prevent rabies by expanding dog vaccination, sterilisation and public awareness programmes, with nearly 1.7 million dogs vaccinated in 2025 as the country continues its push to reduce human deaths from the disease, according to the National Anti-Rabies Campaign of the Public Health Veterinary Services.

The number of dogs vaccinated reached a record 1,690,887 last year, up from 1,280,775 in 2024 and 1,317,422 in 2023. The increase has coincided with a decline in human rabies deaths, which fell from 28 in 2022 to 16 in 2023, 20 in 2024 and 14 in 2025.

According to Dr Niroshan Gamage, Director of the National Anti-Rabies Campaign, dogs remain by far the main source of human rabies exposure in Sri Lanka. Laboratory surveillance conducted by the Medical Research Institute across multiple study periods has found that around 85% to 97% of confirmed rabies-positive samples come from dogs, while cats account for approximately 2% to 8%.

This makes mass vaccination (injections) of the canine population the main focus of Sri Lanka’s rabies-control efforts. The programme covers owned as well as community and free-roaming dogs. Oral rabies vaccination is also used for dogs that are difficult to catch or handle. The need for continued vaccination remains significant, as Sri Lanka is still considered a canine-rabies-endemic country, with the disease claiming an estimated 20 to 30 lives each year and hundreds of thousands of people bitten by dogs annually.

The country’s approach also extends beyond vaccination. Since 2007, Sri Lanka has followed a “No Kill” policy, replacing dog culling with animal birth control measures such as sterilisation and, where appropriate, hormonal contraception to manage the free-roaming dog population.

The Public Health Veterinary Services coordinates the National Rabies Control Programme, with regional and district veterinary teams carrying out dog vaccination and sterilisation programmes while also conducting public awareness and school education initiatives.

Another key component is protecting people after exposure. Rabies is transmitted through the saliva of an infected animal, most commonly through a bite, although scratches or licks on broken skin or mucous membranes can also transmit the virus. Once clinical symptoms appear, the disease is essentially untreatable, making prompt post-exposure treatment critical.

Post-exposure treatment for people bitten or otherwise exposed to potentially rabid animals is provided free of charge through government hospitals, forming the second major line of defence alongside preventing transmission among dogs.

The programme also relies on surveillance and coordination between veterinary and medical authorities, including the Medical Research Institute and the Department of Animal Production and Health, with technical support from international organisations such as the World Health Organisation (WHO), Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) and World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH).
