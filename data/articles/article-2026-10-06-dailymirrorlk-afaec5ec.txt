NPP’s dilemma over the PTA

The National People’s Power (NPP) government is facing a serious dilemma in keeping some of its pledges to the people given during the last Presidential and Parliamentary elections. The reason could be argued both ways -- insincerity as well as circumstantial pressure, based on one’s political affiliation.

One important acid test for the government is to adopt a new constitution replacing the current one, while another is replacing the current Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA) with another anti-terrorism Act that could be acceptable to the international community.

A new constitution would not be complete but would be a complete farce if it dodges two important issues –- finding a lasting solution to the ethnic problem and abolishing the executive Presidency, as promised by the NPP. Yet, no government would be successful in satisfying both the nationalists of either side of the ethnic divide.

On the other hand, given the threats to the NPP government in the light of its fragile vote bank, one should not be so innocent as to believe that the government would take the risk of taking measures to resolve the national issue and abolishing the executive Presidential mode of governance, in the near future.

However, it would be difficult for the authorities to drag their feet in implementing an alternative to the PTA. On the one hand, morality pressures the government. The JVP, the core party in the NPP coalition, had a strong case against the PTA as many of its past political activities were described by the past governments as terrorism, and thus came under the ambit of this law. In fact, the terms terrorists and terrorism  first appeared in Sri Lankan political literature and media to refer to the JVP and its activities during and subsequent to their first insurrection in 1971. Hence, the party has been since calling for a total repeal of the Act, without introducing any substitute.

Nevertheless, in a practical sense, the NPP learnt following its ascent to power that forces against the power is not always so innocent as to be handled leniently. Hardcore criminals and terrorists do not cooperate with investigators without pressure. They wouldn’t reveal vital information in response to a kind request. In some cases, long detention is needed to extract information or protect witnesses.

Yet, the PTA no doubt is a draconian piece of legislation. It violates the basic rights of those whom it is used against. It allows authorities to use confessions extracted through coercion—and even torture—as admissible evidence. It is one of the most vicious tools of suppression. Local as well as international human rights organisations have condemned it as one which goes against the ethos of internationally accepted huma rights and humanitarian laws. The United Nations Huma Rights Council (UNHRC) and the European Union have been pressing  past governments to rid it.

Finally, the Yahapalana Government had an undertaking with the UNHRC to replace the PTA with a counter terrorism law compliant with the international standards. Accordingly, that government in 2018 presented the Counter Terrorism Bill (CTB), but only to be rejected by  local as well as international human rights organisations, as it was more suppressive than the PTA, with the definition of terrorism in the new Bill being broader. Again, in 2023, the UNP/SLPP government presented a similar Bill, the Anti-Terrorism Bill (ATB) which too, was rejected on the same grounds.

Meanwhile, European Parliament on June 10, 2021 has threatened to withdraw the GSP + concessions awarded to Sri Lanka unless the country takes some positive steps towards PTA and those who had been arrested under it including Shani Abeysekara, the DIG who is now overseeing the CID. Sandwiched between the local circumstances and  international pressure, the NPP government presented a set of proposals for a  Bill called “Protection of the State from Terrorism” Bill (PSTB) last year and a modified version of it is to be presented in Parliament today.

In a way, the current Opposition parties – the SJB, UNP and the SLPP - are also in a dilemma in this regard. On what moral standing would they criticize the NPP’s PSTB after having presented harsher laws like the CTB and ATB during their time in government?

Any piece of legislation of this kind would contain measures to curtail personal liberty and would be prone to criticism as it is a device of suppression, not of concession. The government’s prudence lies in its ability to strike a balance between personal liberty and the national security.
