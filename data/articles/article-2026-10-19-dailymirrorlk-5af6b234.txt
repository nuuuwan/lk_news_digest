Don’t repeat Slave Island mistakes in Fort and Pettah, warns Ondaatjie

Sri Lanka must avoid repeating the redevelopment mistakes of Slave Island, where colonial mansions were demolished at the expense of local authenticity, and instead channel investment into transforming Colombo Fort, Pettah and regional fortifications into “living museums”, Tangerine Group of Hotels Managing Director Angeline Ondaatjie said.

Speaking at the 4th THINC Sri Lanka & Maldives conference in Colombo last week on the need to disperse tourism away from congested traditional sites, Ondaatjie noted that most of the island’s 2,000 years of heritage infrastructure remains untapped. While Slave Island has lost some of its authenticity after colonial mansions that should have been saved were pulled down, she pointed out that Fort and Pettah still hold underutilised assets, including the Dutch Museum, the museum at the Old Town Hall, an old bell tower and identifiable remnants of the original fort gates.

Stressing that these landmarks need protection, proper signage and a stronger visitor experience, she called for private capital to breathe new life into the precinct.

“There’s a lot of opportunity for investment to go into those old buildings and become maybe villas, restaurants, museums, galleries,” she remarked, adding that “it should be a fort that is a living fort”.

Her call dovetails with the World Bank’s planned Colombo tourism project, which World Bank Group Country Manager for Sri Lanka and Maldives Gevorg Sargsyan told the same panel would be worth roughly US$ 80 million, with Colombo Fort among its key clusters for infrastructure upgrades and a quasi-equity fund to back private-sector experiences. The project aims to build a 48 to 72-hour Colombo experience, whereas the average tourist currently spends less than a day in the capital, according to Sargsyan.

Outside Colombo, Ondaatjie highlighted that historic forts in Mannar, Jaffna and Trincomalee, as well as the Star Fort in Matara, lack adequate protection and fail to convey their pre-colonial history to visitors on the ground. Pointing to Trincomalee’s Fort Frederick, she observed that the site carries links to the Ramayana, while remnants of an old temple destroyed by the Portuguese still lie outside Swami Rock.

Rather than relying on the standard pitch of visiting the Cultural Triangle and spending a few days on the beach, she urged the industry to reshape its narrative around the island’s ancient maritime history. By connecting the dots between historic ports such as Roman-era Mantai in Mannar and Trincomalee, Sri Lanka can position Anuradhapura as a cosmopolitan crossroads where traders were forced to stay between the monsoons, she argued, pointing to artefacts such as its Nestorian cross, which departs from traditional Buddhist iconography.

Opening up these ancient routes would draw travellers to what she described as lesser-visited sites such as Tantirimale, Koneswaram Temple, Velgam Vehera and the coastal Muhudu Maha Viharaya in the east, though she cautioned that visitor capacity must be limited so they do not become “another Sigiriya”.

She also contended that the country now has the opportunity to market Polonnaruwa as one of the best-preserved Chola cities, an angle she said would have been controversial over the past 75 years, and to link the architecture of Yapahuwa with Cambodia’s Angkor Wat, which belongs to the same historical timeline. Surrounding these lesser-known heritage sites with privately built museums and smaller villas, she added, would allow Sri Lanka to “make the journey the adventure”. (NF)
