Cricket lies dying as politics and cash rule the roost

The cricketing season is with us. Our men’s and women’s teams are doing battle with the best and weakest cricket playing nations. Our men’s team is being mercilessly thumped in England. The Lankan lasses are doing much better,  though without the fanfare their male counterparts receive.

Most of us Lankans -- thanks to the indebtedness of our country -- cannot afford to follow our teams as they travel to diverse cricketing venues in different parts of the world. But, thanks to our government’s efforts in sending our young people to different parts of the world in search of employment, it has ensured in our cricketers receiving a degree of support as they match skills with their international counterparts.

Cricket in its pristine form was about the spirit of sportsmanship, fairness and respect of law. It was above the final runs on the board. It was not about winning or losing, but how you played the game. But as Bob Dylan sang not so long ago, ‘The Times They Are-a Changing’.

Cricket like all things in this world is also changing. Sometimes for the better, but occasionally things get worse before they get better. For instance during a bygone era, the ‘umpire’s word in cricketing parlance ‘was law’. He/she – the umpire -- was on-field and none questioned his decision.

That law now has now been turned on its head. The on-field umpire’s word is no longer law. Players often question his decisions and are referred to a third umpire sitting alone with machines to aid him. He verifies the on-field umpire’s decision and delivers his verdict.

While the third umpire decisions ensures exactitude, acceptance of an umpire’s decision was an acceptance that an umpire could be wrong, but his/her mistake was due to human error and had to be accepted as such. It also ensured the respect due to the position of the umpire.

The change has eroded players’ attitude to the umpire as well as their behaviour on and off field. For instance after the Asia Cup in September last year, the Indian team refused to shake hands with their Pakistani counterparts. Yet respecting ones opponents has been a tradition of the game.

At the time India was angered at a terror attack carried out allegedly by a Pakistani group at Pahalgam. If the Indian cricketing authorities wanted to protest the attack, they could have protested Pakistan’s participation in the tournament or refused to play against that team. By refusing to shake hands,  they dragged India’s national politics into the game.

According to the ‘Indian Express’, reports made it clear the act (refusing to shake hands) was “…premeditated and carefully choreographed. At the toss both captains skipped the usual pre-match handshake – a directive reportedly relayed by the match referee to Pakistan in advance.

During the recently concluded Women’s Asia Cup tournament, the victorious Indian women’s cricket team refused to accept the trophy from the president of the Asia Cup tournament because he was also a Pakistani Cabinet Minister.

Yet, the ICC took no corrective action against the BCCI. However, when the Lankan government took steps to stamp out corruption in Sri Lanka Board of Cricket, the ICC immediately took away Lanka’s hosting rights to the inaugural Women’s Champions Trophy (2027) claiming government interference in cricket administration!

In February this year, there was another controversy as the Bangladeshi Cricket Board (BCB)  requested their T20 World Cup Tournament games scheduled to be played in India be shifted to Sri Lanka  citing security concerns due to growing tensions between the countries.

ICC rejected the request. After being given a final deadline to play in India, the BCB refused to travel, and  ICC  replaced Bangladesh with Scotland.  Bangladesh felt poorly treated.

Cricket fans around the world ask why this double standards where the BCCI is concerned. Is it because of the BCCI’s huge financial clout,  as during the colonial era ‘Britain ruled the waves and waived the rules.’

But then again these ‘outdated’ rules and traditions were made at a time when cricket was played for the joy of  the game. Today cricket has become a well-paid job and is played as an occupation.
