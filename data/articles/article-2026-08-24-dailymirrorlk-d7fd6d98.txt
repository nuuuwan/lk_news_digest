As inflation bites, shoppers ditch brands for bargains

Sparkwinn’s household panel shows spending on non-branded essentials rebounded to 57% by June, as consumers scaled back branded consumption to cope with rising costs

Headline inflation accelerated to 7.2% in July, driven by food inflation climbing to a multi-year high of 6.3%

Budget brands and supermarket private labels may gain traction as consumers turn increasingly value-conscious, Sparkwinn’s Perampalam says

Sri Lankan consumers are spending less on branded goods and more on loose, generic products as inflation rises, according to the latest household panel data from Sparkwinn Research.

The shift comes after a year in which branded spending had been recovering, following a period of deflation that set in after the 2022 economic crisis, when inflation topped 70 percent.

The panel shows branded goods’ share of household spending rose from 38 percent in July 2025 to a peak of 49 percent between February and April 2026, a period of relatively low inflation. That share fell to 44 percent in May and 43 percent in June as inflation picked up, while the share of non-branded goods — loose, generic and unpackaged items such as vegetables, fruit and meat — rose to 57 percent.

Headline inflation rose from 5.4 percent in April to 7.2 percent in July. Food inflation, which hits households more directly, reached a multi-year high of 6.3 percent in July. Wages haven’t kept pace, and the price increases have pushed consumers to prioritise necessities over discretionary purchases.

The reversal has tracked a sharp acceleration in prices. Headline consumer inflation climbed from 5.4 percent in April to 5.5 percent in May and 6.8 percent in June, before rising further to 7.2 percent in July. Food inflation, more directly felt at the household level, rose even faster, hitting a multi-year high of 6.3 percent in July. With wages struggling to keep pace with the rising cost of living, the consecutive price hikes have placed renewed and severe pressure on household purchasing power, forcing shoppers to prioritise basic necessities over discretionary spending.

“Over the past two to three quarters, our household panel had shown a gradual increase in consumer spending on branded goods. However, as inflation gathers momentum, household budgets are likely to come under renewed pressure, prompting consumers to seek more affordable and value-driven ways to meet their everyday needs,” Sparkwinn Research Managing Director Suthaharan Perampalam told Mirror Business.

Sparkwinn has been running the household panel for the past 14 months, tracking consumer spending on branded and non-branded goods among a selected panel of respondents through a diary-based approach.

The shift is already showing up in corporate earnings. Among listed companies with FMCG operations, the slowdown in demand has been most evident in non-essential, discretionary categories such as homecare, where households scaled back consumption during the June quarter to protect budgets for essentials.

Despite the contraction in branded goods’ share, Perampalam cautioned against reading the trend as a wholesale rejection of brands, describing it instead as a strategic budgeting response by households.

“At the outset, it may appear that a fraction of consumers are moving away from brands, but what we are seeing is a declining trend in spending on branded goods,” he said. “This could be an early indication, potentially linked to rising inflation. Consumers may not be rejecting brands; they are managing branded consumption to stay within tighter budgets.

They may trade down, buy smaller packs, purchase less frequently, choosing cheaper ones over premium, or wait for promotions, while continuing to choose trusted brands selectively.”

For FMCG companies navigating the shift, Perampalam pointed to pack-size and pricing strategy over blanket discounting.

“For brands, the opportunity is to protect affordability through pack-size options, value packs, targeted promotions and clear value propositions, rather than relying solely on price cuts,” he said.

Looking ahead, Perampalam said budget brands and supermarket private labels could gain further ground as household budgets stay stretched.

“As inflation rises and household wallets come under pressure, budget brands and supermarket private labels may gain traction as consumers become increasingly value-conscious,” he said. “Where the price differential is meaningful, consumers may be more willing to consider supermarket-own label alternatives, particularly in frequently purchased, everyday categories.”

He added that the shift could reshape competition between retailers and established brands.

“This could create an opportunity for supermarkets to strengthen their own-label propositions, while established brands may need to reinforce their value proposition by balancing affordability with the quality and trust that underpin brand choice,” he said.
