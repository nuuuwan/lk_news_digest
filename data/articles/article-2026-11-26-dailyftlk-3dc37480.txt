Dengue: A persistent national  threat demanding year-round action

Dengue has become an all-too-familiar part of life in Sri Lanka. Each year, particularly with the arrival of the monsoon rains, rising case numbers prompt renewed public concern, mosquito-control campaigns and calls for greater vigilance. Yet, as the immediate threat subsides, public attention often diminishes, only to return with the next outbreak.

This recurring pattern raises an important question: Have we begun to accept dengue as an inevitable part of life, rather than a preventable public health threat that demands sustained national action?

For decades, dengue has remained one of Sri Lanka’s most persistent public health challenges. Despite considerable progress in disease surveillance, clinical management and vector control, it continues to impose a substantial burden on individuals, families, healthcare institutions and the national economy.

The magnitude of this challenge is evident in our epidemiological experience. In 2017, Sri Lanka experienced its largest recorded dengue epidemic, with 186,101 suspected cases and 440 reported deaths. Nearly a decade later, the country continues to face significant outbreaks, demonstrating that dengue remains far from being brought under sustainable control.

The situation in 2026 is particularly concerning. According to surveillance figures reported in November, Sri Lanka had recorded 110,431 dengue cases and 82 deaths by November 2026. The Western Province continued to bear a disproportionate burden, while transmission remained a concern across the country.

These figures represent much more than epidemiological statistics. Behind every reported case is an individual whose health, education or livelihood may be disrupted, a family experiencing anxiety and, in severe cases, a patient requiring intensive medical care. Every preventable death is a reminder that dengue remains a potentially fatal disease, even in a country with extensive experience in its clinical management.

Early recognition and timely medical intervention are therefore critical. Dengue commonly presents with fever, headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, nausea and sometimes a rash. However, the most dangerous period may begin as the fever subsides, when some patients enter the critical phase of illness.

Warning signs such as severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, marked lethargy, restlessness or signs of circulatory compromise require urgent medical assessment. Patients with suspected dengue should seek timely medical advice, maintain appropriate hydration and avoid medications such as aspirin and ibuprofen because of the risk of bleeding. Early identification, careful monitoring and appropriate fluid management can substantially reduce the risk of death.

Importantly, dengue is no longer a concern confined to Colombo or other densely populated urban centres. Although the Western Province accounts for a substantial proportion of cases, the disease affects communities across Sri Lanka. Rapid urbanisation, inadequate waste management, construction activities, population movement, rainfall patterns and environmental conditions all contribute to the persistence and spread of mosquito breeding sites.

Changing weather patterns and increasing climatic variability further complicate dengue prevention. However, rainfall alone does not explain the problem. Poor environmental management, neglected premises, improperly stored water and inadequate community participation create conditions that allow mosquito populations to thrive.

The fundamental challenge is therefore not simply controlling dengue during an outbreak but preventing the conditions that allow repeated outbreaks to occur.

Sri Lanka has developed considerable expertise in dengue management, supported by dedicated healthcare professionals, public health teams and national surveillance systems. The National Dengue Control Unit and other health authorities have played an important role in coordinating prevention and response activities. Nevertheless, the continuing burden of disease demonstrates that healthcare-sector interventions alone cannot provide a lasting solution.

Every household should routinely inspect its surroundings and eliminate potential mosquito breeding sites. Water-collecting containers, discarded tyres, blocked gutters, flowerpots, construction materials and improperly managed waste require particular attention. Water-storage containers must be securely covered and regularly cleaned. Personal protection through appropriate clothing, mosquito repellents and measures to prevent mosquito bites during the day should also be encouraged.

However, individual responsibility, while essential, is insufficient without supportive public systems.

Local authorities must ensure effective solid-waste management, drainage maintenance and environmental sanitation. Schools and workplaces should adopt regular, structured dengue-prevention programmes rather than limiting activities to periods of heightened transmission. Construction sites abandoned buildings and public spaces require systematic monitoring and accountability.

Equally important is strengthening coordination between the health sector, local government, education authorities, environmental agencies, community organisations and the private sector. Dengue control should be embedded within routine institutional responsibilities, supported by clear accountability, reliable surveillance and timely action.

Sri Lanka’s National Dengue Review in August 2026 reinforced the importance of evidence-based interventions, stronger operational coordination, sustained preparedness and active community participation. These priorities should now translate into measurable action at national, provincial, district and community levels.

A more proactive approach is essential. Surveillance data, mosquito breeding indices, weather information and local epidemiological trends should be used to identify high-risk areas and guide targeted interventions before outbreaks escalate. Public communication must also move beyond occasional warnings towards sustained education that encourages practical behavioural change.

Ultimately, successful dengue control should not be measured solely by the number of emergency campaigns conducted or premises inspected during an outbreak. It should be judged by sustained reductions in transmission, preventable hospitalisations and deaths, supported by healthier and safer environments.

Sri Lanka has demonstrated its capacity to achieve remarkable public health outcomes through strong preventive services, community engagement and coordinated national programmes. The same commitment must now be directed towards dengue.

The goal should not merely be to respond effectively to the next dengue outbreak, but to prevent that outbreak from occurring wherever possible.

Dengue does not respect administrative boundaries, socioeconomic divisions or seasonal public health priorities. Our response must therefore be continuous, coordinated and inclusive.

The responsibility extends beyond the Ministry of Health. It belongs to every institution, every community and every citizen.

Dengue may be a year-round threat but preventing it must become a year-round national commitment.

(The author is Consultant Paediatrician, President-Elect, Sri Lanka College of Paediatricians and Immediate Past President, Sri Lanka Medical Association)
