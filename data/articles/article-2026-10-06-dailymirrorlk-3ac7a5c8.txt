Gen Z reshapes political change across the world

Gen Z frenzy is spreading in Europe. From September to mid-November 2025, the website esu-online.org  tracked 25 distinct student protests across the European Higher Education Area, with some starting even in January 2024

The dynamics of political change across countries are undergoing changes. Political change is no longer brought about only by elections involving conventional political parties and established leaders, but by agitations and street actions by Gen Z.

A Gen Z grouping is an inchoate, unorganised, and spontaneously formed group surfacing in response to the emergence of intolerable conditions in the social, economic or political environment. Gen Z have taken to the streets to bring down the mighty, once thought to be invincible, and institutions believed to be stable, enduring, and taken for granted.

In recent years, one has been seeing this phenomenon in country after country. In the South Asian region, it appeared first in Sri Lanka in 2022, when the Gen Z-led non-partisan “Aragalaya” movement ousted strongman Gotabaya Rajapaksa. Two years later, agitating university students drove  entrenched Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina into exile. In 2025, a short but violent street agitation by youngsters led by  popular rapper Balen Shah, overthrew the rule of the old guard whose epitome was the aged veteran, Prime Minister K.P.Oli.

Come 2026, India’s Gen Z woke up from deep slumber and defiantly agitated to get rid of  powerful Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan for alleged corruption. It is now gunning for the scalp of the Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar and even the iconic Prime Minister Narendra Modi for allegedly vitiating the electoral system.

Though South Asia, governments’ efforts to break the will of the agitators with brute force only added fuel to the fire. The Indian agitation led by the Cockroach Janata Party (CJP) and the Congress is spreading across the country. Political parties wedded to conventional electoral politics are now being forced to rethink their staid and conventional ways and ride on the new Gen Z wave.

This growing trend could force the political class and the general public to revise their long-held theory that political change can only come about through conventional party politics and elections. The new political tsunami wrecks systems taken to be deeply rooted. Instruments of power, deadly in normal times, suddenly fail to function.

Even as India is engulfed in a Gen Z agitation, school and university students in Paris and other towns of France are on a rampage to draw the attention of the authorities to the poor educational infrastructure and a general neglect of education as such by the government. The French government is struggling to deal with its worst crisis in months. Hundreds of young people have been arrested as the movement threatens to roll on into next week. The government has had to close 400-500 schools.

In Paris, a Spanish language student, Sophia, on the frontline of a barricade told “The Guardian”: “We just want to be listened to by the government. We’d rather stand here doing this than study under poor conditions. It’s the only way to make ourselves heard.”

“School is a right, not a privilege,” read a cardboard sign as Sophia listed the grievances of teenagers across France: a lack of state investment in education, overcrowded classes of up to 40 children in crumbling buildings, poor teacher pay, staff absences and an unequal system in which social background determined success.

“The education budget is being cut to shreds while so much extra funding is being put into the army,” Sophia said as several teachers stood by the barricades in support of their students. They said ceilings partially collapsed during class, leaks, cockroaches and buildings in disrepair were common. Others complained of a lack of teaching staff.

The government said the grievances were legitimate but condemned what it called “urban violence”. But students have accused the police of using excessive force and causing injuries.

Several students have been injured in the protests, while the education ministry said 65 education staff were injured, including 40 school principals. An investigation is under way after a 14-year-old boy in Val d’Oise suffered serious injuries to his mouth and jaw after police allegedly beat him and fired a rubber-bullet launcher. The family’s lawyer said the boy had thought he was “going to die”.

Camille, 18, said pupils were also protesting over the French government’s lack of engagement over poor results in international education rankings. “France has disastrous results and yet we’re one of the countries with the longest school days,” she said.

Sofiane, 19, who was studying literature, said students feared the government would scrap its special €1 meals for struggling students and reduce welfare aid for housing. Time magazine reported that 3,000 people have been arrested since the protests began nearly two weeks ago outside of Paris, According to education minister Édouard Geffray, nearly 400 police officers have also reportedly suffered injuries.

Education Minister Geffray met with education unions, student organizations, and parent associations on Friday morning in an attempt to temper the protests through dialogue.  He announced a platform students can use to submit proposals for education reform, which he said will be evaluated and used for a “national report.” But High school student organisations, however, have called for continued demonstrations.

“We are 35 per ‌class. We don’t have enough chairs in classrooms,” a 15-year-old student told Reuters in the Paris suburb of Pantin.

“I hear and understand the anxieties and anger that our youth may have,” French President Emmanuel Macron said on Wednesday while speaking in Madrid. “However, none of these debates, none of these fears legitimises violence.”

Gen Z frenzy is spreading in Europe. From September to mid-November 2025, the website esu-online.org  tracked 25 distinct student protests across the European Higher Education Area, with some starting even in January 2024. They stretched across 13 countries: Armenia, Belgium, France, Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy, the Netherlands, Romania, Serbia, Spain, Switzerland and Türkiye. This is not yet a complete picture of Europe, but it is already a significant cross section.

Fifteen of the 25 protests were coded as “nationwide”, meaning that they spread beyond a single campus or city; seven were medium-sized actions (around 100 to 1000 participants), and two were large mobilisations (over 1000 participants). Twenty-three of the 25 protests started in 2025, showing how quickly this new wave escalated; two had begun already in 2024 and continued into 2025.

Most protests were “intersectional”, touching several issues at once. Even so, some themes dominated. Out of 25 protests, 13 cases explicitly about governance/democracy; three added corruption and nepotism and three highlighted expression and media freedom. In other words, students are not only fighting for educational goals but for democratic space in the society at large.

Twelve of the twenty five protests were over economic and living conditions; twelve to access and safety in education; twelve referenced social Justice and equity. Eight protests targeted governance in educational institutions, covering issues around campus decision-making, privatisation, disciplinary procedures, divestment and the political responsibility of universities. Seven protests included Human Rights & International solidarity. But only one protest is tagged under Environmental & Climate Justice.

If we look at the demands behind these causes, certain demands are repeated such as: higher education funding, housing, abolition of fees, government opacity, safety and infrastructure, accountability for violence and direct or indirect involvement in wars abroad.

Student activism does not happen only online and on social media. Across the twenty five protests studied, street demonstrations appear in twenty four out of twenty five cases, rallies in nineteen, and marches in seventeen. Therefore, physical presence in a specific area remains the backbone of student power.

Strikes were a feature in twelve protests. Laying siege, boycotting and blockading, each appeared in four cases. Students not only sent messages; they also interrupted the normal functioning of universities and public life to make their case heard.
