Unregulated digital currency use by tourists and expats sparks concerns over formal forex drain

A leading legal expert has raised concerns over the growing use of cryptocurrency in Sri Lanka’s informal sector, warning that widespread digital asset transactions by foreign tourists and emigrating locals are bypassing the formal banking channels and directly impacting the country’s foreign exchange inflows.

Sudath Perera Associates Partner and Head of Corporate and Commercial Law Dushyantha Perera recently observed that popular tourist destinations such as Unawatuna are seeing a proliferation of unregulated crypto exchanges.

“One thing that struck me is how prevalent cryptocurrency use is for cross-border capital transfers and current transactions, particularly among the Russian and Ukrainian tourists,” Perera noted, following a recent visit to the southern coast.

Establishments openly offering to buy and sell crypto have emerged alongside traditional tourist infrastructure, enabling the visitors to bring funds in and out of the country outside the formal financial system.

This parallel financial ecosystem is believed to be impacting the state tax revenues and domestic economic indicators. Although Sri Lanka is aggressively pursuing national targets of 5 million arrivals and US $ 8 billion in tourism income, the authorities have been attempting to understand a recent decline in individual tourist spending.

“Most of these Russian and Ukrainian visitors trade in crypto to get funds in and out of the country and to pay for things locally,” Perera stated.

He suggested that this shift to the crypto space should be closely evaluated alongside the depreciation of the rupee and changing tourist demographics.

The issue extends beyond the tourism sector, serving as a conduit for significant capital flight among the locals. With strict limitations on outward investment and foreign exchange allowances for migration, Perera highlighted that capital flight through the use of cryptocurrency and major exchanges has been common in Sri Lanka since the COVID-19 pandemic.

He noted that the current regulations impose a 20 percent migration tax and limit the initial migration allowances to US $ 100,000, which has inadvertently driven the individuals toward the grey-market methods to bypass the formal banking sector, despite the associated legal risks.

To address these systemic leaks, Perera stressed the necessity of a more proactive and holistic regulatory approach.

“We need to move towards a more comprehensive framework that also recognises these as an asset class and has provisions for licensing, taxation and consumer protection,” he urged, noting that Sri Lanka could take guidance from jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom, Australia and Singapore.

In a promising shift from previous Central Bank warnings, the government has recently accelerated efforts to formalise the sector. In June 2026, the Securities and Exchange Commission and Digital Economy Ministry launched a joint initiative to explore and regulate virtual assets, aiming to develop a comprehensive regulatory roadmap.

Furthermore, the government’s Sub Committee on Virtual Asset Service Providers, chaired by Digital Economy Deputy Minister Eranga Weeraratne, has been actively advancing a regulatory framework to strengthen the oversight in the rapidly evolving space.
