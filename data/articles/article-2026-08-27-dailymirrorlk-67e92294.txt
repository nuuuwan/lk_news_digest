Returning refugees and challenges ahead for the govt.

The letter noted that around 89,000 Sri Lankan Tamils continue to reside in Tamil Nadu, both inside refugee camps and outside.

Sri Lankan Tamils who fled to Tamil Nadu during the height of the armed conflict have been living in isolated conditions for many years. In May 2025, a 75-year-old Tamil refugee was arrested as he returned to the island at Palaly airport, despite being recognised by the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) and obtaining all necessary clearance. This incident raised concerns regarding Sri Lanka’s sensitivity towards the refugee repatriation process.

The issue of repatriating Tamil refugees has been brought to the attention of successive governments despite having no response.

Following a cabinet meeting held earlier this month, it was decided that the government would facilitate clearance of immigration during the voluntary repatriation of individuals who had left the country without using an approved port and a formal passport during the period of the war, sought refuge in India, but proved the nationality as Sri Lankan.

The United Nations and many international organisations welcomed this decision, recognising it as a ‘key step towards a safe and dignified return after years of displacement.’

In February this year, just two months prior to the Tamil Nadu Assembly elections, Chief Minister M. K. Stalin wrote a letter to Prime Minister Narendra Modi seeking a “humane and pragmatic” resolution to the long-pending citizenship issue and legal status of Sri Lankan Tamils living in the state for over four decades.

The letter noted that around 89,000 Sri Lankan Tamils continue to reside in Tamil Nadu, both inside refugee camps and outside. Nearly 40 per cent of them were born in India, and many have lived here for over 30 years. But according to Indian media reports, despite their prolonged stay, only 11 Sri Lankan Tamil refugees have been granted citizenship. Of them, just one has secured voting rights, while the remaining ten have applied for inclusion in electoral rolls, highlighting the limited progress in regularising their status.

A survey conducted by the State Commissionerate of Rehabilitation and Welfare of Non-Resident Tamils revealed that 130 individuals born before July 1, 1987 were eligible to apply for Indian passports.

In May 2025, the Supreme Court of India dismissed a petition from a Tamil refugee and former LTTE member who sought permission to remain in the country with his family, in a ruling that underscores the precarious legal status of Tamil refugees in the country. A bench comprising Justice Dipankar Dutta and K. Vinod Chandran ruled that “India is not a dharamshala to host refugees from all over the world and that the country is struggling with a 140 crore population.”

Returning to their motherland is not an easy process. Recent policy changes require refugees to undergo mandatory screening by the State Intelligence Service rather than regular police checks. Returnees accused of treason, murder or other wrongdoings would be detained upon their arrival. Children born in Indian refugee camps face complex citizenship processing hurdles.

Repatriating Tamil refugees is indeed a welcome move. They have lived in exile their entire life and they have a right to return to their motherland. But it has to be done with a strategy. They shouldn’t face a plight similar to that of Malaiyaha Tamils. They were brought down as indentured labour,  but it took them decades to get an identity of their own and voting rights.

The government should be transparent about their plan to repatriate refugees. It cannot be done in an ad hoc manner such as on a day to commemorate refugees etc. It certainly cannot be another media show because this government needs to be moved beyond just ticking off their bucket list of promises for the sake of it. People need tangible change. Not the kind of change that would look good on record books.
