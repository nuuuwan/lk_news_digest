The familiar cycle of a power system in crisis

But despite this sudden turn of events, the government seems to be handling the situation wisely.

Sri Lankans experienced a nostalgic feeling on October 17 following the islandwide blackout. But this time it hadn’t been a monkey that caused the chaos.

The blackout lasted for over three hours impacting water treatment plants, mobile connectivity, and other essential services. The suspected cause according to Energy Minister Anura Karunathilaka is a failure at the Upper Kotmale hydropower station combined with grid instability. This reportedly forced all three units at the Norochcholai power plant to trip and shutdown as a safety precaution. As a result, the NSOC requested all rooftop solar system owners to disconnect their systems until further notice to support the restoration process.

However, opposition factions speculated whether the blackout had any link to substandard coal imports due to the breakdown of Generator No. 1 at the Norochcholai power plant. But cabinet spokesperson Dr. Nalinda Jayatissa denied these claims.

According to NSOC, the output of Generator No. 1 at the Norochcholai power plant had plunged from 187 Megawatts to less than 5 Megawatts within a very short time. This had contributed to the islandwide power failure. Speaking at a special media briefing, NSOC Chief Executive Officer Buddhika Samarasekara explained that the stability of the grid depends on maintaining system frequency within the prescribed operating range. Subsequently, all three generators were shut down to prevent equipment damage. The national grid has lost 810 Megawatts as a result of the breakdown of three generating units at the Norochcholai Power Plant. However, the sudden disruption had caused the system frequency to collapse rapidly, resulting in the blackout.

Subsequently the Public Utilities Commission of Sri Lanka (PUCSL) approved one hour power cuts in four different zones from October 19 till October 21 to manage the electricity supply.

Addressing Parliament,  Minister Karunathilaka questioned why there had been such risky maintenance work at the coal power plant on this particular day when there was enough sunlight to generate solar power and the electricity demand was minimal. He also raised a question as to how the breakdown in one generator affected the entire system.

Minister Jayatissa said that Generator 1 was shut down for 61 days due to maintenance work and that since September 4, newly imported coal is being used. He said that an internal investigation is underway to determine why Generator No. 1 was shutdown.

One of the main objectives of restructuring the Ceylon Electricity Board was to carry out unbiased grid planning, energy procurement and supply-demand balancing on system needs rather than utility preferences. Financial losses, structural and operational pressures also contributed to this overhaul.

Sri Lankans faced severe inconveniences in 2022 due to prolonged power cuts that went beyond 3 hours. The PUCSL said that the country lost Rs. One billion per day as a result of these power cuts in 2022. Speaking in Parliament MP Ravi Karunanayaka claimed that the loss incurred due to the recent blackout is Rs. 56 billion.  But despite this sudden turn of events, the government seems to be handling the situation wisely. With past experiences, people are already frustrated whenever the government announces a power cut or water cut.  Disconnecting supplies for maintenance activities is understood,  but when sudden incidents take place, people may go into panic mode. Frequent power cuts and sudden blackouts may not reflect positively on the incumbent government; particularly because of the promises that they have delivered.

Perhaps this is an opportune moment to do away with archaic approaches to procuring coal, emergency purchases and relying on fossil fuels. Instead,  the government could gradually implement greener and sustainable alternatives and ensure that there are no more surprises in future !
