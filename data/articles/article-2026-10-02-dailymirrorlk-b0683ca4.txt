SL must take disability hiring out of CSR box to catch up: HR expert

Sri Lanka must move disability employment out of the corporate social responsibility (CSR) and welfare box and onto an economic agenda if persons with disabilities are to secure dignified and productive work, with the country still lagging on workplace access, DFCC Bank Chief Human Resources Officer Padmasiri Senanayake urged.

Addressing a session on partnerships for social inclusion and equity at the 49th Annual Meeting of the Association of Development Financing Institutions in Asia and the Pacific (ADFIAP), hosted by DFCC Bank in Colombo this week, Senanayake argued that employability, more than access to credit, offers the most sustainable route to inclusion for women, youth and persons with disabilities. “Persons with disability, if I take Sri Lanka, we are still a bit backward in this area and the journey is much further,” he told delegates, observing that support for the community varies widely across Asia.

Advocata Institute Chief Executive Officer Dhananath Fernando told an earlier session that 1.8 million young people would join the labour force over the next few years. Senanayake estimated that around 45 percent of them would be women and 4 to 5 percent persons with disabilities. That would place between 72,000 and 90,000 persons with disabilities in the cohort, and the question he raised was how many of them are actually employable. His estimate sits just above the national picture. As per DCS census data for 2024, 727,293 people aged five and above were identified as having significant disabilities, a national rate of 35 per 1,000, or 3.5 percent. A wider group of 3,223,413, or 157 per 1,000, reported at least one physical or mental difficulty. Sri Lanka enacted the Protection of the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act No. 28 of 1996 and ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities in February 2016.

Even so, the 3 percent employment quota for persons with disabilities covers only government jobs, with no equivalent guideline for the private sector, and even the public sector quota is often not honoured.

Senanayake traced the gap to corporate mindsets, putting the question directly to the audience. “If I ask any of you in this audience under which agenda would your employing of the disabled come into play, in most cases you would say it might be a CSR agenda or it might be a welfare agenda,” he remarked.

Hiring under such an agenda, according to him, becomes a numbers exercise. “Right now, being a welfare agenda, we look at how we bring in somebody and there are percentages of staff given under the CSR agenda, so there’s no major value addition which comes in,” he observed.

“The day the country or an organisation moves from a welfare agenda to an economic agenda, our focus on building these areas would be much stronger,” he stressed, adding that the shift would begin breaking down barriers within organisations and across the country.

He also called for attention to the quality of the work itself. “How do you create an employee who is disabled to have a dignified work situation?” he asked. “That is something that we need to really look at.” He pointed to accessibility, fair recruitment pathways and workplace accommodations that let employees move around freely.

Assistive technology, in his experience, has already shown what is possible. “There are organisations I’ve seen and worked in where even the blind community have different technology which enables them to provide a lot of value to the organisation,” he recalled. “So the priority has to be access, opportunity and the chance to contribute.”

However, he cautioned that attitudes remain as formidable a barrier as infrastructure. “If you look at the elephant in the room, it is the element of unconscious bias. We start stereotyping people,” he warned. In his view, true inclusion rests on whether marginalised groups feel accepted, connected and valued, and whether they get a fair chance to progress.

Jobs and talent both exist, he noted, yet the two are poorly matched, since many from under-represented groups lack the education, skills and avenues to become employable. With most employment concentrated in urban centres, he reasoned that building those skills would also strengthen rural communities. The resulting independence and earnings would, in turn, deliver the financial inclusion development banks pursue.

Senanayake called on development finance institutions (DFIs) to look beyond concessionary lending and fund the education and skill-building channels that create such talent. “It is very important that we also support those organisations to move this agenda from a social or a charity-based or welfare-based agenda to an economic agenda,” he insisted.

Meanwhile, ADFIAP Chairman and Agricultural and Rural Development Bank of Cambodia Chief Executive Officer Dr. Kao Thach identified accessibility of services as the principal hurdle for persons with disabilities. He argued that programmes must be built around the specific barriers each group faces.

“The goal is not equal treatment but equitable support,” he pointed out. Pag-IBIG Fund Strategic Marketing Group Vice President Domingo C. Jacinto noted that the Philippine provident and housing fund works with cooperatives, including those of persons with disabilities, to identify housing needs. He cited a blind couple among its borrowers who had secured a home in one of its housing developments.
