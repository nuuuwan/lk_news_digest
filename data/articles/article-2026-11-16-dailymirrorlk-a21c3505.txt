Budget 2027: What Is Beyond Colombo Port City?

Global trade and investment are important drivers of economic growth, with the ultimate aim of improving people’s living standards. Special Economic Zones (SEZs) are one of the instruments used by countries around the world to attract investment. They are designated areas within a country’s sovereign territory where businesses are offered specific incentives and operating conditions to encourage economic activity.

China’s first Special Economic Zone was established in Shenzhen on August 26, 1980, under the leadership of Deng Xiaoping.

Shenzhen has since developed into a major global manufacturing and technology centre, producing a substantial share of the world’s consumer electronics and hosting technology companies such as Tencent, Huawei and DJI.

French geographer and economic-zones expert Professor François Bost of the University of Reims has examined the development of SEZs, their contribution to economic growth and their increasing importance in attracting foreign investment.

In his paper, he argues that SEZs have become important instruments for countries seeking to accelerate industrialisation, diversify their economies, generate employment and integrate into international trade and investment networks.

According to UNCTAD statistics cited in the paper, the world had 5,383 active SEZs in 2019, spread across approximately 150 countries. Of these, 4,046, or 75%, were located in Asia, highlighting the region’s dominance in the development of such economic zones.

China stands out as the leading example, accounting for 2,543 SEZs, or 47.2% of the global total. Since 1978, China has made the development of SEZs a major component of its economic development strategy. These zones have helped attract domestic and foreign investment, facilitate manufacturing,expand exports and introduce new economic activities. Importantly, many Chinese zones also cater to the domestic market rather than being exclusively export-oriented.

The paper also highlights the changing nature of SEZs. Traditionally associated with manufacturing,warehousing and export processing, they have evolved to accommodate high-technology industries,financial services, digital data processing, science and technology enterprises and other specialised   services.

However, the international experience also demonstrates that establishing an SEZ alone is not a recipe for economic success. Some countries have experienced disappointing results in cases of weak institutional arrangements, limited integration with the domestic economy, tax evasion and smuggling.

The question, therefore, is whether Colombo Port City can provide Sri Lanka with an opportunity to position itself within the expanding global network of investment and business centres. Colombo Port City is a geographic area reclaimed from the sea adjacent to the Colombo Port and gazetted as part of the Colombo District in the Western Province. The more important question is not one of sovereignty, but how the project can move beyond property development and establish itself as an internationally competitive destination for financial services, technology-based enterprises, professional services and other high-value economic activities.

The experience of established Asian SEZs, particularly those in China, demonstrates the potential of such developments to support economic diversification and attract international capital. Colombo Port City, however, is still at a nascent stage compared with mature zones in China, Dubai, Malaysia and India. Sri Lanka is not yet widely recognised as an international investment destination and does not have the depth of business ecosystems, financial markets or investor networks available in those centres.

To do so, it needs a regulatory environment that offers credible fiscal and non-fiscal incentives capable of attracting capital, while providing the oversight and predictability investors require. Incentives are an essential part of the proposition, particularly when Sri Lanka is competing with established regionalhubs. But the ability to attract reputable businesses, protect their capital and safeguard their reputations will depend equally on effective regulation, monitoring and enforcement. The Government and the Colombo Port City Economic Commission must therefore ensure that adequate institutional capacity and resources are available.

Reports of cybercrime networks shifting westwards from established centres in Cambodia, Myanmar and other parts of Southeast Asia raise legitimate concerns for Sri Lanka. The regional scam-compound industry involves online fraud, romance scams, fake cryptocurrency investment schemes and illegal  betting operations, with criminal networks moving when enforcement pressure increases in one jurisdiction. The United Nations has estimated that hundreds of thousands of people have been trafficked into such operations across the region.

Sri Lanka has also recorded arrests linked to cybercrime operations. Official figures cited in reports indicate that about 430 foreign nationals were arrested over cybercrime-related offences in 2024, while more than 1,000 were reportedly arrested during the first five months of 2026. The arrest of 130 Chinese   nationals in Kundasale in June 2025, 85 of whom were subsequently deported, was another high-profile incident. These cases point to a problem that warrants sustained attention, but they should not be used to attribute criminal conduct to any particular nationality or to assume that Port City itself is a centre for such activity.

The regulatory arrangements in Port City are important in this context. The Colombo Port City Economic Commission (CPCEC) has said that it has introduced layered vetting of foreign visa applicants and screening of companies, shareholders and directors. According to details reported earlier, these checks include sanctions, politically exposed persons, adverse media and law-enforcement information, beneficial ownership, overseas business activities, operating history, the source of investment funds and financial capacity.

The Commission has also said that sponsoring companies must screen foreign recruits and submit recent police-clearance certificates authenticated by a diplomatic mission. Employment contracts, passport details and job designations are checked against approved manpower plans, while new measures include cyber-abuse and cybercrime audits of relevant authorised persons and monthly risk reporting.

Companies employing foreign nationals are also required to submit monthly staff information and provide the nearest police station with employee lists and residential addresses.

Importantly, the CPCEC does not have the final authority to grant visas or permit entry into Sri Lanka. It makes recommendations, but the Department of Immigration and Emigration retains responsibility for immigration and security checks, including screening against Interpol records, international police notices and information from local and foreign law-enforcement agencies. Even a Commission-recommended endorsement does not guarantee admission; immigration officers may still refuse entry. The Commission has also said it is working towards memoranda of understanding with domestic agencies and regulators to formalise information-sharing and coordination.

These measures indicate that steps are being taken to address the risks. At the same time, the existence of screening procedures should not lead to complacency. The reported regional movement of cybercrime networks, together with cases detected in Sri Lanka, underlines the need for continued vigilance.

Sri Lanka cannot expect to compete for international investment on the same terms as mature global financial and commercial centres. Established hubs have stronger business ecosystems, deeper financial markets and well-developed investor networks. As Sri Lanka continues to establish its position as an investment destination, tax incentives are therefore a necessary policy tool to bridge the competitiveness gap, reduce initial barriers and encourage international businesses to consider locating here.

Such incentives need to be supported by regulatory certainty, efficient infrastructure, reliable public services, skilled human resources and a business-friendly environment. The objective should be to attract reputable companies whose activities generate employment, foreign exchange, technology transfer and opportunities for domestic businesses.

What differentiates a promising SEZ from an underperforming one is not simply the scale of the incentives offered, but the quality of its oversight. The Government and the CPCEC must have the capacity to monitor beneficiary companies, ensure that commitments are honoured and assess whether   incentives are producing the intended economic returns. Monitoring should be sufficiently robust to protect the integrity of the zone and the reputation of the businesses operating within it.

What is now noteworthy is that the economic logic behind the Colombo Port City has been well established by the government. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake, in his November 12 budget speech, laid emphasis on attracting foreign investment into Colombo Port City. That is with the prime intention of placing Sri Lanka as a regional hub for digital services, financial activities and technology-based businesses. This gives credence to the economic relevance of the project. It is not meant to lie idle.  A key highlight is the proposed amendment to the Colombo Port City Economic Commission Act. The draft Colombo Port City Economic Commission (Amendment) Bill has been referred to the Attorney General, with the stated objective of attracting more foreign investment and providing greater facilities to investors and people employed within the Port City.  Besides, the government has proposed establishing a National Single Window for Investment, with an allocation of Rs.100 million, alongside a Treasury-owned investment promotion company. It also plans to introduce an Investment Protection Bill and a Public-Private Partnership Act, while appointing a   committee to address delays in releasing land for investment projects.

Colombo Port City has the potential to become a meaningful part of Sri Lanka’s economic development strategy, but a project of this scale requires proactive and forward-looking action by all stakeholders,particularly the public sector. SEZs do not develop in isolation: they require consistent government support, policy stability, effective institutions and coordination among the agencies responsible for investment, immigration, security and regulation. The budget 2027 bears testimony to it.
