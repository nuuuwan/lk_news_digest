US$36bn export dream needs labour reform, not a 1940s engine: TPA’s Barath Arullsamy

Colombo, Aug. 25 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka’s ambition to earn US$36 billion from exports by 2030 risks becoming “a glossy brochure resting on a 1940s labour engine” unless the Government urgently introduces a modern Employment Act, Vice President (International Affairs) Democratic Peoples Front - Tamil Progressive Alliance  Barath Arullsamy said.

“The Government wants factories to compete in the economy of 2030 while employers and workers remain governed by laws written in 1941, 1942, 1950 and 1954. A national export strategy without a national employment strategy is merely a press release,” he said.

Nearly two years after assuming office, the Government has still not published a consolidated labour reform draft or fixed legislative timetable. Although Labour Minister Anil Jayantha Fernando has announced a unified labour code before the end of 2026, Arullsamy said “soon” could not substitute for a transparent deadline.

He noted that the previous administration had already presented a proposed New Employment Act to the NLAC and referred the reform process towards legal drafting. While unions legitimately criticised its content and consultation process, the present Government had inherited substantial institutional work and ILO-related expertise.

“It was entitled to correct the previous process, but not to bury the reform. A change of government cannot become a two-year pause button,” he said.

Arullsamy’s policy study mapped approximately 44 labour-related enactments, with around 17 forming the principal operative framework. These include the Wages Boards Ordinance of 1941, Factories Ordinance of 1942, Industrial Disputes Act of 1950, Shop and Office Employees Act of 1954, EPF Act of 1958, Termination of Employment Act of 1971 and ETF Act of 1980.

“The fragmented system confuses employers, delays justice for workers and weakens enforcement. Modernisation should provide businesses with certainty while guaranteeing workers enforceable rights,” he said.

Sri Lanka’s National Export Development Plan seeks to increase export earnings from US$16.1 billion in 2024 to US$36 billion by 2030. Arullsamy said this could not be achieved by concentrating almost exclusively on apparel.

Rubber products, processed food, fisheries, spices, electronics, boatbuilding, pharmaceuticals, gems, logistics and knowledge services also required manufacturing support, skilled workers and stable employment relations.

He also warned against treating RCEP membership as an automatic economic solution.

“RCEP is a doorway, not a factory. If Sri Lanka has no competitive products to carry through it, imports will enter while our manufacturers remain outside. Labour reform is not an RCEP condition, but it is essential for surviving the competition that RCEP will bring,” he said.

Latest official figures place labour-force participation at only 49.2% in the first quarter of 2026. Arullsamy said low unemployment could not be celebrated when more than half the working-age population remained outside the active labour force.

He acknowledged the increase of the national minimum wage to Rs.30,000 per month and Sri Lanka’s ratification of ILO Convention No. 190 on workplace violence and harassment. However, he said wage amendments and international ratifications were not substitutes for a comprehensive employment framework.

The urgency was also connected to GSP+. Sri Lanka received an estimated €139 million in EU tariff exemptions in 2024. Under the revised framework, beneficiary countries will be assessed against 32 international conventions, including ILO standards on labour inspection and tripartite consultation.

“GSP+ is not a charity. It is a compliance dividend. Ratification certificates will not protect market access if enforcement remains weak,” he said.

Arullsamy said conditions in private estates were among the concerns discussed during a recent meeting between a TPA delegation led by Mano Ganesan MP and UN Resident Coordinator Marc-André Franche.

An Amnesty International investigation covering 45 private estates and smallholdings in Galle and Matara documented allegations of intimidation, violence, withheld wages, debt bondage and movement restrictions, with several practices reportedly meeting ILO indicators of forced labour.

“These allegations require independent investigations. Sri Lanka cannot strengthen Customs screening for foreign goods allegedly produced through forced labour while looking away from credible allegations on its own estates,” he said.

Published estimates place estate employment at around 100,000, down from approximately 300,000 in the 1990s. Arullsamy estimated that the core active registered field workforce could now be closer to 72,000, depending on whether casual and off-payroll workers were included.

He warned that arbitrary outgrower and revenue-sharing models could become “disguised informalisation” if workers lost guaranteed earnings, EPF, ETF, leave, maternity protection, collective bargaining and termination safeguards.

“Productivity reform is necessary, but efficiency cannot mean transferring every commercial risk from the company to the worker,” he said.

Arullsamy called for a unified Employment Act covering permanent, casual, contract, part-time, platform and remote workers; digital wage records; portable social protection; modern occupational-safety rules; time-bound dispute resolution; stronger plantation inspections and accessible Tamil and Sinhala complaint mechanisms.

He added that digital labour and export systems also required proper cybersecurity and data protection, noting that the Online Safety Act was not a substitute for a comprehensive Cyber Security Act.

“This is not merely a Tamil, plantation or trade-union issue. The person plucking tea, stitching garments, processing rubber, building boats or coding export services is earning Sri Lanka’s foreign exchange. Economic patriotism begins by protecting the hand that earns the dollar,” Arullsamy said.
