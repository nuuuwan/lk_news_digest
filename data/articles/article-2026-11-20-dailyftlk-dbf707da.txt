A financially unsavvy CEO is like Columbus sailing without a compass

It is a quiet, disheartening tragedy to watch Chief Executive Officers (CEOs), even at large, powerful organisations, freeze when faced with basic finance and accounting questions. They often retreat behind the reflexive, rehearsed refrain: “I’ll have to check with my Chief Financial Officer (CFO and get back to you.” Whilst it is disturbing to witness such slavish reliance on the CFO to respond to basic queries, it reveals a glaring void in foundational financial literacy where it ought to be. Seeing someone hold absolute strategic authority while lacking a basic grasp of his/her own balance sheet, cash flows, profitability indicators, et cetera, exposes an uncomfortable reality. It is essential that a CEO is in intimate touch with the core of the business. Running a multi-billion-rupee enterprise on pure vibes and financial blindness is not just dismal; it is the gateway to failure.

In the modern corporate arena, the CEO is revered as the captain steering strategy, inspiring talent, and delivering long-term value. Yet across many Sri Lankan corporates, from listed conglomerates to family enterprises, an uncomfortable paradox persists. CEOs, while proficient in marketing, supply chain, and new business development, and sometimes highly effective as leaders, operate with striking vulnerability in finance due to an ultra-dependent relationship with their CFOs. This is not a delegation. It stems from a self-inflicted fundamental knowledge deficit. Many CEOs struggle with the basic mechanics of working capital cycles and cash flow, viewing financial statements as cryptic compliance documents rather than as navigational dashboards. When confronted with concepts like Discounted Cash Flow (DCF), Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC), or Return on Invested Capital (ROIC), the response is often quite bewildering. Financial statements are treated as compliance documents to be signed off, rather than first-instance dashboards for strategic decision-making.

This disproportionate reliance on the CFO creates a dangerous organisational dynamic. When a CEO lacks foundational financial literacy, the CFO ceases to be a strategic partner and inadvertently becomes the sole arbiter of corporate viability. Strategic discussions about capital allocation, merger and acquisition valuations, debt structuring, or pricing models risk becoming one-sided. The CEO is left unable to challenge assumptions, stress-test financial projections, or ask the critical, uncomfortable questions that safeguard a business. Decision-making becomes reactive, driven by a blind trust in spreadsheet outputs rather than a deep, intuitive understanding of financial reality.

Consider a financially unsavvy CEO who uncritically accepts CFO’s optimistic projections based on cheap foreign debt financing and projected export growth, and takes reckless, unmitigated risks. Failing to stress-test currency volatility, liquidity crunches, and rising foreign interest rates leaves the company vulnerable to severe financial distress. Had the CEO possessed basic financial literacy regarding capital structure, foreign exchange exposure, and how an appreciating local currency erodes export competitiveness, he/she could have challenged the CFO’s assumptions before committing to disproportionately heavy foreign debt. Rigorous scrutiny would have driven safer strategic alternatives, such as currency hedging or a phased, risk-controlled expansion.

The danger of this prevailing dilemma cannot be overemphasised, particularly in Sri Lanka’s volatile economic environment. In a landscape marked by shifting interest rates, foreign exchange fluctuations, and tight liquidity constraints, financial illiteracy at the helm is an existential risk. Capital allocation errors, such as over-leveraging during economic upturns or misjudging cost structures during downturns, often stem directly from CEOs who cannot read the financial pulse of their own companies. Furthermore, this dynamic exposes organisations to severe governance risks. Without an independent financial lens at the executive level, oversight diminishes, and operational blind spots multiply.

Admittedly, in corporate leadership, the CEO, irrespective of his core expertise, is expected to be an integrator. He is not expected to understand the granular details of finance. Any attempt to do so will be a poor use of CEO bandwidth because it actively hampers strategic focus. There is, however, a fundamental difference between operational level ‘meddling’ and directorial/managerial questioning. Delegating the technical execution of finance and accounting to the CFO is logical and prudent but delegating their comprehension and application solely to the CFO is reckless.

At its core, corporate finance and accounting provide the language for articulating a company’s operational health, market position, and future trajectory. A CEO who lacks fluency in this language risks making decisions based on blind trust rather than data. Conversely, a CEO who understands balance sheets, capital structures, and cost drivers can lead with precision, allocating resources to build long-term value while safeguarding the organisation against systemic risk. A CEO must be able to read the story behind the figures, detect subtle systemic risks before they surface as crises, and ensure that every financial decision directly aligns with long-term strategy. Without this directorial grasp, a CEO is effectively operating blind, completely dependent on the CFO and the finance function without the capacity for meaningful oversight, constructive dissent, or strategic direction. A company cannot be sustainably led by a chief executive who is blind to the intricacies of finance.

Working alongside finance-savvy leaders, such as the late Anderson Mazoka, former CEO of Boart Longyear and Managing Director at Anglo American Corporation Central Africa (Anglo), Susantha Ratnayake, former Chairman/CEO of John Keells Holdings (JKH), and Ajit Gunewardena, former Executive Director of JKH and current Chairman of PickMe, Fintrex, and Teejay, and various CEOs/CFOs in the JKH Group, impressed on me the critical value of executive financial literacy. Their sharp, searching questions kept me on my toes. Having also served as CEO of Anglo following Anderson Mazoka’s resignation to contest Zambia’s presidency, and as Group Finance Director at both Anglo and JKH, I have experienced firsthand the extent of financial literacy a CEO must possess to lead effectively.

A CEO must read the primary financial statements as an interconnected account of operational decisions, not as separate accounting exercises. Together, the balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement reveal a company’s assets, liabilities, shareholders’ equity, gearing, profitability, and cash generation. Understanding them helps a CEO assess short-term liquidity and long-term leverage. Overlooking these fundamentals can be fatal. The collapse of Toys “R” Us stands as one of corporate history’s most striking examples of how aggressive financial engineering can extinguish a retail giant. At its peak, the brand commanded immense cultural mindshare and strong seasonal sales, yet structural debt eliminated the strategic flexibility required to survive changing consumer realities.

While the Balance Sheet is a snapshot of net wealth, the income statement records revenues, operational costs, and net margins over a specified reporting window. A CEO must look past top-line growth to evaluate fixed versus variable overhead and identify margin contraction early. Spotify built its audio platform on this level of scrutiny, balancing heavy content licensing fees and royalty costs against subscriber acquisition and retention to achieve sustainable profitability.

The cash flow statement tracks the actual movement of capital across operations, investing, and financing activities. Revenue and reported net income mean little if an enterprise cannot convert sales into cash. The dramatic crash of Carvana in 2022 serves as a case study in how growth-at-all-costs models, excessive leverage, and paper inventory assets can collapse when underlying macroeconomic tailwinds suddenly reverse.

Extracting strategic intelligence from financial statements requires analysing key performance metrics across four primary areas: profitability, liquidity, solvency, and operational efficiency.

Profitability ratios such as gross, operating, and net margins, alongside Return on Equity (ROE) and Return on Assets (ROA), measure how efficiently revenues and capital translate into bottom-line profit. Howard Schultz, during his tenure as CEO of Starbucks, consistently focused on maintaining healthy profit margins while expanding the company’s global footprint. This involved careful management of raw material costs, operational efficiencies, and strategic pricing decisions.

Liquidity ratios, including the current and quick ratios, assess an organisation’s capacity to cover short-term obligations using liquid assets. During the early supply chain shocks of 2020, retailers with deep cash reserves navigated severe disruptions, while under-capitalised chains faced sudden operational freezes. Solvency ratios, such as debt-to-equity and debt-to-asset ratios, evaluate long-term stability and reliance on borrowed capital. Efficiency ratios, such as inventory turnover and asset turnover, reveal how effectively company assets and working capital are deployed. Zara founder Amancio Ortega transformed fast fashion by driving exceptionally high inventory turnover, freeing up capital, and reducing markdown risks through tight, real-time supply chain feedback loops.

A primary responsibility of executive leadership is determining where capital should be deployed. Evaluating strategic growth initiatives, whether internal research and development, physical expansion, or corporate acquisitions, requires a firm grasp of discounted cash flow mechanics.

Net Present Value (NPV) discounts projected cash inflows back to present-day dollars using a risk-adjusted discount rate. A positive NPV demonstrates that an initiative is expected to yield value beyond its risk premium-included capital cost. The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) identifies the break-even discount rate where NPV equals zero. Projects with an IRR exceeding the firm’s hurdle rate usually warrant investment consideration. Meanwhile, the Payback Period measures the time needed to recoup the initial cash outlay. While it ignores the time value of money, it provides a quick assessment of capital recovery risks. During Nvidia’s early development of the CUDA architecture, now beleaguered, Jensen Huang routinely prioritised long-term R&D spending, and parallel computing infrastructure over short-term earnings. Supported by disciplined capital allocation models, these investments built the foundational moat for the company’s ultimate dominance in artificial intelligence computing.

Sustaining profitability requires an understanding of how fixed overhead and variable costs interact under changing market conditions. Fixed costs represent overhead expenses that remain constant regardless of output volume, such as facilities leases and administrative salaries. Variable costs fluctuate directly with production levels, including raw materials, direct labor, and shipping. Identifying the break-even point establishes the precise sales volume needed to cover these combined costs, setting a baseline target for commercial strategy. In mature, capital-intensive sectors, cost control dictates survival. Ceylon Cold Stores (Elephant House) consistently applied this structural discipline in determining pricing of carbonated soft drinks in the face of powerful international and local competition.

Executive decision-making requires looking beyond historic performance to map out future financial trajectories. Financial leadership relies on three foundational processes: budgeting, forecasting, and scenario planning. Budgeting establishes granular resource constraints and financial targets across business units. Forecasting projects future performance using historical operational metrics, market trends, and economic indicators. Scenario modeling builds organisational resilience by mapping out best-case, base-case, and worst-case conditions against potential economic shocks. When Adobe CEO Shantanu Narayen shifted the company’s financial model from perpetual desktop software licenses to a recurring Creative Cloud subscription model, the strategic pivot required long-term financial modeling to manage temporary top-line revenue dips while funding cloud infrastructure, accelerating lifetime customer value.

CEOs are responsible for protecting corporate assets and maintaining the integrity of public reporting. This requires monitoring operational, market, and credit risks while establishing strict internal controls to prevent accounting errors or fraudulent activity. The collapse of Wirecard remains a cautionary example of failed governance. Under Markus Braun, aggressive off-balance-sheet misdirection and a total breakdown of internal controls obscured 1.9 billion euros in non-existent cash reserves, leading to complete insolvency and regulatory ruin.

Corporate expansion often involves acquiring other market players. Successfully acquiring and integrating target firms requires fluency in valuation, due diligence, and post-merger integration. Valuation involves applying income, market, and asset-based approaches to establish a fair intrinsic price for the target company. Due diligence demands comprehensive audits of the target’s financial ledgers, legal exposures, and operational dependencies. Integration requires merging systems, corporate structures, and workforce cultures post-transaction to capture projected synergies. JKH’s acquisition of Asian Hotels, comprising Cinnamon Grand, Cinnamon Lakeside, Crescat Apartments, and unused freehold land, was a textbook example of acquisition and integration.

When an executive team operates without financial fluency, systemic vulnerabilities multiply rapidly. Strategic capital flows toward low-return initiatives, starving core growth drivers. Ill-considered debt structures expose the enterprise to default during downturns, while inaccurate forecasting damages market credibility and poor oversight invites operational blind spots, fraud, and legal liabilities.

CEOs do not need to handle daily bookkeeping, but they must be capable of auditing the logic behind every financial proposal. Corporate finance fluency transforms quantitative data into a strategic framework, enabling CEOs to ask probing questions of their CFOs, make data-backed operational decisions, and articulate a coherent strategy to shareholders. Ultimately, financial literacy provides the clarity required to navigate economic uncertainty and build enduring enterprise value.

A CEO without financial literacy is akin to Columbus sailing without a compass. He/she may command a magnificent vessel and inspire a brilliant crew yet would be steering blind. In modern business, vision alone cannot navigate the hidden reefs of cash flow crises and volatile markets. Leaving the numbers entirely to the finance team is not delegation; it is surrendering the wheel. True leadership demands reading the financial instruments, interpreting balance sheet currents, and turning hard data into bold strategy.

When a CEO masters the language of finance, the entire dynamic shifts. Uncertainty turns into opportunity. Risk transforms into calculated reward. The fog clears, revealing a sharp, undeniable path to lasting growth. So, step up to the helm. Master your numbers, command your course, and steer your organisation not just toward survival, but toward absolute market dominance. The sky is clear, the ocean is open, and the ultimate destination is yours to claim.
