Govt. to bring private investors into ECT management

President Anura Kumara Dissanayake in conversation with Ceylon Chamber of Commerce Vice Chairperson Bingumal Thewarathanthri during the SL Economic Summit’s fireside chat - Pic by Pradeep Dilrukshana

The government will bring private investors into the management of the East Container Terminal (ECT), President Anura Kumara Dissanayake said, revealing a Cabinet decision to open shares in a proposed management company while retaining state ownership of the terminal.

Several major international shipping companies are already in discussions regarding the terminal’s management, the President told the inauguration session of the Sri Lanka Economic Summit 2026 on Monday.

The government is looking to make greater use of the terminal, which Dissanayake said currently handles approximately 500,000 containers against a capacity of 3.2 million.

“I do not believe that the government can continue to manage the East Container Terminal entirely on its own. The Cabinet has therefore decided to retain ownership of the terminal while establishing a company to manage it and transferring shares in that company to private investors,” he said.

The President was responding to a question from Ceylon Chamber of Commerce Vice Chairperson Bingumal Thewarathanthri during the summit’s fireside chat on why Sri Lanka’s ports were not making a larger contribution to the economy.

“Ports are one of the most important components of our economic strategy,” Dissanayake said, identifying the gap between ECT’s container volumes and capacity as an issue requiring attention.

“This means that we are not making full use of this asset on a daily basis.”

The President did not specify the shareholding to be offered, name the shipping companies involved or provide a timetable for the proposed arrangement.

The ECT initiative forms part of a wider government plan to expand port capacity and develop logistics and other maritime services.

Dissanayake said the next project would be the second phase of the West Container Terminal, for which a breakwater must be constructed. Its estimated cost has risen to US$1.4 billion from US$1.1 billion.

“If we undertake this expenditure and construct the breakwater, the West Container Terminal will also be able to handle 3.2 million containers,” he said.

The forthcoming Budget will allocate funds for preliminary studies. The Asian Development Bank is preparing the feasibility study, with an interim report expected in December and the complete report next April.

Dissanayake also questioned whether Sri Lanka should limit its port business to container transhipment, calling for the development of a logistics hub.

“We must also consider whether ports should be limited to the transhipment of containers. Is there more we can do beyond that?” he asked.

He said tender-related disputes surrounding a logistics project, which had reached court, had been resolved through an agreement to pay compensation to the companies concerned.

The Government is also taking steps to establish a container yard at Kerawalapitiya to reduce congestion within the port.

On bunkering, Dissanayake said the Government was examining a tax-related issue and hoped to provide relief in the forthcoming Budget.

“Since this is an international trade activity, taxation must be imposed in accordance with international standards,” he said.
