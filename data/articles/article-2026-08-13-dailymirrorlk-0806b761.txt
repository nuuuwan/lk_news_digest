Re-evaluating Abortion Laws in Sri Lanka:  A Call for Change Towards Women’s Health and Rights

Despite punitive sanctions, daily abortion rates are reported to be as high as 658 abortions a day

Apart from objections based on religious grounds, patriarchal attitudes and conventional social and cultural norms harmful to women dominated the debate within parliament and drowned out the opposition outside parliament.

Taking the legal position first, Sri Lanka maintains one of the most restrictive abortion laws in South Asia. Abortion is permissible only in the gravest of circumstances to save the life of the mother. It is punishable with imprisonment ranging from three to twenty years with or without a fine. Legal restrictions are further reinforced by a medical protocol that requires the concurrence of three doctors before the pregnancy can be medically terminated.

This has become a thorny problem. Restrictive criminal laws on abortion have driven women underground and they  resort to dangerous means to terminate an unwanted pregnancy. A vast and open network of clandestine back-alley abortion clinics thrives unregulated, posing a risk to women who are unable to afford private medical facilities locally or overseas.

Despite punitive sanctions, daily abortion rates are reported to be as high as 658 abortions a day, with actual figures estimated to be higher. Restrictive laws also undermine public health. Efforts made to improve family planning and reproductive health are hampered by penal sanctions that drive the issue further underground. Although Sri Lanka boasts one of the lowest maternal mortality rates (MMR) in the region, reports indicate that 10-13 per cent of maternal deaths are attributed to unsafe abortions, making it the third largest cause of maternal deaths in the country. To address this issue, the National Guidelines on Post-Abortion Care 2015 were issued by the Ministry of Health (MOH) to provide medical care to women for complications arising out of illegal abortions without fear of any prosecution. It further prevents conscientious objections from healthcare providers or the refusal to treat based on personal, moral or religious grounds. However, social stigma, discrimination and exploitation prevent women from receiving early treatment and, in some instances, cause preventable maternal deaths.

Efforts to reform the abortion law have been undertaken since the 1970s, yet all have met the same futile end.  Following a resurgence in women’s rights with the ratification of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) in 1981 and the Women’s Charter in 1993, several amendments to the criminal law along these liberal lines were introduced. Therefore, in 1995, a draft amendment to the penal code to decriminalise abortion in specific circumstances such as rape, incest, and congenital abnormalities incompatible with life, along with additional conditions for the authorisation of the medical procedure, was presented to parliament only to be inauspiciously withdrawn due to heated opposition, particularly from religious minority groups.

Apart from objections based on religious grounds, patriarchal attitudes and conventional social and cultural norms harmful to women dominated the debate within parliament and drowned out the opposition outside parliament. The fear that decriminalisation would open the ‘floodgates’, encourage promiscuity and indecency, especially among the young, and lead to the breakdown of traditional power structures within the family and generally impose severe consequences on ‘our culture’ prevailed.

The next attempt to rekindle the debate was made in 2011 and again in 2017 by recommending decriminalising abortion on similar grounds as a goal under the National Action Plan for the Protection and Promotion of Human Rights. The 2013 proposals by the Law Commission of Sri Lanka recommending the medical termination of a pregnancy in instances of rape and serious foetal impairment, inclusive of detailed, rigid procedures for medical approval, were met with the same fate. Recommendations made by the Justice Aluvihare Special Committee to allow abortions in cases of rape and incest, pregnancy in a girl below 16 years of age and with serious foetal impairments did not materialise as well. The former Minister of Justice, Ali Sabry, announced the need for a national consultation on the issue in 2022, drawing on the concerns of the incidence of suicide due to unwanted pregnancies. This resurgence was immediately overshadowed by the economic crisis that engulfed the country. The National People’s Power (NPP), elected in 2025, pledged in its election manifesto to expand legal grounds for abortion based on the 2013 Law Commission report. The recent proposal presented in March 2025, driven by three medical colleges, is currently limited to allowing women to terminate pregnancies with unviable foetal abnormalities, and falls short of the recommendations made by the Law Commission. However, civil society activists have cautiously welcomed the move as a first step towards a just and equitable legal framework that recognises women’s bodily autonomy and her right to equality, dignity, and choice.

One of the significant challenges to legal reform is a lack of consensus and a coordinated approach on the issue. The abortion debate in Sri Lanka is shaped by religious opposition, deep-rooted patriarchy that regulates female morality and sexuality and political inertia. The recent study on public attitudes and perceptions, particularly among young people, regarding abortion, conducted by the Family Planning Association of Sri Lanka in 2023, reinforces some of these arguments, also highlighting self-imposed restrictions by women based on religious and moral convictions. Opinions among physicians and healthcare providers also differ.

Therefore, since its inception, the abortion debate has been centred on two grounds: decriminalisation and legalisation of abortion, recognising women’s right to bodily autonomy and improving public and maternal health by managing maternal deaths arising out of abortions or expanding family planning and reproductive health programmes to prevent unwanted pregnancies. The outcome is a piecemeal approach to legal and health reforms that undermines both objectives.

Numerous legal proposals to decriminalise abortion have been based on limited grounds, specifically in cases of rape, incest, or unviable foetal abnormalities. The argument is that a medical termination of pregnancy is necessitated due to the heinous crime committed against the woman or girl child, or for medical reasons, which was assumed to appease most conscientious objectors. Although the move to decriminalise and expand legal grounds for legalising abortion was significant, the exceptions or limited circumstances identified have always been inadequate, as most women seek abortion for reasons outside these exceptions, such as economic considerations, issues concerning spacing of children, lack of access to contraception, contraceptive failure, or their choice to discontinue the pregnancy. Legal reforms based on a broader interpretation of promoting women’s human rights to equality, autonomy, and lived experiences have eluded policymakers. This has hampered the progress of providing post-abortion care and modern contraception use.

On one hand, sidelining abortion as a human rights issue and, on the other, viewing it through a clinical lens, pose challenges to legal reform. The most recent proposal by three medical colleges in 2025 exemplifies this. The proposal represents a setback in the legal reform trajectory of abortion, as it recognises unviable foetal abnormality as the only ground for a medical termination of pregnancy. This discussion thus far has excluded broader consultation with key stakeholders, including civil society organisations and the Ministry of Women and Child Affairs. The lack of consultation and the exclusion of women from the decision-making process, which undermine good governance and accountability, pose another challenge to achieving a breakthrough for legal reform in the country.

While successive governments have promoted the concept of reform, there is now a clear opportunity to translate these ideas into actionable initiatives that can lead to meaningful change.

Firstly, it is important to implement international obligations voluntarily conceded to under various human rights conventions. Sri Lanka has ratified multiple international human rights conventions, including CEDAW, which imposes a legal obligation to promote sexual and reproductive health, encompassing standards for safe abortion, access to post-abortion care, and associated services. Furthermore, successive concluding observations from the CEDAW Committee, including those made on the latest periodic report in February 2025, strongly recommend prioritising the decriminalisation and legalisation of abortion in all circumstances, and, in the interim, advocating for the provision of legal and safe abortion for victims of rape.

Secondly, it is critical to renew national consultations to legalise abortion with key stakeholders and draft legal amendments based on recent empirical evidence. Although abortion is illegal in Sri Lanka, both the law and enforcement practices send mixed signals. For instance, while abortion is prohibited and carries severe penalties, authorities often turn a blind eye to operational clinics that provide such services. Furthermore, women are immune from prosecution and are encouraged to seek treatment for complications arising from abortions, recognising the adverse effects on maternal mortality as well as physical and psychological health.

Additionally, medical officers are not legally required to report patients referred to them for complications arising from illegal abortions; in fact, they are legally obliged to treat such patients. However, given that abortion is a criminal offence and carries moral censure, women are reluctant to seek post-abortion care, and health service providers are unwilling to offer it. This ongoing dilemma presents an opportunity to assess and review the amendments proposed to the law since 1995. It is imperative to take into consideration recent empirical evidence on why women seek an abortion to address misconceptions, to resume fresh consultations on the subject, not just to ensure formal equality for women, but to achieve substantive equality through resource allocation, sensitisation and empowerment.

Furthermore, any significant discussion on proposing amendments to the criminal law to legalise abortion must involve all key stakeholders, including representatives of women and civil society organisations that advocate for safe abortion. Excluding women, whose rights are central to the debate, undermines their fundamental right to equality and health and their agency to effect change for themselves and foster social transformation for others.

Finally, it is necessary to increase national efforts to promote awareness of sexual and reproductive health, rights, and post-abortion care services. Parallel to the movement to decriminalise abortion, it is critical to expand sexual and reproductive health and rights, including  school children and unmarried women, in line with Sri Lanka’s commitment to achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 3 (ensure good health and well-being) and SDG 5 (achieve gender equality for all women and girls). Moreover, it is essential to utilise the established and efficient network of health administration across the island to provide accurate information on post-abortion care and the health services available to women, aiming to prevent maternal deaths and other health complications. These initiatives should be coupled with targeted awareness, sensitisation, and training for service providers to ensure safe abortion services.

Shermila Antony is a lecturer at the Faculty of Law, University of Colombo.
