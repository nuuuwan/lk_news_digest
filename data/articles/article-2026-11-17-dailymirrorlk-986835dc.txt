Are we going for the 18th IMF programme?

During his 2027 budget speech,  President Anura Kumara Dissanayake stated that a committee has been appointed to help take a decision on another International Monetary Fund (IMF) assisted programme once the current 17th programme  comes to end in March 2027.

The President also said that the country is now moving from stability to economic transformation. One has to accept that the current Extended Fund Facility (EFF) programme of the IMF not only assisted  the country to recover from the bankruptcy it had fallen into some four years ago.

The choice of the National People’s Power (NPP) by the Sri Lankan people at the 2024 Presidential election and the Parliamentary election, in place of the other traditional main political parties, was a dangerous decision, in the light of the NPP’s policy towards the IMF. As a traditional leftist party, the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP)—the core party in the NPP coalition—had completely rejected the IMF and its programmes until just a few weeks before the elections. Yet their stance had shifted when they smelt victory.

When they sensed the prospect of the first major electoral victory in their 60-year long history, pragmatism prevailed among the leadership of the JVP and the NPP. Two days prior to the Presidential election, NPP candidate Anura Kumara Dissanayake announced that they would continue with the IMF programme with modifications decided upon with the IMF officials. Imagine what the situation would be like if the NPP had decided otherwise, given the current fuel price hikes driven by external pressures like the West Asian conflict.

The situation that prevailed when Sri Lanka approached the IMF for assistance in 2022 was a far cry from the current situation in the country. People had to wait in miles long queues sometimes for weeks for fuel and cooking gas due to an unprecedented foreign exchange crisis Sri Lanka faced then. Ministers went begging in oil-rich countries for fuel on credit. Former Foreign Minister Ali Sabry later told those countries declined the requests made by Sri Lanka  also due to government’s imprudent decision to forcibly cremate the cadavers of Muslims who died of COVID 19. The foreign reserved sank to a mere $50 million.

It was against this backdrop that President Gotabaya Rajapaksa approached the IMF for assistance in March 2022, following a long period of hesitation driven by the advice of certain Central Bank and Treasury officials. The IMF’s formula was entirely its own, created without any local inputs. Debt restructuring and structural reforms constituted the main pillars of the IMF’s current EFF arrangement, in line with the recommendations already put forward in the 2021 country report or 2021 Article IV Consultation of the IMF.  A $2.9 billion financial aid package covering four years was agreed upon later, during staff-level discussions between the IMF and the Sri Lankan government.

In a press release by the Executive Board of the IMF on February 25, 2022, the urgency of implementing a credible and coherent strategy to restore macroeconomic stability and debt sustainability was stressed. It was also recommended that ‘reforms should focus on strengthening VAT and income taxes, through rate increases and base broadening measures. Fiscal adjustment should be accompanied by energy pricing reforms to reduce fiscal risks from lossmaking public enterprises.’ These recommendations were later agreed upon at the staff-level agreement and were implemented following the approval of the EFF by the Executive Board on March 20, 2023.

Simultaneously, the government adopted an anti-corruption law and a new Banking Act to help broaden the Central Bank’s regulatory powers which had also been recommended in the Staff report for the 2021 Article IV Consultation. Therefore, the credit for the economic recovery and the stability after the economic crisis mostly goes to the IMF.

However,  the country’s needs have changed now. The President stated that the economy is now moving from stability to transformation. And in the meantime, Cyclone Ditwah and the West Asian war have underscored the urgency of having a backup strategy in the event of such eventualities, especially external shocks. These challenges would guide the deliberations of the committee that is examining the need for another IMF programme.
