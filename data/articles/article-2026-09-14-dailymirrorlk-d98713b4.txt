Tourism authority mounts cleanliness drive as Pekoe Trail passes into state ownership

Trail pass income to fund waste collection by  plantation communities under new non-profit mgt. model

SLTDA engages Finance Ministry to share tourism levy with local councils, taps US$ 100mn WB project  for recycling and Clean Sri Lanka project to fund tourist-friendly eco washrooms

Moves follow latest tourist survey identifying cleanliness as the destination’s weakest link, despite high overall satisfaction

The Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority (SLTDA) will take ownership of the Pekoe Trail this month under a new non-profit model that funnels trail pass income to local government authorities to fund waste collection in tea plantation communities.

The trail handover serves as the focal point for a sweeping national cleanliness programme assembled by the authority, which also spans visitor washrooms at key sites, a proposed revenue-sharing model for the tourism development levy, and a US$ 100 million World Bank-funded recycling initiative in the North and East.

The measures advance a wider repositioning of the destination toward experience-based and sustainable tourism, aimed at repeat visitors and higher-yielding segments. Crucially, they address the destination’s clearest vulnerability, identified in the authority’s latest survey of departing foreign tourists. General cleanliness ranked weakest among all ten service attributes tested. Nearly one in ten tourists rated cleanliness poorly, a stark contrast to the near-perfect scores awarded for general welcome and hospitality.

SLTDA Chairman Suranjith Wevita told Mirror Business the Pekoe Trail’s route dictated its unique funding design, acting as a much-needed economic intervention for local councils that lack a formal tax base to manage waste in marginalized estate sectors.

“We are trying to design a model for trail management, like the Pekoe Trail, because it actually lies through the tea country,” he said. “Those are line room communities, mainly plantation workers, so they don’t generate much income. The Pradeshiya Sabhas in those areas don’t collect any assessment tax or anything, so they don’t collect garbage.” Under the new arrangement, a portion of the income generated from trail passes will be transferred to those local authorities to fund garbage collection from plantation worker housing schemes.

Governance of the trail will rest with the SLTDA and other state entities, holding the larger share of directorships alongside private sector nominees on a structure drawn from long-distance trail management practices abroad. The Chairman confirmed the management entity is a non-profit organisation with no shareholders, meaning no dividends will be declared and all generated income will be used to develop the trail. Following other foreign donor agencies, the Asian Development Bank has signaled a willingness to fund further development and maintenance once the state stakeholders are fully on board.

The trail runs 300 kilometres across 22 stages through the Central Highlands, from south of Kandy through Hatton, Haputale, and Ella to Nuwara Eliya. It was developed under the Tourism Resilience Project with European Union funding and support from the US Agency for International Development, and has since been listed by Time among the World’s Greatest Places in 2025 and by National Geographic among its top 20 travel experiences for 2024. The survey data underlines why the highlands are the primary testing ground for this pivot. Hiking and trekking has emerged as a top visitor draw, slightly edging out culinary tourism and surfing. Consequently, highland destinations are claiming a larger share of travel itineraries, with Ella now attracting more than half of all visitors, and Nuwara Eliya and Adam’s Peak also seeing steady gains. However, capturing these tourists for longer stays remains a challenge. Visitors average just over a day in Nuwara Eliya and Adam’s Peak, and a single day at Horton Plains, falling well short of the multi-day stays recorded at coastal spots such as Arugam Bay.

The cleanliness reading, however, sets a strict limit on how far that highland positioning can be pushed. Less than a fifth of tourists rated general cleanliness as “Excellent,” making it the only attribute to score so poorly in the top tier. While the majority still found cleanliness at least satisfactory, the combined positive ratings lagged significantly behind other destination strengths, trailing hospitality by nearly 30 points and value for money by 20 points.

Unprompted criticism followed the exact same pattern. Environment and cleanliness generated the largest share of negative feedback, far outpacing complaints about cost, pricing, or scams. These frustrations centered largely on litter, pollution, and degraded beach conditions. Furthermore, facilities at tourist sites - covering restrooms, signage, and paths - ranked as the third weakest attribute overall.

To address this, the Chairman said tourist-friendly washroom construction at key locations would be funded through the Clean Sri Lanka project. He added that the authority is currently negotiating with the Finance Ministry over sharing the tourism development levy with local authorities, which would then be positioned to handle most of the localized garbage collection. The levy is charged under the Finance Act No.25 of 2003 at 1 percent of turnover for licensed tourism institutions, and 0.5 percent for those with an annual turnover below Rs. 12 million.

These state-level interventions come as the private sector voices mounting frustration over the pace of environmental action. Jetwing Hotels Chairman Hiran Cooray has previously described Clean Sri Lanka as a lost golden opportunity to reposition the island as a pristine destination, arguing that a message of an environmentally, ethically, and politically clean country offered the strongest basis in decades for a national tourism brand.

SLTDA’s current scramble to implement decentralized waste management and localized funding reflects an urgent need to close this gap between industry expectations and ground realities.

A separate US$ 100 million World Bank allocation covers recycling plans in the North and East. These provinces currently see the lowest visitor footfall, with destinations like Trincomalee, Jaffna, and Pasikudah capturing only a fraction of total itineraries. Yet, they successfully hold those who do visit for significantly longer periods, averaging stays of around three days.

Despite environmental hurdles, overall sentiment toward the destination remains resiliently strong, with the vast majority of tourists rating their experience highly and over 80 percent stating they would likely return.
