Vision Beyond Sight: A Day of Fellowship at the School for  the Blind, Ratmalana

Visually impaired students are keen and capable players of cricket.

Education is often thought of as something found within the four walls of a classroom. Yet some of the most valuable lessons cannot be found in a textbook.

On  June 9, 2026, the Sub-Prefects’ Guild of Wesley College visited the School for the Blind, Ratmalana, for a day of fellowship and learning. While the visit included music, games, conversation, and shared laughter, it also offered something far more meaningful: the opportunity to better understand a way of life unfamiliar to many of us.

The programme began with a warm welcome and an introduction by the Principal of the Blind School, followed by an introduction to the Prefects’ Guild of Wesley College delivered by one of our own Sub-Prefects. From there, the formalities quickly gave way to something much more natural. Music became the first common language.

Students from both schools took turns performing, sharing songs with one another before the atmosphere gradually shifted from one of introductions to one of friendship. Conversations became easier, laughter became louder, and before long, there was little sense that these were students meeting for the first time.

One of the most eye-opening moments of the day came during a brief introduction to Braille education. For many Wesleyites, Braille had always been something they had heard about but never truly understood. We learned that the students follow the very same national curriculum as any other school in Sri Lanka. The subjects, lessons, and examinations are no different.

The only difference is the medium through which they learn. Instead of printed pages, they read and write using Braille. It was a simple yet powerful reminder that blindness does not lower the standard of education, it simply changes the way that education is accessed.

The afternoon brought one of the day’s highlights: a game of cricket.

Before play began, the students of the Blind School introduced us to the adapted version of the game they play. A rattle is placed inside the ball, allowing players to track its movement by sound rather than sight. At first, it seemed almost impossible. Yet after watching the students play, it became clear that they weren’t overcoming blindness every time they stepped onto the field. They were simply playing the game they knew, with remarkable confidence and skill. In fact, we struggled to keep up with them despite our alleged “advantage” of sight.

For us Wesleyites, it was a reminder that ability often depends far more on opportunity and adaptation than on the assumptions we make.

The rest of the afternoon passed over cups of tea, pieces of cake, and countless conversations. By then, it hardly felt like two different schools spending a few hours together. It simply felt like children being children—sharing jokes, asking questions, teasing one another, and enjoying each other’s company.

No invisible lines were separating the seeing from the visually impaired. There were only students.

Reflecting on the visit, Vice Principal Mr. Iresh Ravindra described it as “a meaningful outreach programme aimed at cultivating empathy, compassion and a deeper understanding of others among students.” He noted that through singing together, playing cricket, sharing experiences, and enjoying tea together, the day became “a powerful lesson that went beyond academics, reminding everyone that true education lies in developing kindness, empathy, and humanity towards others.”

Senior Prefect of Wesley College, Master Joel De Silva, also reflected on the experience: “It is difficult to leave a place like the Blind School without learning something about yourself. What stood out to me wasn’t what the students couldn’t do, it was everything they could. They welcomed us so warmly that it never felt like we were visitors. We spent the day playing, laughing, and talking just as any group of students would. It was an experience that reminded us that some of the strongest people you’ll ever meet simply see the world differently.”

Blind education is often overlooked in conversations about schools. Yet, institutions such as the School for the Blind, Ratmalana, continue to prove what is possible when students are given the tools and support they deserve. Their classrooms may use Braille instead of printed pages. Their cricket ball may rattle instead of bouncing in silence. Their methods may differ from our own. Yet, their ambitions, friendships, humour, and determination do not.

The visit did not teach us to feel sorry for visually impaired students. It taught us to let go of the idea that they ever needed our pity in the first place.

On that day, two schools came together. By the time we left, we were no longer thinking about who could see and who could not.

We were simply students who had shared a day—and walked away with a few more friends than when we arrived.
