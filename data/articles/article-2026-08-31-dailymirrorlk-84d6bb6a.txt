As governor sees stability people waiting for Godot?

Colombo, Aug. 31 (Daily mirror) - Stability Is Real. Recovery Is Not Complete. The Next Battle Is Investment, Exports, Jobs and Prosperity.

Sri Lanka has come a long way from the economic abyss of 2022. The queues have disappeared. Foreign exchange is available. Inflation has been brought under control. Government revenue has strengthened. Fiscal balances have improved. Debt restructuring has provided breathing space. Economic growth has returned.

Governor was therefore justified last week in highlighting the turnaround and stating that the economy has recovered to almost the size it was before the crisis.

But there is an important distinction that Sri Lanka must not ignore.

The economy has stabilized. That does not mean the country has fully recovered. There is a fundamental difference between stopping an economic collapse and rebuilding national prosperity.

If recovery means ending fuel queues, restoring foreign-exchange availability, controlling inflation and bringing order back to public finances, Sri Lanka has achieved an impressive turnaround.

But recovery means restoring household purchasing power, creating quality employment, attracting large-scale investment, expanding industries, accelerating exports and giving young Sri Lankans confidence to build their futures at home, then the job is far from finished.

The World Bank itself has described Sri Lanka’s recovery as “uneven and incomplete”, noting that poverty remained significantly elevated and that many households had still not regained livelihoods lost during the crisis.

This distinction matters. An economy is not merely a balance sheet at the Central Bank.

It is the factory owner deciding whether to expand. It is the entrepreneur deciding whether to invest. It is the exporter fighting to win another international market. It is the graduate deciding whether to stay in Sri Lanka or leave.

And above all, it is the family asking whether its monthly income is enough to pay for food, electricity, transport, housing, healthcare and education.

This is perhaps the biggest misconception surrounding the recovery. Lower inflation is unquestionably good news. But lower inflation does not mean that prices have returned to where they were before the crisis. It simply means that prices are increasing more slowly. The damage caused by the earlier explosion in living costs does not disappear because today’s inflation rate is lower.

The World Bank noted that food prices more than doubled between 2021 and 2024, while real wages remained below their 2019 levels.

That is why ordinary Sri Lankans may hear impressive macroeconomic statistics while still asking a simple question:

If the economy has recovered, why doesn’t my household feel recovered? That is not cynicism. It is economics. The ultimate measure of recovery must be purchasing power, not simply inflation.

Sri Lanka’s debt restructuring was essential. Without it, sustainable recovery would have been virtually impossible. But restructuring debt does not make debt disappear. The country still carries a heavy debt burden and substantial future financing obligations. Sri Lanka therefore cannot return to the economic model that repeatedly produced vulnerability.

Otherwise, Sri Lanka will have treated the symptoms of the crisis without curing the disease that caused it.

Sri Lanka now urgently needs productive domestic and foreign capital—not merely speculative property development or consumption-driven investment.

Every unnecessary approval, contradictory regulation, bureaucratic delay and unpredictable tax decision has an economic cost.

International investors have choices. Vietnam wants their money. India wants it. Bangladesh wants it. Indonesia wants it. Malaysia wants it. African economies increasingly want it.

Sri Lanka cannot behave as though investors are waiting outside Colombo for permission to enter.

Investors do not wait for countries. Countries compete for investors.

That means Sri Lanka needs a genuine one-stop investment mechanism with real decision-making authority . Not another office that merely redirects investors from ministry to ministry.

An investor should not need political connections simply to navigate bureaucracy.

Foreign Direct Investment must become one of Sri Lanka’s principal engines of growth.

And this is where the country’s sovereign credit rating matters.

S&P Global Ratings affirmed Sri Lanka’s long- and short-term sovereign ratings at CCC+/C with a stable outlook in July. Although S&P simultaneously upgraded its country-risk assessment for Sri Lanka from “very high risk” to “high risk”, CCC+ remains deep within speculative-grade territory.

This is not merely a Treasury problem. It is an investment problem. A weak sovereign rating affects how international lenders, investment committees and global corporations assess country risk. It can raise financing costs, increase required returns and make otherwise attractive projects harder to finance.

Sri Lanka therefore needs to treat improvement of its sovereign rating as a national investment priority.

Fiscal discipline, debt reduction, stronger reserves, credible institutions, policy consistency and structural reform are not simply IMF requirements. They are the foundations upon which investor confidence is built.

Today, some of leading companies carry stronger credit ratings / profiles than the sovereign itself. That is both a tribute to the resilience of our private sector and a warning about the state of our economy.

When businesses are stronger than the sovereign, entrepreneurship is not the problem , country risk is.

A weak sovereign rating becomes an invisible tax on every credible Sri Lankan company seeking international capital. Higher borrowing costs, restricted financing and lost investment opportunities.

Sri Lanka cannot build a globally competitive economy while its strongest companies are forced to carry the burden of sovereign weakness. Fixing the sovereign rating is not about borrowing more it is about allowing the entire economy to breathe, invest and grow.

Our companies should not have to pay the price for the sovereign’s weaker credit standing. Rebuilding Sri Lanka’s sovereign rating is therefore not merely a government financing issue; it is fundamental to unlocking private-sector growth, attracting FDI and restoring the country’s competitiveness in glo

We were spending foreign exchange faster than we were sustainably earning it.

Tourism and worker remittances are enormously important. But a modern economy cannot depend indefinitely on tourism, remittances and external borrowing to finance its ambitions.

The country must aggressively expand higher-value export oriented manufacturing. And economic diplomacy must become part of that national export strategy.

Sri Lankan missions abroad cannot remain primarily ceremonial institutions. Every mission should have measurable economic targets. How many investors did you bring to Sri Lanka? How many new markets did you open for Sri Lankan exporters? How many commercial partnerships did you facilitate? How much investment and trade resulted from your work? There is an equally important question that is rarely asked: How many Sri Lankan companies did you help take beyond our shores? How many Sri Lankan businesses did you facilitate to establish operations, factories, investments or partnerships in the countries where you were stationed or accredited? Opening doors for Sri Lankan enterprises abroad is just as important as attracting investors into Sri Lanka. Economic diplomacy must be measured by results, not receptions and cocktails , speeches, photographs or protocol. If a mission cannot demonstrate what economic value it has created for the country, then we must ask what exactly its economic diplomacy has achieved. Ambassadors should not be cocktail ambassadors but a marketing manager for the country. Economic Diplomacy must deliver strictly measurable economic outcomes.

There is another lesson from 2022 that Sri Lanka must never forget.

Governments make major decisions based on the information and advice placed before them.

Therefore, economists, officials, advisers and institutions occupying influential positions carry enormous responsibility.

Sri Lanka cannot afford advisers who tell political leaders what they want to hear merely to preserve their positions, influence or relevance.

Economic advice must serve the country—not the careers of those giving it.

The most valuable adviser is sometimes the person prepared to walk into the room and say. “No. This will not work.”

Sri Lanka paid an enormous economic and social price when warning signs were ignored, uncomfortable realities were postponed and difficult decisions were avoided.

This Government must insist on evidence, accountability and intellectual honesty from those advising it.

The objective cannot be to recreate Sri Lanka 2019 or Sri Lanka 2021. The objective must be to create a fundamentally more competitive Sri Lanka for 2030 and beyond.

Recovery is not simply when reserves rise. It is when exports and productive industries continuously generate foreign exchange. Recovery is not simply when government revenue rises. It is when businesses grow, wages rise and prosperity naturally expands the tax base. Recovery is not simply when credit ratings improve. It is when global investors trust Sri Lanka enough to commit billions in long-term capital. Recovery is not simply when GDP returns to its pre-crisis size. It is when families regain purchasing power and businesses regain the confidence to invest, hire and expand.

And recovery is not simply when a country avoids another default. It is when the economic structure that caused the previous crisis has fundamentally changed.

Sri Lanka has won the first battle. It has restored stability against extraordinary odds.

Now comes the harder battle: turning stability into recovery ie; investment, investment into production, production into exports, exports into foreign exchange, and economic growth into household prosperity.

Recovery will restore prosperity. Transformation must ensure Sri Lanka never returns to 2022.
