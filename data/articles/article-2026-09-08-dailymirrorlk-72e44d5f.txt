‘I’m not a war criminal’: Shavendra Silva hits back at Western sanctions

Colombo, September 08 (Daily Mirror) - Former Sri Lanka Army Commander and Chief of Defence Staff General Shavendra Silva has defended the military’s conduct during the final stages of the country’s civil war, rejecting allegations of war crimes and criticising the sanctions imposed on him by Western governments. Speaking during an in-depth interview with the YouTube programme 'Impact Stories', Silva discussed the final phase of the conflict, 'international criticism of Sri Lanka’s military campaign and his journey from a school prefect to one of the country’s most senior military officers.

Addressing sanctions imposed by Western governments, including the United States and the United Kingdom, Silva denied allegations of wrongdoing and maintained that his actions were carried out in accordance with military law. He said the armed forces were engaged in a campaign to defeat terrorism and protect the country, while operating under established military rules concerning combat operations and the surrender of fighters.

“I have been sanctioned by the United States of America, and I was sanctioned recently by the United Kingdom. I've been asking: tell me what I have done wrong. I’m not a war criminal. I fought for my country. What I've been tasked to do, I do. If I had gone against military law, I should be punished if I had done something wrong.

Silva also highlighted the rehabilitation of former members of the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE), saying approximately 12,000 former LTTE combatants were rehabilitated and subsequently reintegrated into society.

"We were very concerned about our own people. Although they were terrorists, they were our own people. As far as they surrendered their weapons, we had no issue taking them in. That is how 12,000 people were rehabilitated later on—terrorists who fought the war, who accepted they were terrorists, who had blasted the airport, who had attacked the sacred Relics of Lord Buddha. Yet they have been given a pardon, whereas the professional military who were on the front lines are being sanctioned all over the world.

As far as I know, the LTTE agenda was to divide the country. It was driven by a very small number of people. We can’t say the population didn't have grievances—they would have had grievances to address, but there are proper ways and means. No one should take up weapons and fight. A lot of parents lost their children—not because they willingly gave them away, but because they were forcefully taken by terrorists. In Sri Lanka, a President was assassinated; Rajiv Gandhi was killed—all of that happened at the hands of terrorists. Can anyone truly say the LTTE was merely an organisation fighting for their rights? No," he said.

The former Army Commander also criticised international media coverage of the war, arguing that some foreign reports presented an inaccurate picture of events on the ground. He recalled instances in which television footage was accompanied by what he described as incorrect translations of conversations, potentially creating misleading impressions about military operations and alleged attacks on civilians. Silva said international organisations and foreign governments should engage directly with Sri Lankan authorities and examine information from multiple sources rather than relying solely on reports from third parties.

"Those who are campaigning abroad have mostly been wrongly briefed. At the end of the day, I don't blame all of them. I blame successive governments for failing to take the right story to the world.

What I suggest to the international arena—for those who feel the Sri Lankan armed forces have done wrong—is to come to a direct dialogue. Speak to us. Tell us: ‘You did this on this particular day at this particular time.’ That way we can clarify the facts directly, without relying on third- or fourth-party information. If we have genuinely done something wrong, we will accept it. But you must give us the chance to address it directly," General Silva said.

During the interview, Silva also discussed aspects of his personal life and early upbringing. He credited his mother, who worked as an English teacher, and his father, who served as Chairman of the Sri Lanka Transport Board, for helping shape his discipline, character and leadership qualities. He reflected on how his childhood experiences and education contributed to his eventual decision to pursue a career in the military.

Silva further emphasised the importance of Sri Lanka’s longstanding defence relationship with India. He praised the close cooperation between the two countries’ armed forces and spoke positively about his personal relationships with senior Indian military officials. According to Silva, the strong defence ties between Sri Lanka and India have played an important role in maintaining cooperation between the two countries.

Reflecting on May 18, 2009, General Shavendra Silva described the day as the most emotional and significant moment of his life, recalling his role on the ground during the final stages of the Sri Lankan civil war.

“I would say that the most emotional thing was the day of 18th May 2009—the day that Sri Lanka was liberated from the clutches of terrorism. I was on the ground. As mentioned, I was the luckiest person because my division finished the last day of the war. And I was the one who called the Army Chief then and said: ‘Sir, we have liberated the last inch of the land, and you can announce to the country and tell the President that terrorism is over.’

When I said terrorism is over, now we are in the 17th year, right? Terrorism is over on Sri Lankan soil. So for being born, with all other emotions, that achievement that I had in my life—nothing of that can be matched for what I had done for the mankind of Sri Lankan society, for the people of Sri Lanka.

As you know, there were times when parents used to take two children in two separate vehicles. Father and mother never travelled in one vehicle, so if one got caught in a bomb—which terrorists were doing in Colombo—at least the other parent would survive to rescue the other child. That was the time that we had.

Today, what you see in Sri Lanka—the freedom that they enjoy, irrespective of which religion or which caste you belong to, you can go to any part of Sri Lanka without any problem, day or night. You see, that freedom was given by the gallant men and women of the Sri Lankan armed forces on the ground.

I, being the most successful division commander in Sri Lankan history, am very happy and proud. Even though I had a little bit of issues of my own, personally, with how I have been treated, the fact remains that that was the day I was so happy.

Until I live, even on my last breath, I'll be happy that I was a Sri Lankan, that I joined the army, for my parents giving birth to me, for me to give birth to two daughters. For everything, I have done my job as a military person. There's nothing I could say to match this day as the most emotional day in my life.”
