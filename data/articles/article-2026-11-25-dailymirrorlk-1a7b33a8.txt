Sri Lankan refugee dies months after forced UK resettlement in Eswatini

BBC - The UK forcibly resettled a refugee and convicted sex offender in an African country with which he had no connection, in a deal not disclosed to the UK Parliament or the local community.

The man, who was from Sri Lanka and had a history of severe mental illness and suicide attempts, took his own life months after arriving in Eswatini.

The BBC has obtained a copy of the draft deal between the two governments, in which the UK pledges to cover the costs of his resettlement for up to three years.

The UK government was finalising plans for 35-year-old Kishanth Peramalya to live there permanently when he died.

Migration experts told the BBC this was the first known case of the UK government forcibly resettling in such a manner a refugee permanently overseas.

In 2024, the Labour government scrapped a controversial plan to send asylum seekers with pending claims to Rwanda. Now it wants to send failed asylum seekers overseas to third countries when they cannot be sent to their home countries, but is yet to announce any deals. Last week, five EU countries reached a deal to send people whose claims have been refused to Rwanda.

The Eswatini deal raises questions about how much the UK government should disclose about such arrangements, and how it protects both the people it sends overseas and the communities that receive them.

The UK government told the BBC that protecting the British public was its priority and that it "continue[s] to work with the Eswatini authorities in relation to this case".

Peramalya's family is demanding answers about his treatment, while critics in Eswatini have accused Britain of using their country as a "dumping ground".

In 2021, the Royal Navy rescued Peramalya in the Indian Ocean, along with dozens of other Sri Lankan asylum seekers, as they tried to sail from India to Canada.

They were then detained in a camp on Diego Garcia, an island in the British Indian Ocean Territory, run exclusively as a UK-US military base.

The island's authorities, who answer to London, recognised that Peramalya and others could not safely return to Sri Lanka, and awarded them refugee protections. But the group remained in the camp for three years while British officials debated their future. A court later ruled their detention had been unlawful.

As a teenager, Peramalya had been tortured and sexually abused by officers in the Sri Lankan military and was later diagnosed with PTSD. During his detention on Diego Garcia, his mental health deteriorated, according to court papers. He suffered from depression and anxiety, and repeatedly attempted suicide. UN inspectors who came to the island raised concerns over the conditions he was being kept in.

While there, he committed a series of crimes, including assault and arson, during two suicide attempts, and spent 24 weeks in prison. He was also convicted of four counts of sexual assault for which he received a suspended prison sentence.

In December 2024, most of the Sri Lankan asylum seekers were allowed to enter the UK but ministers refused to let Peramalya join them because of his crimes. He remained on Diego Garcia.

The High Court upheld the UK's refusal to admit him, in part because of the risk he posed and because ministers were making genuine efforts to find somewhere else safe for him to live. Court documents, seen by the BBC, show the UK approached more than 20 countries and could not secure an agreement.

Then, in February 2026, the US began using its military base on Diego Garcia in its war with Iran. The island authorities began withdrawing non-essential personnel.

At the end of March, the Maldives offered to take Peramalya for 30 days. He was transferred there before being sent on to Eswatini, formerly known as Swaziland.

At the time of his death, the British and Eswatini governments had been finalising the agreement for Peramalya to live there permanently, with the UK to pay for the "reasonable costs" of his accommodation, healthcare, and other support for up to three years.

The document does not specify a total cost or explain how the two governments would manage the risks Peramalya posed to himself and others. But it explicitly acknowledged he required support that would meet his basic needs and protect his physical and mental health, and wellbeing.

Peramalya's family has told the BBC he was initially placed in an isolated guesthouse on his own.

Speaking from India, his sister said the family had not been aware of the plan to make his transfer permanent, and that his mental health had worsened while in the country.

She said he would call her four times a day. "My brother did not want to go there at all," she said. "He couldn't manage being there alone, he was very lonely." The family also said it had been unaware of sexual offences committed by him.

He initially saw a psychiatrist weekly, she said. Later, after his mental health declined, he had daily appointments, and an attendant was brought in to prepare his food.

The UN's refugee agency, which had previously raised concerns about Peramalya's condition on Diego Garcia, said it was "deeply saddened" by his death and stressed that refugees being transferred to other different countries must be protected.

"UNHCR recognises that asylum seekers and refugees may, in certain circumstances, be lawfully transferred to a third country. However, this requires that specific safeguards are in place and those conditions are fulfilled in practice."

Peter Walsh, of Oxford University's Migration Observatory, described the case as "highly unusual".

"In most cases, people with criminal convictions who cannot be returned to their home country would simply remain in the UK. Third countries are generally quite reluctant to accept foreign national offenders," he said.

It is unclear how Britain chose Eswatini, but the kingdom had already agreed to accept US deportees under President Trump's wider programme of sending migrants to countries across Africa and Latin America. In 2025, the Trump administration paid Eswatini $5.1m (£3.9m) to accept up to 160 people.

Melusi Simelane, of the Southern African Litigation Centre, who is challenging the US deal in court, said migrant transfers were putting pressure on his country's limited resources.

"Our healthcare system is crumbling, not to mention our mental healthcare, so it is a big ask to bring people into our society who need a lot of support," he said.

"Africa is becoming a dumping ground for the world's unresolved problems."

Zakithi Sibandze, of the Eswatini Rural Women's Assembly, which campaigns to protect women and girls, said it was "very painful" that a sex offender had been relocated to her country.

"Why did they think it was okay to send him to Africa?" she asked. "The UK seems to be saying it is fine for people to commit crimes against women in poor countries."

Earlier this year, the UN's special rapporteur on migrants' rights, Gehad Madi, urged governments to publish the terms of their migration arrangements with other countries and subject them to parliamentary and public oversight. "Strengthening transparency is essential to reduce human rights risks, ensure accountability and uphold the rule of law," he said.

Neither the UK nor Eswatini authorities answered the BBC's questions on the circumstances surrounding Peramalya's resettlement, but a spokesperson for the British government said: "Keeping the British public safe will always be our first priority, and we will continue to take the decisions necessary to protect the British people. That includes taking steps to ensure high-harm foreign criminals cannot enter the UK."
