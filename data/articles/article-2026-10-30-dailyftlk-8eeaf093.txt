Israel’s electoral post-mortem: Caretaker’s paradox and rigid coalition matrix

The October 2026 election underscores that the era of a monolithic far-right executive branch in Israel has been checked by the electorate, yet the Opposition has failed to construct a seamless alternative.  For the wider Arab world, this signals a transition into an era of highly transactional, defensive, and fractured governance in Jerusalem

To understand what Benjamin Netanyahu’s political standing means for the Middle East, one must first analyse the internal forces molding his domestic base. Rather than a sudden collapse, the election on 27 October 2026, resulted in a complex political deadlock, cementing Netanyahu’s role as a consummate political survivor while leaving the country facing massive gridlock.

Critics frequently accuse his Government of failing to prevent the assault and subsequent multi-front escalations. They view his strategic maneuvering not only as an effort to prolong regional conflict for personal political survival, but as an affront to the families of the victims who demand accountability. For decades, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s primary contract with the Israeli public was centred on guaranteeing national security. That perception was profoundly challenged during the Hamas-led attacks of 7 October 2023.The public has remained deeply divided over the systemic intelligence and operational failures that occurred under his watch. While opponents view his subsequent actions through the lens of political self-preservation, his core supporters continue to view him as the most capable hand to navigate existential multi-front security challenges.

Netanyahu’s political alliances with ultra-Orthodox (Haredi) parties have increasingly alienated mainstream secular and nationalist voters. The boiling point arrived over military conscription exemptions for Haredi men. While secular Israelis and reservists bore the immense physical and economic burden of ongoing conflicts, Netanyahu’s legislative coordination with ultra-Orthodox parties drawing public ire sparked widespread demands for systemic reform. Opposition figures like Gadi Eisenkot have strongly mobilised voters by campaigning for a “Zionist Government of enlistment” to counter what they characterise as a “draft-dodging bloc.”

As Israel evaluates its long-term direction, the impact of Netanyahu’s historic 19-plus years in office remains a central debate:

Pros: Supporters credit Netanyahu with transforming Israel into a global technological and economic powerhouse, successfully bypassing traditional diplomatic blockades to secure the historic Abraham Accords, and maintaining an unyielding, necessary deterrent posture against Iran and its proxy network.

Cons: Critics point to deepening domestic polarisation, institutional strain caused by controversial judicial overhauls, international isolation over civilian casualties during the regional conflicts, and long-running corruption trials that opponents argue compromise his governance.

Preliminary results from the Israel Central Elections Committee reveals that neither major political bloc secured an outright majority to easily form a Government.

According to official preliminary counts with over 90% of standard ballots processed:

Netanyahu’s Right-Wing Bloc: Projected to hold 53 to 54 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, short of the 61 needed for a majority. Netanyahu’s Likud party remains the largest single party in parliament, outperforming pre-election polling to clinch a projected 26 seats.

The Opposition “Change” Bloc: Led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot and his Yashar party (projected at 21 seats), the anti-Netanyahu coalition stands at a combined 48 seats.

The Arab-Led Parties: Spurred by a massive mobilisation rejecting Netanyahu’s platform, Arab-led parties surged to an unprecedented record of 19 seats.

The Joint List leadership signaled on October 28, that Arab voter turnout had played a decisive role in keeping Netanyahu short of a parliamentary majority. However, because most mainstream Jewish Opposition parties have historically declined to form direct governing coalitions with Arab-led parties, a rigid coalition matrix emerges. Without them, Eisenkot has no clear path to a 61-seat majority, leaving Netanyahu to remain in power as caretaker prime minister indefinitely while political gridlock plays out.

With Netanyahu locked into a caretaker status and neither bloc holding a clear mandate Israel’s strategic posture vis-à-vis its neighbours enters a decisive phase governed by a complex domestic stalemate.

Netanyahu’s signature regional strategy has been defined by an aggressive, high-stakes confrontation with Iran. The depth of this brinkmanship was laid bare when it emerged that Netanyahu had pressured its Israeli military to authorise pre-election strikes against Tehran; a move that prompted Opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot to publicly warn security chiefs to resist political exploitation of national defense. While an indefinite caretaker administration technically retains full wartime powers, institutional analysts at the IDI note that transitional Governments are legally bound by a strict “duty of restraint”. Any attempt by Netanyahu to leverage security policy for coalition negotiations will face unprecedented domestic oversight and legal friction from a deeply divided Knesset.

The normalisation paradigm faces a critical juncture. President Donald Trump publicly endorsed Netanyahu ahead of the vote and claimed credit on social media for converting what could have been a decisive defeat into a come-from-behind stalemate. Analysts suggest that Trump may use his significant leverage to press Netanyahu into forming a broader unity Government, potentially sidelining far-right extremist ministers like Itamar Ben-Gvir and Bezalel Smotrich. Sidelining far-right elements would remove a primary friction point for Arab capitals like Riyadh, which have conditioned further integration on meaningful diplomatic movement for Palestinians.

The political gridlock delays a clear post-war roadmap for Gaza and the WB. While Gadi Eisenkot and centrist factions are highly vocal about engaging with international frameworks and U.S.-led peace plans to resolve Gaza’s governance, they lack the parliamentary numbers to bypass Netanyahu. As such, the entry door to comprehensive regional diplomacy remains heavily guarded by a deadlocked domestic system.

The political gridlock in Jerusalem injects profound uncertainty for both Washington and the ME, particularly as a fractured Israeli caretaker Government navigates the fallout of a direct U.S.-Iran military conflict. With Netanyahu lacking a clear majority but retaining full security powers, the U.S. faces an uphill task of managing an unpredictable ally while attempting to contain regional escalation. In a direct confrontation between Washington and Tehran, any unilateral Israeli strike against Iranian assets could instantly drag the U.S. into a deeper, open-ended war, further straining American military and diplomatic resources. For the wider Middle East, this volatile dynamic threatens to destabilise vital maritime trade corridors like the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea, triggering severe global economic shocks through spikes in oil prices and disrupted supply chains. As such, Gulf capitals, hemmed in by a gridlocked Israel and an aggressive Iran, find themselves compelled to fast-track localised de-escalation and defensive security pacts, effectively sidelining broader, U.S.-led regional agreements.

The ongoing political deadlock, coupled with the fallout of the U.S.-Iran conflict, sets the stage for highly volatile economic and security outcomes. Real-time assessments point toward two primary trajectories:

The Fragmentation Scenario (High Risk): On the security front, an unchecked Israeli caretaker Government operating without a strong legislative mandate might engage in miscalculated tactical escalations against Iranian proxies to assert domestic strength. Economically, this would solidify the closure of vital maritime trade corridors like the Strait of Hormuz. For the global economy, prolonged disruptions there risk driving oil prices past $100 a barrel, severely choking energy-dependent European and Asian markets and inducing a persistent inflationary shock. Within Israel, the lack of a cohesive state budget due to gridlock, paired with astronomical defense spending, threatens to trigger further credit rating downgrades and capital flight.

The Transactional Stabilisation Scenario (Moderate Risk): Alternatively, heavy diplomatic pressure from Washington could force a frozen status quo. In this security framework, Israel shifts toward a purely defensive posture, relying heavily on the US regional air-defense umbrella while avoiding deep offensive incursions. Economically, this baseline stability would allow GCC states to accelerate localised trade corridors and supply-chain workarounds that bypass active conflict zones. While broader regional normalisation agreements would remain frozen, defensive security pacts and energy-sharing agreements between Israel, Jordan, and Egypt would function on a strictly transactional, back-channel basis to protect baseline regional infrastructure.

The October 2026 election underscores that the era of a monolithic far-right executive branch in Israel has been checked by the electorate, yet the Opposition has failed to construct a seamless alternative.  For the wider Arab world, this signals a transition into an era of highly transactional, defensive, and fractured governance in Jerusalem. While security concerns across the Israeli political spectrum remain deeply conservative, the immediate future will be dictated by intense coalition bargaining, external pressure from Washington, and the inescapable matrix of a divided parliament.
