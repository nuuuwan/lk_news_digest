When will South Asia wake up to climate reality?

It no longer arrives as a distant warning. Climate change is here, it is relentless, and it is unforgiving. Across South Asia, from the flooded plains of Bangladesh to the heat-scorched regions of India and Pakistan, the signs are unmistakable. Yet, governments continue to react to disasters rather than prepare for them. Nowhere is this more evident than in Sri Lanka.

For years, scientists and environmentalists have sounded the alarm. Rising temperatures, erratic rainfall, prolonged droughts and intense storms were all predicted. In Sri Lanka alone, floods and landslides have become an almost annual tragedy. Entire communities are displaced, homes are destroyed, and lives are lost. Still, each disaster is treated as an isolated incident rather than part of a growing climate crisis.

The recent Ditwah disaster stands as a painful reminder. Hundreds of lives were lost. Key infrastructure collapsed. Roads were washed away, cutting off entire villages and leaving families stranded without access to basic necessities. It was not just a natural disaster. It was a failure of preparedness. It exposed how vulnerable the country remains despite repeated warnings.

South Asia is one of the most climate vulnerable regions in the world. According to global climate risk assessments, countries like Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and India rank among those most exposed to extreme weather events.. When floods hit or droughts stretch for months, the impact is not just environmental. It is economic, social and deeply human. In Sri Lanka, the numbers paint a worrying picture. Thousands are affected every year by floods. Landslides alone have claimed dozens of lives annually in high-risk districts such as Kegalle, Kalutara and Nuwara Eliya. Yet, budget allocations for disaster preparedness and climate adaptation remain insufficient and often poorly utilised.

There is a dangerous cycle at play. Disaster strikes. Emergency relief is mobilised. Funds are released. Promises are made. And then, slowly, attention fades until the next disaster hits. This reactive approach is no longer acceptable.

First, governments must prioritise early warning systems. Technology exists to predict heavy rainfall, floods and landslides days in advance. Yet, gaps in communication mean that warnings often do not reach the most vulnerable communities in time. Second, infrastructure must be built to withstand extreme weather. Roads, bridges and drainage systems in Sri Lanka are not designed for the intensity of rainfall now being experienced. Poor urban planning has only worsened the situation, with wetlands reclaimed and natural waterways blocked. Climate-resilient infrastructure is no longer a luxury. It is a necessity.

Third, land use policies must be enforced. Year after year, we see homes built in landslide-prone areas and flood plains. Regulations exist, but enforcement is weak. Political interference and short-term interests often override safety concerns. This must change.

Fourth, South Asia must invest in regional cooperation. Climate change does not respect borders. Sharing data, resources and strategies can significantly improve preparedness. A coordinated regional response could help mitigate the impact of disasters that affect millions.

Funding remains a key challenge, but it is also a question of priorities. International climate finance is available, yet accessing and utilising these funds effectively requires strong governance and transparency. Equally important is public awareness. Climate change is not just a government issue. Simple actions such as maintaining drainage systems, avoiding illegal construction and protecting natural ecosystems can make a significant difference.

The cost of inaction is far greater than the cost of preparedness. Every flood that destroys homes, every landslide that takes lives, and every drought that devastates livelihoods adds to an already heavy burden. For developing economies in South Asia, repeated disasters can stall growth and push vulnerable populations deeper into poverty.

Sri Lanka’s experience should serve as a wake-up call for the region. Climate change can no longer be treated as an environmental issue alone. It is a national security concern, an economic challenge and a humanitarian crisis.  South Asia stands at a crossroads. It can continue down the path of reaction and regret, or it can choose preparedness and resilience. What is needed now is action.. The real question is whether we will act before the next disaster, or once again count the cost after it.
