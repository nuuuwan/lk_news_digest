Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill (PSTB): Why isn’t there a big fuss over PSTB as against 22A?

The Ranil Wickremasinghe administration proposed several drastic bills with a view to suppress public protests

At the same time, those who draft laws and pass them in Parliament cannot predict whether authorities like the police will abuse a law or use it to torment others. The infamous PTA serves as a prime example as no one would have imagined at its inception that someone could be detained under it for over 20 years. Yet, it happened.

The Supreme Court on Tuesday started considering 22 petitions challenging the “Protection of the State from Terrorism” Bill, which was recently tabled in Parliament.

This is one of three repressive bills published in the gazette by the government within a week, starting from September 30. The three bills are: The Protection of the State from Terrorism Bill (PSTB), the Prevention of Foreign Terrorist Fighters Bill and the Prevention of Organised Crime Bill.

The PSTB was published on September 22 and presented in Parliament on October 6,  and the other two were published on September 30.

We call them repressive bills solely because they have been introduced with the specific intent of suppressing certain forces that are inimical to the society. Among these three Bills, the piece of legislation that demands careful scrutiny is the PSTB,  as the other two will not significantly impact ordinary, peace-loving citizens.

Historically, the government that attempted to bring in the most repressive laws in the shortest span of time was the one led by President Ranil Wickremesinghe from 2022 to 2024, backed by the Sri Lanka Podujana Peramuna (SLPP). About two months after Ranil Wickremesinghe assumed the presidency—specifically on September 23, 2022—his Justice Minister, Dr. Wijeyadasa Rajapakshe, tabled a Bill in Parliament to establish a rehabilitation bureau.

This clearly appeared to be a ploy to detain dissenting individuals without trial. Consequently, the Supreme Court, which examined petitions filed against it, ruled it unconstitutional, leading the government to drop it.

On that very same day, September 23, 2022, President Wickremesinghe issued another repressive measure via a gazette notification under which he declared eight High Security Zones in Colombo and its suburbs. It became glaringly obvious that the purpose of these High Security Zones was to suppress public protests at a time when the country was not at war. When several organizations, including the Samagi Jana Balawegaya (SJB), moved to approach the Supreme Court against this, President Wickremesinghe withdrew the notification through a fresh gazette just a week later, on October 1, 2022.

However, the government did not abandon its efforts to enforce control. It published a new Anti-Terrorism Bill via a gazette on March 22, 2023. It had been framed in such a way that taking to the streets to protest for a demand or launching a strike could be classified as a terrorist offense. Following widespread domestic and international criticism from various quarters, the government deferred tabling it in Parliament indefinitely.

Next, the Wickremesinghe administration drafted a Bill to appoint a Broadcasting Regulatory Commission. It was also viewed as another attempt to strip away the people’s freedom of expression. Due to public opposition, it was never even presented to Parliament. However, amidst fierce resistance, the government successfully passed the Online Safety Bill in October 2023.

The reason Wickremesinghe repeatedly attempted to introduce such restrictive laws was his awareness that he lacked public backing. He harboured a fear that the public, who rose against Gotabaya Rajapaksa’s government and toppled it, might overthrow his administration as well.

His very first action upon taking the oath as President was deployment of the military to assault and disperse the protestors who had paved his way to power. Following that event, when two women carrying a banner travelled from Kalutara to Colombo, an entire police contingent was dispatched just to stop them.

Regarding the repressive bills currently introduced by the National People’s Power (NPP) government, neither the opposition parties nor the human rights organisations—which typically speak out against such restrictive laws—have uttered a single word against the Prevention of Foreign Terrorist Fighters Bill. It remains unclear whether they are unaware of it or are simply unconcerned. Even upon a superficial reading and examination of the Bill’s clauses, it does not appear to adversely affect Sri Lankans. The Bill defines travel from Sri Lanka to foreign countries or entering Sri Lanka from abroad for terrorist activities as offenses.

However, it also specifies that Sri Lankans, not just foreign nationals, can be penalised under this law. While this Bill may have been drafted to satisfy international players, there is no guarantee that future governments will not weaponise it to persecute their adversaries.

The PSTB, which is before the Supreme Court now, is intended to replace the Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA) of 1979, which has been used against thousands of people over the decades. The so-called “Yahapalana” government had previously assured the international community that it would repeal the PTA and enact a new law to tackle terrorism in compliance with international human rights standards. In line with that commitment, the current National People’s Power government has brought forward this new Bill.

The Yahapalana government had introduced the “Counter-Terrorism Bill” (CTB) in 2018, to fulfill the promises made to the international community, including the UN Human Rights Council. It was abandoned after generating severe domestic and international backlash for being worse than the PTA. Later, Ranil Wickremesinghe, who was Prime Minister at the time, assumed the presidency and reintroduced the exact same legislation in 2023 under the name “Anti-Terrorism Bill,” (ATB) which was referenced above.

Both the Counter Terrorism Bill and the Anti-Terrorism Bill presented by Wickremesinghe classified those acts that compel the government to do or not to do something as an act of terrorism. However, the current PSTB adds a proviso stating that public protests and strikes advanced for legitimate demands will not be treated as terrorist activities.

Is this the reason why opposition parties, the Bar Association, and other organisations—who previously mounted massive months-long struggles and filed over sixty petitions in the Supreme Court against the 22nd Constitutional Amendment that aimed at extending the term of judges—have not launched any major protest against this Bill?

Generally, laws function as mechanisms to regulate or control human behaviour. Once a law is enacted on a subject, violating its provisions becomes a punishable offense. Therefore, any law will inherently contain elements that restrict public actions. Yet, the degree of restriction should not violate basic human rights.

At the same time, those who draft laws and pass them in Parliament cannot predict whether authorities like the police will abuse a law or use it to torment others. The infamous PTA serves as a prime example as no one would have imagined at its inception that someone could be detained under it for over 20 years. Yet, it happened. Similarly, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) Act, based on an international covenant, was enacted in 2007 during Mahinda Rajapaksa’s administration. While the prime objective of that covenant was to protect human freedoms, including the rights of minority communities, the resulting domestic law has predominantly been used against minorities.
