Sri Lankan by passport: Foreigner at uni gate: Betrayal behind Sri Lanka’s expatriate  higher  education crisis

Presidents of the Sri Lankan expatriate community in Qatar (from right) Sri Lanka Business and Professional Council Dr. Mohamed Rislan, Sri Lankan Cordinaton Committee Dulip Liyanage and Sri Lankan Community Benevolent Forum Ajith Ishantha  hand over the proposal to Prime Minister Dr. Harini Amarasuriya

Are fathers who keep their children in Sri Lanka for higher education doing it at the unbearable cost of sacricing their futures?

A reform submitted to the Prime Minister requests a clearly defined sub category for Sri Lankan expatriate children seeking uni entry

UGC’s Admission Handbook states that only 0.5% of the total intake is reserved, fee-free, for children of diplomatic personnel

Most Sri Lankan state officials cannot afford to send their children to study in Sri Lankan universities

An engineer who has spent over twenty years pouring concrete and steel into buildings across the Gulf maintains that the hardest structure he never managed to build was a future for both his daughters. “My elder daughter studied at a Sri Lankan university. I paid fees so high they nearly broke me, month after month, just to keep her there. When it was my younger daughter’s turn, I sat down and did the maths, and I knew I could not survive it twice. I sent her to Bangladesh instead.” He pauses before adding the line that says everything: “I gave one daughter her country. I could not afford to give the other the same gift.”

4.5 percent is set aside for foreign-quota university admissions, a category that also includes SAARC applicants, other foreign nationals, and, remarkably, Sri Lankan citizens living abroad. Our children, who possess Sri Lankan passports and have never renounced their nationality, are folded into this foreign quota and charged the very same fees as applicants with no Sri Lankan citizenship

A quantity surveyor, twenty-five years into a career built almost entirely away from home, tells a quieter, but no less devastating story. Every rupee he had saved, year after patient year, to open a small business in Sri Lanka when he finally returned for good is gone now; spent entirely on his son’s Sri Lankan university degree. “I have no regrets about my son’s education,” he says. “But I am sixty next year, and I have nothing left to retire on, and no business to come home to. I paid a foreign national’s fee to educate a Sri Lankan child, and it cost me my own future.”

Another father did keep both his daughters studying in Sri Lanka, a rare achievement under the current system, but the cost was his life’s work. “I lost everything I had saved,” he says simply. “The business I dreamed of starting when I came home no longer exists. Only on paper.” Two daughters graduated. One dream did not survive.

Perhaps the cruelest story belongs to a senior officer at a Sri Lankan diplomatic mission abroad, a man whose entire career has been built on representing his country’s interests to the world. His own two daughters are studying in Estern Europe at present not because he wanted them a continent away from their grandparents, their language, their roots, but because even he, an official of the Sri Lankan state itself, could not afford to send them to a Sri Lankan university. “I represent Sri Lanka every day of my working life,” he says. “But when it came to my own children’s education, Sri Lanka would not have me.”

Four families. Four professions. One devastating pattern, repeating itself in Doha, Kuwait, Riyadh and every international city where Sri Lankans work: parents who have given their country decades of labour and billions of dollars in remittances, only to find that when it is their own child’s turn to walk through a Sri Lankan university gate, that gate treats them as strange foreigners.

This is not a new problem, and it is not the failure of any single government. The University Grants Commission’s own Admission Handbook lays it out plainly: 0.5 percent of total intake is reserved, fee-free, for the children of Sri Lankan diplomatic personnel. A separate 4.5 percent is set aside for foreign-quota admissions, a category that also includes SAARC applicants, other foreign nationals, and, remarkably, Sri Lankan citizens living abroad. Our children, who hold nothing but a Sri Lankan passport and have never renounced their nationality, are folded into this foreign quota and charged the very same fees as applicants with no citizenship link to this country at all. Successive governments, of every political colour, have inherited this anomaly and left it untouched for years.

Worse still, Sri Lanka has made itself more expensive for its own citizens. A medical degree that costs an expatriate family nearly US$ 15,000 a year at home can be pursued for a fraction of that in Georgia, Malaysia, India, Armenia or Bangladesh. Families are not abandoning Sri Lanka by choice. Sri Lanka’s own policy is pushing them out the door.

The reform submitted to Prime Minister Harini Amarasuriya asks for something precise, and deliberately modest: a clearly defined sub category for Sri Lankan expatriate children, carved out from within the existing 4.5 percent foreign quota, not a single seat beyond it. Of that 4.5 percent, a minimum of three percentage points would be ring fenced for our children, with clear published eligibility rules, fees set at roughly a quarter of the current international rate, fee-free places for the highest achievers, guaranteed hostel accommodation for children arriving without parents, and honest information reaching families through our embassies abroad.

This is the detail that matters most, and the one most often missed: these seats already exist. The 4.5 percent foreign quota is not new capacity the state must build, it is already outside the pool reserved for local students, already budgeted for, already accounted for in every university’s intake planning. We are not asking for a bigger slice of the cake. We are asking for a fairer division of the slice that has already been cut, one that no local Sri Lankan student will ever feel. Not one local applicant loses a seat. Not one rupee of government or university budget is touched. The only change is who, within an allocation that already exists, gets to walk through the door.

In conversations with policymakers, one mistaken fear keeps surfacing: that any new quota for expatriate children must come at the expense of local students, or strain university resources already stretched thin. This fear misunderstands the arithmetic entirely. We are not asking to expand the foreign quota from 4.5 percent to anything higher we are asking that three of those 4.5 percentage points, already set aside for non-local applicants, go to Sri Lankan citizens first, before any other foreign national. If a university currently admits, say, 45 foreign quota students out of a 1,000-seat intake, this reform does not ask for a 46th seat. It asks that roughly 30 of those same 45 seats go to Sri Lankan passport holders whose parents built this economy from abroad, rather than to applicants with no citizenship connection to Sri Lanka at all. Where policymakers should genuinely focus their energy is not on resisting this reallocation, but on the separate, larger question of whether university capacity overall needs strengthening more lecture halls, more laboratories, more academic staff  an investment this very reform helps part-fund, since fee-paying expatriate seats generate revenue no local student’s seat does.

A second misunderstanding, equally damaging, has taken root: the fear that this reform would allow academically unqualified expatriate children to buy their way into Sri Lankan universities simply because their parents can pay, while local students who cannot pay are held to a strict, merit-based Z-score cut-off for the very same courses. This impression is understandable, but it is incorrect, and it deserves to be addressed directly. This proposal is not, and has never been, a request to lower the bar for our children. It is a request to hold them to the same bar their local peers already clear. Category (b) eligibility is deliberately designed to run parallel to the local merit system, not around it: applicants must meet subject by subject academic benchmarks calibrated to be equivalent to what a local A-Level student must achieve to qualify for that same course, verified through recognized international curriculum boards, against clear published thresholds, not a discretionary or negotiable standard. Where a local medical aspirant must clear a demanding Z-score cut-off, an expatriate applicant for that same discipline would need to demonstrate the same order of academic distinction, in their own examination system, before the proposed fee is even relevant. Money is not being asked to substitute for merit, it is being asked to fund a fair fee for a seat that a qualified expatriate child has already earned academically, in a category that sits entirely outside the pool of seats reserved for local students. No underqualified applicant displaces a Sri Lankan student, because no Sri Lankan student is competing for this quota in the first place and no expatriate applicant occupies it without first meeting the same academic yardstick as the local peers this reform is so often, and wrongly, accused of disadvantaging.

For the nation, the gains run deep. Every family that no longer sends its savings to Tbilisi, Dhaka or Yerevan instead spends that money inside Sri Lanka on accommodation, on daily life, on family visits keeping hard earned foreign exchange circulating at home. Every expatriate child educated here grows up with genuine roots in Sri Lanka, not a fading, sentimental attachment to a homeland that once turned them away. These are tomorrow’s doctors, engineers, accountants and entrepreneurs the ones most likely to return, to invest, to employ.

For families like the engineer, the surveyor, the father of two daughters, and the diplomatic mission officer, the gain is simpler and more human: an end to the impossible choice between a parent’s retirement and a child’s education. An end to fathers watching their children grow up strangers to their own homeland.

An end to twenty-five years of hard earned savings vanishing into fees that should never have been so high for a Sri Lankan child in the first place.

The true value of this reform is best understood not in a single admission year, but across a generation. Today, Sri Lanka’s expatriate community represents one of the country’s greatest untapped resources. Many Sri Lankans living abroad have gained international exposure, advanced technical knowledge, professional experience and global networks built over decades of hard work in demanding foreign environments. Their children are growing up in multicultural settings, fluent in more than one system of education, with skills that can directly benefit Sri Lanka in fields such as technology, medicine, engineering, research, entrepreneurship and international business. A student who completes higher education in Sri Lanka, rather than abroad, may eventually become an entrepreneur, an investor, a researcher or a professional contributing directly to national development not from a distance, but from within. A fair admission pathway does more than fill a lecture hall: it encourages more expatriate families to maintain stronger educational and emotional links with Sri Lanka, generation after generation, rather than watching that link quietly fade.

Consider a Sri Lankan child in Doha today, admitted under this new category to study medicine in Colombo instead of Tbilisi. Her family pays roughly US$ 3,750 a year, money that stays inside Sri Lanka, spent on rent, food, transport and family visits in Colombo rather than in Georgia. Over five years, that is a Sri Lankan landlord, a Sri Lankan grocer, and a Sri Lankan transport operator who benefit, instead of their counterparts abroad. She graduates with Sri Lankan classmates, Sri Lankan mentors, and a Sri Lankan medical registration not a foreign one that may never transfer home. Ten years later, there is a real chance she is practising in a Sri Lankan hospital, training the next generation of doctors, rather than being a distant, sentimental well-wisher scattered across another country’s healthcare system.

Multiply that single story by the roughly five hundred expatriate students this reform could realistically bring into Sri Lankan universities each year, and the picture becomes national in scale: a steady, self-funding pipeline of doctors, engineers, accountants and entrepreneurs who grew up abroad but were educated, professionally formed, and rooted at home. Some will return permanently. Others will build careers overseas but keep businesses, investments and family ties anchored in Sri Lanka precisely because they spent their formative university years here, not in Minsk or Yerevan. A country that trains its own diaspora’s children does not just save on tuition outflows; it builds, deliberately and cheaply, the very network of loyal, globally connected citizens that other nations spend decades and fortunes trying to manufacture through diaspora engagement programs. Sri Lanka does not need to invent that network. It already has it, waiting at its own embassies and consulates. This reform simply stops turning it away.

This proposal backed by verified data and seven detailed reform measures has been formally handed over to the Prime Minister, and sent to the Offices of President, Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the Ministry of Education and the Chairman of the University Grants Commission.

Behind every clause of this document stands a real family: an engineer who could only afford to bring one daughter home to study. A surveyor who spent a lifetime of savings so his son could sit in a Sri Lankan lecture hall. A senior officer at diplomatic mission who serves his country by day and grieves, quietly, that his own daughters cannot study in it. A father who gave everything so both his girls could stay.

A country cannot ask its citizens to maintain lifelong connections while unintentionally creating barriers for their children, who holds only Sri Lankan passport. The question is not whether Sri Lanka should support local students or expatriate students. The real question is whether Sri Lanka can recognize both groups as part of the same national family.

A child holding a Sri Lankan passport, born to Sri Lankan parents and connected to Sri Lankan culture should not feel like a foreigner when seeking higher education in Sri Lanka. Correcting this policy gap is not about giving someone an advantage. It is about ensuring fairness. It is about protecting the rights of citizens. And ultimately, it is about strengthening Sri Lanka’s future by bringing its global community closer not pushing it away. The writer is President of the Sri Lankan Business & Professional Council (SLBPC), Qatar, and one of the signatories to the formal proposal submitted to the President and Prime Minister of Sri Lanka on behalf of the Sri Lankan expatriate community.
