Sri Lanka’s corporate debt swells to US $ 86.5bn as IIF flags data gaps

Sri Lanka’s corporate debt climbs to 72% of GDP (US $ 86.5bn), highest among South Asian peers

Estimate leans on domestic loan data, with no foreign currency breakdown available

Government debt data to be updated from next issue, with restructuring about 94% complete

Sri Lanka’s non-financial corporate debt is estimated to have risen to 72.0 percent of GDP or US $ 86.5 billion, by June 2026, the highest among the South Asian economies tracked, with the ratio rising in every quarter since early 2024, as per the data from Washington-based Institute of International Finance (IIF). The ratio has climbed three percentage points from 69.0 percent a year earlier and 6.5 percentage points from 65.5 percent in March 2024. In dollar terms, the estimate has grown 21.1 percent from US $ 71.4 billion in June 2025, close to twice the 11.2 percent increase in the emerging market corporate debt to US $ 42.8 trillion over the same period.

The sharpest move came in the first quarter of 2026, when the dollar figure jumped 12.6 percent from US $ 74.5 billion to US $ 83.8 billion. Sri Lanka’s corporate leverage now sits well above India’s 48.4 percent, Bangladesh’s 30.3 percent and Pakistan’s 10.7 percent and marginally above

Thailand’s 70.3 percent, though it remains below the emerging market average of 89.1 percent and Malaysia’s 88.7 percent.

The build-up contrasts with the household sector, where the debt ratio eased to 9.9 percent, from 11.2 percent a year earlier, leaving corporate debt more than seven times the size of household borrowing relative to output.

Responding to the queries from Mirror Business, IIF Global Markets and Policy Director Emre Tiftik explained that the estimate combines the cross-border bank lending data from the Bank for International Settlements, outstanding bond data from Bloomberg and domestic lending data from the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank.

“Domestic loans account for the majority of corporate debt and are estimated using the IMF Financial Soundness Indicators, supplemented where necessary by historical growth assumptions,” he noted.

“Given the limited availability of comprehensive underlying data, a currency breakdown of Sri Lanka’s non-financial corporate debt is not readily available,” he said.

As a result, the share of the corporate debt stock denominated in foreign currency and therefore exposed to the exchange rate and refinancing pressures, cannot be gauged from the IIF data.

Meanwhile, the IIF confirmed that Sri Lanka’s government debt figures for the quarters after end-2022 were carried forward in error. The database holds government debt at US $ 76.8 billion in every quarter from end-2022 to June 2026, while the report’s country table prints an identical 115.9 percent of GDP for both June 2025 and June 2026.

“The latest government debt figure for Sri Lanka in our database is for end-2022. The figures carried forward thereafter were the result of a data-entry error,” Tiftik stated, adding that Sri Lanka has not been included in the IIF’s aggregate emerging market government debt calculation since 2022 and that regular updates are expected to resume in the next issue as more official data become available.

On the sovereign side, Tiftik stressed that Sri Lanka’s debt restructuring is not yet fully complete, with the agreements covering around 94 percent of external debt within the restructuring perimeter finalised, leaving roughly 6 percent still to be concluded.

“Completing the remaining steps as soon as possible will be important to restore and deepen international market access,” he observed.

While the emerging markets should continue to benefit from favourable global financial conditions as long as US dollar funding conditions remain supportive, he cautioned that the gains would be uneven.

“While market access has improved, private capital is still trickling, not flowing at scale, to many frontier and developing economies,” Tiftik pointed out, adding that stronger domestic capital markets are essential to attract international capital, as they improve market depth, transparency and liquidity.

He also highlighted timely and reliable information as a key constraint for the international investors, noting that Sri Lanka has made meaningful progress through more regular and substantive engagement with the creditors, as reflected in the IIF’s latest investor-relations scores.

Globally, debt rose by more than US $ 10 trillion in the first half of 2026 to US $ 365.5 trillion, with non-financial corporate debt reaching a record US $ 105.1 trillion, up 6.1 percent from a year earlier, as per IIF data.

Across the emerging markets, excluding China, the governments and non-financial corporates accounted for most of the US $ 1.7 trillion rise in debt during the period, which lifted the total to a record US $ 38 trillion.
