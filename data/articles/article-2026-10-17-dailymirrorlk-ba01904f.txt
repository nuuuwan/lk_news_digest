It Is Time to Unite the Sri Lankan  Sańgha

Sri Lankan bhikkhus have continuously performed the noble duty of guiding society towards moral and spiritual well-being

According to the Theravāda commentaries, ancient Sri Lankan society, considered as a whole, was a morally upright society with relatively few forms of misconduct

Unfortunately, however, in recent times, several serious deteriorations have become apparent within the very Sańgha that once brought peace and well-being to Sri Lankan society. A considerable number of young bhikkhus show signs of spiritual deterioration

Is there no lasting solution to the present crises facing the Sri Lankan Sańgha? Is it not possible to unite the entire Sri Lankan Sańgha while safeguarding the independence and distinct identities of all Nikāyas and their respective branches?

This article discusses a structural solution proposed for this purpose. It provides a general introduction to the structure and functions of the three principal institutional pillars of the proposed solution: The Supreme Saṅgha Council (Uttarītara Saṅgha Sabhā), the Saṅgha Affairs Executive Council (Saṅghabhāra Nitthāraka Sabhā), and the Saṅgha Courts (Saṅghādhikaraṇa).

The noble Mahā Saṅgha who have become the spiritual sons of the Buddha, and are worthy of veneration and offerings even from kings and ministers, are indeed a blessing to the entire world. A bhikkhu who neither torments himself nor torments others (nevattantapanaparantapa) is one who is inclined to promote the welfare of others, not only physically and verbally but also mentally. Through a bhikkhu who is devoted to helping all living beings without discrimination, with thoughts, words, and deeds guided by spiritual friendship, the flames of hatred within society are extinguished, while the coolness of harmony among different communities is fostered.

Thus, an entire society is brought towards peace and wellbeing. According to the Theravāda commentaries, ancient Sri Lankan society, considered as a whole, was a morally upright society with relatively few forms of misconduct. The fundamental foundation for this was laid by  Sri Lankan bhikkhus. Following the arrival of Mahinda Mahā Thera, as Buddhism spread rapidly throughout Sri Lanka, many sons of respectable families renounced household life and entered the Saṅgha. By properly fulfilling their duties as renunciants, they contributed to the moral and spiritual well-being of Sri Lankan society, whose members supported them with the four requisites.

Sri Lankan bhikkhus have continuously performed the noble duty of guiding society towards moral and spiritual well-being. Unfortunately, however, in recent times, several serious deteriorations have become apparent within the very Saṅgha that once brought peace and well-being to Sri Lankan society. A considerable number of young bhikkhus show signs of spiritual deterioration. Apart from a relatively small number of bhikkhus who correctly propagate the Dhamma through the internet, those engaged in academic Buddhist studies at university level, and few others devoted to studying the Dhamma and Vinaya, it is evident to discerning observers that bhikkhus, in general, are moving away from the profound study of the Dhamma and Vinaya.

Consequently, there is now a noticeable scarcity of learned, competent, and capable bhikkhus possessing profound knowledge of the Dhamma and Buddhist scholarship, such as those who  flourished in Sri Lankan society from ancient times until a few decades ago and played a leading role in guiding society along the proper path.

Due to the deterioration of the essential qualities of the teacher-pupil relationship expected by the Tathāgata, fundamental monastic training programmes capable of producing bhikkhus qualified to become independent of their preceptors and teachers (nissayamttaka) have become exceedingly rare. Even the existing basic Pariveṇas do not appear to fulfil the educational and spiritual requirements of the Sāsana adequately. These deteriorations within the structure of the Saṅgha have given rise to several serious crises. The number of bhikkhus who preach, write, and propagate teachings fundamentally contrary to Theravāda Buddhism is increasing. Spiritual deterioration and deficiencies in fundamental monastic training have resulted in some bhikkhus openly engaging, without hiri (moral shame) and ottappa (moral dread), in activities entirely incompatible with the life of a renunciant.

Such conduct deeply distresses devoted Buddhists. Statements such as “There is no rebirth” and “There is no merit or demerit that can give results in the future”, which are entirely contrary to fundamental Buddhist teachings, are now publicly made by certain individuals wearing the Buddhist robe. Consequently, increasing numbers of young people are developing fundamentally mistaken understandings of Buddhism. The number of lay Buddhists who question whether the Saṅgha continues to serve a useful purpose in society is also increasing. Even sincere lay devotees who earnestly seek the path to Nibbāna find it exceedingly difficult to distinguish the true Dhamma from erroneous teachings.

According to statistics maintained by the Department of Buddhist Affairs, the number of bhikkhus disrobing annually is increasing at an alarming rate. In this unfortunate context, the very identity of the Sri Lankan Buddha Sāsana is facing a grave danger. The crisis appears to be intensifying day by day, without even the slightest indication of a lasting solution in sight. It is also evident that almost all previous attempts to introduce permanent solutions to these problems have failed due to legal or other practical difficulties.

Unity of the Saṅgha Lasting solutions to the burning crises facing the Sri Lankan Saṅgha can be achieved only when all the Buddhist Nikāyas and their respective branches in Sri Lanka unite and function within a common institutional framework. Such a framework must safeguard the independence and distinct identities of the respective Nikāyas and branches (Pārśva) while being founded upon the Dhamma and Vinaya taught by the Tathāgata and the common Saṅgha regulations (katikāvata) agreed upon by the united Saṅgha. The principal obstacle to finding lasting solutions to the serious crises described above is the absence of a common institutional framework within which the various Nikāyas and their branches in Sri Lanka can function collectively. In a context where thirty-one independent branches exist under three principal Nikāyas, collective action cannot reasonably be expected without a previously established common agreement.

Therefore, the time has now come to undertake the great responsibility of uniting the Sri Lankan Saṅgha. The harmony of the Saṅgha, namely, living and functioning together with common moral discipline and understanding within the same framework of Dhamma and Vinaya, is a noble condition conducive to the happiness not only of human beings but also of divine beings. It was for this reason that the Tathāgata declared in the Dhammapada, “Sukhā saṅghassa sāmaggī” – “Blissful is the harmony of the Saṅgha.” The harmony of the Saṅgha not only brings peace and comfort to the hearts of human beings but is also an auspicious condition for the continued flourishing of the noble Saddhamma of the Tathāgata throughout the world. The unity of the Saṅgha proposed by the Sri Lanka Sāmaggi Mahā Saṅgha Council Proposing Committee (Śrī Laṅkā Sāmaggi Mahā Saṅgha Sabhā Yojaka Maṇḍalaya) does not mean amalgamating all Nikāyas and their branches into a single Nikāya or branch. Rather, it means preserving the distinct identities of the respective Nikāyas and branches while uniting them to act collectively on matters concerning the Dhamma and Vinaya of the Tathāgata and the common Saṅgha regulations accepted by all.

The proposal is not to merge the Saṅgha into a single Nikāya, but to unite the Saṅgha within a common institutional framework. The judgment delivered by the Sri Lanka Supreme Court concerning the issuance of identity cards to Bhikkhunīs also highlights important considerations regarding the independence of the various Nikāyas and branches in Sri Lanka and the authority vested in their respective Mahānāyaka Theras to make decisions on matters concerning the Sāsana. When a Mahānāyaka Thera of one Nikāya or branch makes a decision concerning a particular matter of the Sāsana, the Mahānāyaka Theras of other Nikāyas and branches may disagree with that decision. In the absence of a common institutional framework accepted by all Nikāyas and branches, however, there is no collective mechanism through which such disagreements can be resolved. This constitutes a serious problem.

Disagreements concerning the interpretation of the Dhamma and Vinaya may also arise among different Nikāyas and branches in the future. In such circumstances, it is exceedingly important to have a common institutional mechanism through which all Mahānāyaka Theras, together with learned and competent bhikkhus representing their respective Nikāyas and branches, can collectively deliberate and arrive at common agreements. The disagreements concerning the Bhikkhunī Sāsana may also be regarded as an instance highlighting the necessity for such a common mechanism.

Accordingly, not only are there thirty-one independent branches within the Sri Lankan Saṅgha, but there are also thirty-one independent ecclesiastical authorities capable of making different decisions concerning matters of the Sāsana in the absence of a common agreement. This presents a serious practical difficulty for the coordinated functioning of the Sri Lankan Buddha Sāsana. 4 How unfortunate is this situation? Is there no remedy? The only effective and lasting solution is to establish a Supreme Saṅgha Council (Uttarītara Saṅgha Sabhā), uniting all Buddhist Nikāyas and their respective branches in Sri Lanka, and thereby systematically organising the affairs of the Sāsana.

The Supreme Saṅgha Council of Sri Lanka – Uttarītara Saṅgha Sabhā In order to provide lasting solutions to the serious crises that have arisen at present and those that may arise in the future, the establishment of a Supreme Saṅgha Council, uniting the Sri Lankan Saṅgha on the basis of the Dhamma, Vinaya, and common Saṅgha regulations, is one of the most urgent requirements of the Sāsana in our time. Before the Parinibbāna of the Tathāgata, certain great responsibilities concerning the future of the Sāsana were entrusted to the Saṅgha.

Following His Parinibbāna, among the hundreds of thousands of bhikkhus who had gathered at Kusinārā, Mahākassapa Mahā Thera, with the approval of the Saṅgha, selected five hundred bhikkhus, comprising four hundred and ninety nine great Arahants accomplished in the analytical knowledges (paṭisambhidā) and well-versed in the Tipiṭaka, together with Ānanda Mahā Thera, the Treasurer of the Dhamma (Dhammabhaṇḍāgārika).

Subsequently, these five hundred Theras assembled at the Sattapaṇṇī Cave in Rājagaha and made decisions affecting the entire Buddha Sāsana. Thereafter, whenever serious crises arose within the Sāsana, learned and accomplished Mahā Theras assembled in groups of five hundred or more and made decisions concerning the entire Sāsana, thereby resolving the crises that had arisen. Following the path adopted by those ancient elders of the Saṅgha, it is possible even today to find solutions to common problems of the Sāsana by uniting the Saṅgha in Sri Lanka.

The proposed Supreme Saṅgha Council, established in accordance with the example set by the ancient Mahā Theras, will consist of six hundred learned and competent bhikkhus, including all Mahānāyaka Theras, Anunāyaka Theras, and Secretary Theras of the respective Nikāyas and branches. This Council will be responsible for determining the authoritative interpretation of the Dhamma and Vinaya of the Tathāgata and adopting common Saṅgha regulations applicable to the Sri Lankan Saṅgha.

Its principal objective will be to maintain doctrinal and practical unity within the Sri Lankan Saṅgha in matters concerning the study and preservation of the Dhamma (pariyatti) and its practice (paṭipatti). In order to promote the flourishing of the Pariyatti and Paṭipatti Sāsana among the bhikkhus of Sri Lanka, the Supreme Saṅgha Council must establish a Saṅgha Affairs Executive Council (Saṅghabhāra Nitthāraka Sabhā).

The mere adoption of decisions by the united Saṅgha, based on the Dhamma and Vinaya of the Tathāgata and common Saṅgha regulations, will not in itself resolve the crises facing the five Sāsana. It is essential to establish an institutional mechanism capable of translating those decisions into practical reality. A long-term programme must be implemented to develop the spiritual progress, education, administrative and leadership skills of bhikkhus, regulatory procedures concerning public Dhamma sermons and publications, and programmes for the preservation of the Dhamma. For this purpose, the Saṅgha Affairs Executive Council must be established under the Supreme Saṅgha Council. This Executive Council will function under the leadership of an Anunāyaka Thera appointed for a period of four years by the Supreme Mahānāyaka Council. It will comprise several boards and committees, including the Pariyatti and Paṭipatti Sāsana Board, the Healthcare and Nursing Board, the Sāsana Publications and Literary Board, the Monastery Development Board, the Sāsana Funds Board, the Dhammadūta and Sāsana Relations Board, the Sāmaṇera Welfare and Protection Board, the Sīlamātā Welfare and Protection Board, and the Vinaya Executive Committee.

Through this institutional structure, the Saṅgha Affairs Executive Council will be responsible for implementing programmes necessary for the spiritual development of bhikkhus and improving Pariveṇa education. Among the principal initiatives envisaged are the establishment of advanced Pariveṇas capable of conferring monastic qualifications such as Nissayamuttaka; the establishment of a Buddhist College of Law to train qualified judicial Theras capable of delivering ecclesiastical judgments that may command respect even in civil courts; and the establishment of a College of Saṅgha Administration to produce competent bhikkhus equipped with leadership qualities appropriate to a changing society.

Such bhikkhus should be capable of understanding the attitudes and expectations of society and maintaining environmentally friendly and attractive monasteries while preserving the fundamental ideals of monastic life. Other important initiatives include the establishment of a Tamil Buddhist Faculty and a Sāsana Text Preservation Board.

Through these and other activities of great benefit to the Sāsana, it is expected that lasting solutions can be provided to the fundamental causes of the present crises. The long-term survival and flourishing of the Sri Lankan Saṅgha cannot be ensured merely by applying temporary remedies to the crises that exist today. For this purpose, institutional mechanisms must be developed to provide young bhikkhus, in particular, with training of the highest quality, enriching them with knowledge of the Dhamma and Vinaya and nurturing their spiritual qualities.

We must always bear in mind that the future of the Buddha Sāsana depends upon the scholarly and spiritual qualities of the generations of bhikkhus yet to come. The Supreme Saṅgha Council will unite the entire Sri Lankan Saṅgha under the Dhamma and Vinaya of the Tathāgata and common Saṅgha regulations, while the Saṅgha Affairs Executive Council will undertake the necessary measures to promote the flourishing of the Pariyatti and Paṭipatti Sāsana.

Nevertheless, these mechanisms alone will not be sufficient to resolve every crisis of the Sāsana. In particular, a common judicial mechanism of the united Saṅgha is essential to bring bhikkhus engaged in conduct contrary to the Vinaya back into proper discipline and to make decisions in accordance with the Dhamma and Vinaya concerning serious disciplinary offences.

The Saṅgha Courts (Saṅghādhikaraṇa) must always function in accordance with the seven methods for settling disputes (adhikaraṇa samatha) laid down by the Tathāgata, the Dhamma and Vinaya, and fundamental principles of law. It is expected that Saṅgha Courts comprising qualified judicial Theras trained at the proposed Buddhist College of Law will be capable of guiding bhikkhus who engage in activities contrary to the Vinaya back towards proper monastic discipline. Matters falling within the jurisdiction of the internal ecclesiastical courts of the respective Nikāyas and branches must continue to be resolved within those Nikāyas and branches themselves.

The newly established Saṅgha Courts will not interfere with such internal judicial processes. Nevertheless, a common judicial mechanism is essential for resolving disputes arising between bhikkhus belonging to different Nikāyas and branches, as well as disputes concerning monasteries that involve more than one Nikāya or branch. For this purpose, the establishment of Primary Saṅgha Courts (Mūlika Saṅghādhikaraṇa) is proposed.

Furthermore, it is proposed to establish a common Saṅgha Judicial Council (Kāraka Saṅghādhikaraṇa), empowered to make decisions in accordance with the Dhamma and Vinaya concerning bhikkhus who engage in serious disciplinary misconduct and cannot be brought into proper discipline even after appropriate efforts have been made to establish them in future restraint (āyati saṃvara). The misconduct of certain bhikkhus brings disrepute not merely to the particular Nikāya or branch to which they belong, but to the entire Saṅgha. At present, there is no common procedure binding upon all Nikāyas and branches concerning the admission of a bhikkhu who has been expelled or removed from one Nikāya or branch into another. Consequently, it is essential to establish a common agreement and a Vinaya-compliant mechanism through which decisions made upon a monk of a certain Nikāya or branch concerning serious disciplinary misconduct may be recognised and respected by the other Nikāyas and branches. The Kāraka Saṅghādhikaraṇa serves this purpose. Furthermore, the proposed Saṅgha Judicial Council may perform the Pattanikkujjana Kamma, in accordance with the Vinaya, in relation to lay Buddhists who unjustifiably and maliciously insult or disparage the Buddha Sāsana and the bhikkhus.

By means of this procedure, as exemplified by the disciplinary action authorised by the Tathāgata against Vaḍḍha the Licchavi, the Saṅgha may formally withdraw its customary  religious association with those who engage in acts harmful to the Sāsana and their houses. Such measures are expected to minimise the harm caused to the Sāsana by malicious conduct. Finally, a Supreme Saṅgha Court (Uttarītara Saṅghādhikaraṇa) must also be established to provide opportunities for appeals against judgments and decisions delivered by the Saṅgha Courts. Through this mechanism, it is expected that the fundamental Vinaya rights of bhikkhus will be safeguarded within the Sāsana.

There has never been a more appropriate time to establish such a united Saṅgha mechanism capable of providing both short-term and long-term solutions to the crises facing the Sāsana. Indeed, we have already delayed this undertaking for far too long. Further delay will only allow the existing crises to intensify until they reach a stage at which effective remedies become exceedingly difficult to implement. Therefore, the time has now come to undertake, without further delay, the great responsibility of uniting the Sri Lankan Saṅgha. We should always bear in mind the noble words of the Tathāgata: “Yena me idaṃ kalyāṇaṃ vattaṃ nihitaṃ anuppavatteyyātha, mā kho me tumhe antimapurisā ahuvattha.’’

“You should continue this noble tradition established by me. Do not be the last men in my dispensation.” Bearing these noble words of the Buddha in mind, we must act with unwavering determination so that we do not become the last generation in the dispensation of Gotama Sammāsambuddha, but instead safely transmit the noble heritage of the Sāsana entrusted to us by the ancient elders of the Saṅgha to future generations.

For this purpose, we emphasise that it is the supreme responsibility of all senior (Thera), middle-ranking (Majjhima), and junior (Navaka) bhikkhus who are devoted to the welfare of the Sāsana, to work with dedication and commitment towards making the proposed united Saṅgha mechanism a reality, while safeguarding the independence and distinct identities of all Nikāyas and their respective branches.

(Watagoda Maggavihari Thera, MA (Buddha Dhamma) is Secretary, Sri Lanka Sāmaggi Mahā Saṅgha Council and Proposing Committee Vice President, International Institute of Theravāda (IIT), Sri Lanka).
