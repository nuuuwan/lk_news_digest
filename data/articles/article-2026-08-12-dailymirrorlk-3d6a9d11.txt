Tourist arrivals slip by 8% in early August

Kandy Esala Perahera has traditionally pulled a wave of visitors into the back half of August

Esala Perahera’s impact to offset the broader headwinds remains to be seen

Reaching 2.5 million would require roughly 1.11 million arrivals over the remaining 147 days of the year

Sri Lanka’s tourist arrivals opened August on a weaker note than a year earlier, with 42,810 visitors recorded between August 1 and 6, down 8 percent from the 46,541 arrivals over the same six days last year, according to data from the Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority (SLTDA).

Average daily arrivals fell to 7,135 from 7,757 a year earlier, extending a slowdown that has left cumulative arrivals down around 2 percent year-on-year since the start of 2026, at 1,386,228 against 1,414,829 in the same window last year.

The soft opening also predates Sri Lanka’s biggest seasonal draw of the month which is the Kandy Esala Perahera, scheduled this year for August 18 to 27, with the Kumbal Perahera running August 18-22 and the grand Randoli Perahera August 23-27. The festival has traditionally pulled a wave of visitors into the back half of August, so the early-month numbers do not yet reflect its usual lift. Whether that pull is strong enough this year to offset the broader headwinds remains to be seen.

Much of the slippage traces back to the Middle East conflict that escalated from late February, when a US-Israeli military campaign against Iran triggered months of airspace closures across the Gulf’s major transit hubs. Airlines rerouting around the conflict zone, via Saudi Arabia or longer detours through Central Asia or around the Cape of Good Hope, have added up to three hours to some journeys, pushing up fuel burn, insurance costs and, in turn, ticket fares on long-haul routes into Sri Lanka. With roughly a third of Sri Lanka’s inbound traffic historically routed through Dubai, Doha, Abu Dhabi and other Gulf airports, the disruption hit hard: the combined share of arrivals passing through the four major Gulf hubs fell from close to a third in June 2025 to just 21.3 percent in June 2026. The effect showed up directly in the monthly trend, with arrivals swinging from 16.2 percent growth in February to declines of 19.7 percent in March and 22.3 percent in April, before a partial recovery in May and renewed softness in June and July.

The disruption was cited by Tourism Deputy Minister Prof. Ruwan Ranasinghe as the direct reason for the government’s decision to cut its 2026 arrivals target to 2.5 million from 3 million, alongside a reduction in the revenue goal to US$ 3.5 billion from US$ 4 billion, after airspace closures across the Middle East eroded the momentum built up during a record January and February, he said, adding that he still hoped 2026 would close as a record year for arrivals.

Even the revised target now looks stretched. Reaching 2.5 million would require roughly 1.11 million arrivals over the remaining 147 days of the year, an average of about 7,577 tourists a day, well above the year-to-date daily average of 6,359. September to December is typically Sri Lanka’s strongest stretch, with November and December alone averaging above 197,000 and 253,000 arrivals respectively over the past three years as the peak winter season sets in. Applying last year’s actual monthly volumes for that period, arrivals between August 7 and December 31 would come to around 947,700, putting full-year 2026 arrivals at close to 2.33 million, about 166,000 short of the revised target. Closing that gap would need close to 18 percent growth over the final five months versus the same stretch last year, a big ask given that the projected seasonal uplift itself assumes Gulf connectivity normalises. That is far from guaranteed. As of early August, the EU’s aviation safety agency has extended its conflict-zone advisory over the Gulf through August 31, overflights through Kuwaiti airspace remain prohibited, and fresh attacks near the Strait of Hormuz, including a Houthi strike on a Saudi Aramco refinery on August 9, underline that the standoff remains unresolved heading into peak season, even as diplomatic efforts between Iran, Oman and the US continue.

The pattern is visible in how individual markets have performed. India, which reaches Sri Lanka largely on direct or short-haul routes rather than through Gulf hubs, has strengthened its lead, its share of arrivals rising from around 21 percent in the January–August 2025 period to nearly 25 percent this year. China has also gained, overtaking Russia to become the third-largest source market, while Australia, Japan, the Maldives and Israel posted smaller share gains. By contrast, markets more reliant on Gulf connections for onward travel, including Germany, France, the Netherlands, Italy, Spain and Poland, have all lost share, consistent with the squeeze on long-haul European routings through Dubai and Doha.

Russia’s decline stands out most. Once Sri Lanka’s third-largest source market, its share of cumulative arrivals slipped from about 7.6 percent in the comparable period last year to 5.75 percent this year, with a meaningful share of its traffic historically routed via Dubai and Sharjah, corridors also caught up in the Gulf disruption. The softness persists despite renewed engagement with Russia’s travel trade, including talks on direct flights and simplified visas at Moscow’s “Let’s Travel” forum in June, and separate assurances from tourism officials on the resumption of charter capacity. So far, the arrivals data shows no rebound in Russian or wider CIS numbers, suggesting the Gulf disruption has compounded, rather than eased, the charter-flight volatility that has weighed on the market since last year.
