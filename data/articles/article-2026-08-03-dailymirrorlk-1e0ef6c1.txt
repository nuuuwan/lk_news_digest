Prisons of neglect!

If those in power continue to look away, the next riot will not be a surprise

Sri Lanka’s prisons are not failing quietly. They are collapsing in full view of the State.

And every time the system breaks, lives are lost. From Welikada to Mahara to Negombo, the pattern is painfully clear. The 2012 Welikada prison riot should have been a national wake up call. Twenty seven inmates were killed in a single incident that raised serious questions about command responsibility and use of force. A decade later, accountability came slowly and selectively, but the deeper issues remained untouched.

Go back further to 1983 and Welikada again became a symbol of failure when detainees were massacred inside a high security facility. That tragedy was not just about ethnic violence. It exposed how easily control can be lost inside prison walls.

In 2020, Mahara prison told the same story in a different form. Overcrowding and fear of a COVID outbreak led to unrest. The response was live ammunition. Eleven inmates were killed. More than 100 were injured. Even then, the warning signs were clear. Prisons were overcrowded, under prepared and dangerously volatile. The recent Negombo riot there left at least 26 dead, including prison officers. More than 100 were injured. The violence was linked to gang rivalries and drug networks operating inside the prison. But the deeper cause was far more obvious. Negombo prison was holding nearly 2,400 inmates in a facility built for around 650.

Responsibility cannot be pushed aside any longer. The Ministry of Justice, the Department of Prisons and successive governments must answer for the state of these institutions. Year after year, budget allocations for prison infrastructure and rehabilitation remain minimal compared to the growing prison population.

Funds are found for highways, for large scale development projects and for political priorities. But prisons remain at the bottom of the list. The Department of Prisons has repeatedly flagged capacity issues. Officials within the system have warned about staff shortages and rising inmate numbers. Yet these warnings have not translated into meaningful investment or policy change. Instead, the State continues to rely heavily on incarceration as the default response, particularly for drug related offences. Thousands of individuals are sent into a system that clearly does not have the space or the structure to manage them.

Drug networks continue to operate with alarming ease. Organised gangs control sections of prisons. Those with money and connections gain protection. In Negombo, reports indicate that inmates suspected of cooperating with authorities were targeted during the violence.

And when riots break out, it is not the powerful who suffer most. It is the ordinary inmates. First time offenders. Men serving short sentences and individuals who entered prison for minor crimes. Prison officers, too, are casualties of this failure. Understaffed and often under equipped, they are expected to manage thousands of inmates in high tension environments. In Negombo, several officers lost their lives trying to contain the unrest.

Sri Lanka has not adapted its legal framework to match reality. If prisons cannot cope with the numbers, then the answer cannot be to keep sending more people into them. Yet that is exactly what continues to happen. There are clear alternatives. House arrest for non violent offenders is one. With proper monitoring, it can reduce overcrowding without compromising accountability. Detention centres for minor offences can prevent low risk inmates from being exposed to hardened criminals. Rehabilitation centres, particularly for drug related offences, can address addiction instead of feeding criminal networks.

These are not new ideas. The Ministry of Justice must move beyond statements and begin structural reform. The Department of Prisons must be given the resources it needs, not just to contain inmates but to manage them safely. Parliament must take responsibility for updating outdated laws.

Without this, the outcome is predictable. Welikada, Mahara and Negombo are not isolated incidents. They are warnings that have been ignored for decades. A prison system is a reflection of the State itself. Right now, that reflection is one of neglect, delay and misplaced priorities.

If those in power continue to look away, the next riot will not be a surprise.
