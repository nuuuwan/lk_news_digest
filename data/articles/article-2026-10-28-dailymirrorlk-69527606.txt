Sri Lanka’s indirect taxes are among the highest in the world

Colombo, Oct. 28 (Daily Mirror) - According to latest official data the Sri Lankan economy grew by 4.7 percent during the first half of 2026. Although many analysts hail this as an indication of a stable economic recovery, some argue that economic recovery is hardly felt by ordinary citizens. A recent survey shows that only 25 percent of households in the country earn enough to cover their living expenses, suggesting that the cost of living has reached a high level amid inflation reaching above 8 percent. Hence there appears to be a clear disconnect between the official economic growth numbers and what ordinary citizens experience.

When we analyze the first half of the 2026 GDP data (as shown below), we observe that the economy has expanded by an overall 11.2 percent in nominal terms. However, the increase in real economic activities or Gross Value Addition (“GVA”) was a much lower 8.6 percent. In between the growth of GDP and GVA was a massive 34.6 percent increase in (net) consumption taxes.

As seen above, consumption taxes amount to 12 percent of GDP. This means that the Government takes (on average) a 12 rupee cut on every 100 rupee worth of economic goods (production of goods and services). This cut is in addition to income tax individuals and businesses pay (which are not accounted in the GDP). This tax directly impacts the cost of everything that is sold in the country resulting in the slowing down of consumer demand and pushing up prices. Therefore, we can clearly argue that the government fiscal policy is the main reason behind the widening gap between GDP Growth on paper and what people experience in reality.

Indirect or consumption taxes totaling 12 percent amid slower expansion of GVA points to a major “economic shock” rather than an “economic transformation”. This “shock” is clearly evident when we compare GVA and GDP trends over a longer period of time.

As shown above, the gap between GDP growth and GVA growth have expanded sharply since 2023 driven by contractionary fiscal policies. As seen above, it is possible to have a negative gap suggesting expansionary fiscal policies that prevailed during such periods.

Short and medium term outcome of high indirect taxes or contractionary fiscal policies include rise in prices (inflation) and decline in demand (economic activity). Prolonged high indirect tax regimes could lead to economic stagflation leading to deterioration of export competitiveness. Sri Lanka is already seeing signs of higher inflation together with expected slowdown in overall GDP growth during the next two years. But the worst impact is going to be the gradual shrinking of the Middle Class and increase in poverty.
