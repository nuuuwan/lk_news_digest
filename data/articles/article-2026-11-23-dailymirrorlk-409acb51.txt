Sri Lanka must learn to save its rain

Sri Lanka has never been short of rain. What it has repeatedly lacked is the foresight to save enough of it for everyone.

Every year, the monsoons arrive, rivers swell and reservoirs fill. In some areas, rain falls in torrents, causing floods and sending water rushing towards the sea. Yet, when the dry months arrive, families in several districts queue for water, farmers watch crops fail and authorities send bowsers to communities whose wells have run dry. This should shame a country whose agriculture and civilisation have depended on water for centuries.

Water is fast becoming one of the world’s most critical resources. Climate change is making rainfall less predictable, droughts more severe and competition for freshwater more intense. Population growth is adding pressure. For Sri Lanka, this is not a distant warning. The question is no longer whether Sri Lanka should invest in rainwater harvesting. It is why the country has failed to make it a national priority.

More than half of Sri Lanka’s annual rainfall ultimately flows into the sea without productive use, according to the country’s climate planning documents. Not every drop should be captured; rivers and ecosystems need water. But allowing so much seasonal rainfall to pass through the country without making better use of it, while other communities face shortages, is a failure of planning.

Sri Lanka has had policies on paper for years. A National Rainwater Harvesting Policy and Strategies was adopted in 2005, while later building regulations require harvesting facilities in many new developments within designated urban areas. Community initiatives also exist. In May this year, the Government announced a pilot scheme  to install systems in at least 100 state institutions in Colombo, initially for non-drinking uses.

These are steps in the right direction. But a pilot, a policy document and scattered initiatives do not amount to a comprehensive national programme. The country needs a plan with targets, funding, responsibility and public reporting. It must reach beyond Colombo and state buildings.

Districts that repeatedly experience drought must be at the centre of that plan. Northern, North Central, North Western, Eastern and Uva communities know what it means to live with uncertain rainfall and depleted wells. In September, drought had affected more than 169,000 people across 10 districts, with water bowsers supplying large numbers of households. Rainwater harvesting can be expanded at several levels. Homes, schools, hospitals, offices, factories and apartments should collect roof water in properly designed tanks. Public buildings could use this water for cleaning, sanitation and gardening. In rural areas, household tanks and community storage can provide a reserve through dry periods.

Agriculture must be part of the same strategy. Small village reservoirs, restored tanks, farm ponds, check dams and better catchment management can retain water and recharge groundwater. Existing tanks and irrigation networks must be maintained. Farmers need support to adopt water-efficient irrigation and plan cultivation around reliable water availability. Harvested rainwater can supplement irrigation, protect crops during dry spells and reduce dependence on emergency deliveries.

Collecting water is only part of the task. Storage must be safe and suited to local conditions. Water intended for drinking must be tested and treated appropriately. Authorities must map rainfall, identify suitable sites and protect downstream communities and ecosystems. The expertise exists. What is missing is the political commitment to coordinate it.

Responsibility for water is spread across multiple institutions. This cannot continue when water security affects food, health, livelihoods and economic stability. A national water security plan should bring agencies together, publish district-level targets and report annually on how much water is captured, stored and made available. Funding must be allocated according to vulnerability, not political visibility.

Sri Lanka cannot control the monsoon or climate patterns. It can control whether it prepares for the dry months. It can repair neglected tanks, protect catchments, build storage, enforce existing rules and make rainwater harvesting a normal feature of homes and public infrastructure.

If Sri Lanka one day finds itself running short of water, it will not be because the island was never blessed with rain. The rain will continue to fall. The question is whether the country will finally have the sense to save it.
