Sri Lanka tops IMF’s Asia comparison with 43% graduate youth unemployment

Some 43% of Sri Lankans aged 15 to 24 with postsecondary education are unemployed, the highest in IMF’s regional comparison

Sri Lanka’s figure stands seven points above B’desh and more than three times Thailand’s 13.2%

More than four in ten young Sri Lankans with postsecondary education are out of work, placing the country at the top of a regional comparison of 12 Asia-Pacific economies with an unemployment rate of 43 percent among its most educated youth, according to International Monetary Fund data.

The data, presented at the IMF’s regional outlook briefing for Asia and the Pacific on April 16 and reproduced in the September edition of the Fund’s Finance & Development magazine, show Sri Lanka at the sharpest end of a regional pattern in which joblessness rises rather than falls with educational attainment.

Sri Lanka’s rate sits seven percentage points above Bangladesh, which at about 36 percent marks the lower end of the South Asian range, and is more than three times the 13.2 percent recorded among Thai youth with postsecondary education. Thailand’s overall youth unemployment rate stood at just 3.9 percent last year, leaving educated young Thais roughly three times as likely to be jobless as their peers, a gap the magazine noted is repeated across most of Southeast Asia but is considerably wider in South Asia.

Measured against local data, the IMF figure is more than double the 19.5 percent unemployment rate among all Sri Lankans aged 15 to 24 in 2025 and around 11 times the national rate of 3.9 percent, as per Department of Census and Statistics data. The rate among those educated to GCE Advanced Level and above, across all age groups, declined to 6.0 percent from 7.8 percent in 2024, suggesting that the burden of joblessness falls most heavily on the youngest and most qualified entrants to the labour market.

IMF Asia and Pacific Department Director Krishna Srinivasan, addressing the April briefing, observed that “highly educated young workers are struggling to find positions that match their qualifications.” Artificial intelligence, he cautioned, could lift growth and productivity but may also intensify pressure on young workers if adoption runs ahead of skills development, strengthening the case for universities and technical programmes to respond more closely to employer demand.

Meanwhile, the World Bank estimated in June that around 1.2 billion people will join the labour force in developing economies over the next decade, likely the largest youth cohort ever recorded, entering an economy in which global growth is roughly a third lower than around the turn of the century.
