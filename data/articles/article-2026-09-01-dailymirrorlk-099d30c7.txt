Japan, pacifist since 1947, is now vigorously militarising

Rising threats from China and President Donald Trump’s reluctance to be the sole guarantor of security have pushed Japan towards militarisation

Much of Japan’s defence spending so far has gone on missiles capable of striking targets more than 1,000 km away

Japan was sworn to pacifism after the devastating impact that World War II had on it. Its 1947 constitution had Article 9, through which it explicitly abjured war. It gave up its sovereign right to wage war and also to maintain war potential. Post-war Japan handed over responsibility for its defence to the United States, the occupying power turned ally.

But there was no remorse because Japan’s rather unflattering image, through the 1930s till 1945 as an expansionist power, had been shed. Freed from the burden of building its defences, post-war Japan spent the money and time thus saved to build itself up as a technological and trading world power with single-minded devotion.

However, this is becoming history now. Japan has begun to arm itself vigorously and enter defence alliances in response to mounting threats from China and North Korea, coupled with US President Donald Trump’s reluctance to be the guarantor of security to America’s allies.

Realisation had dawned as early as 1954, after the Korean War in which China and the United Nations, led by the US, clashed. It was at that time that Japan created the “Self-Defence Force” (SDF). But still, the SDF was not meant to be deployed in offensive actions. Much later, in the 1990s, Japanese troops got involved in UN peacekeeping work.

However, the major change came under Prime Minister Shinzo Abe (in power from 2005 to 2006 and again from 2012 to 2016). In

2014–2015, Abe re-interpreted Article 9 of the Constitution to allow “limited collective self-defence” (such as aiding an ally under attack if Japan’s survival is also threatened). Abe passed security laws expanding the SDF’s role and called it “proactive pacifism”.

A key reason for Japan’s re-armament was the rising threat from China, which was aggressively claiming territories and building up a gigantic military. North Korea also posed a threat, armed as it was with missiles and nuclear weapons. Abe also desired that Japan should contribute to regional/international security rather than remain

There is another reason, which is unstated, and that is US President Donald Trump’s demand that allies should not expect the US to finance and provide defence 100%. He has asked at least his European allies to raise their defence expenditure to 5% of their GDP. Japan, a strong US ally, has hiked up its defence expenditure appreciably. Its estimated defence expenditure is US$ 63.5 billion, which will be about 1.9% of its GDP.

Higher defence spending, counterstrike capabilities, and manufacturing arms for export are among the steps envisaged. But the pacifist Article 9 of the constitution remains.

In an interesting development, in August 2026, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi portrayed Japan’s accelerating military buildup not only as a means to protect the country, but as a route

The latest defence White Paper portrays arms production as a support to economic growth, Reuters reported.  The White Paper called for harnessing technology, funding startups and using more private sector-made components in weapons production.

Takaichi’s new security strategy is expected to be primarily directed at deterring China. “China’s military activities and other actions are a matter of serious concern to Japan and the international community, and represent Japan’s greatest strategic challenge,” the White Paper said.

Ties between Japan and China sank to their lowest level in years, after Takaichi said in November 2025 that Japan could deploy its Self-Defence Forces if any Chinese attack on Taiwan also threatened Japan’s survival.

Much of Japan’s defence spending so far has gone on missiles capable of striking targets more than 1,000 km away. A significant share of any further budget increase is expected to go toward drones and other unmanned weapons of the kind deployed extensively by Ukraine in its war with Russia.

According to “Defence Security Asia, ” Japan has crossed one of the most politically sensitive and strategically consequential thresholds in its post-war defence posture by deploying its first domestically developed long-range stand-off missiles to frontline units.

The move places Japan within a rapidly expanding group of Indo-Pacific powers capable of striking hostile naval forces, missile bases and invasion corridors far beyond their own coastlines.

The Japan Ground Self-Defence Force formally redesignated its upgraded Type-12 missile as the Type-25 Surface-to-Ship Guided Missile, or 25SSM, while its island-defence hypersonic weapon was renamed the Type-25 Hyper Velocity Gliding Projectile, or 25HGP.

Together, the two systems represent the first fully domestically developed Japanese stand-off missiles to enter frontline service, giving Japan a new ability to threaten hostile warships, invasion fleets and selected land targets from distances previously unavailable to Japanese forces, Defence Security Asia said.

The emergence of the 25SSM and 25HGP indicates that Japan now believes deterrence can no longer rely solely upon interception, but must also include the credible ability to strike enemy forces before they can sustain an attack.

Therefore, for the first time since the end of the Second World War, Japan is constructing an indigenous counterstrike architecture capable of influencing the military balance across the East China Sea, Taiwan Strait and wider western Pacific.

The Type-25 Surface-to-Ship Missile entered operational service with the 5th Surface-to-Ship Missile Regiment at Camp Kengun in Kumamoto Prefecture on Kyushu, placing Japan’s new missile capability directly opposite the East China Sea and Taiwan approaches, Defence Security Asia said.

Developed by Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, the 25SSM is mounted on a highly mobile truck-based launcher designed to disperse rapidly across Japan’s southwestern islands and complicate enemy targeting. The launcher can be moved between roads, hidden sites and pre-prepared firing positions.

Japanese planners increasingly believe that static air bases and command facilities could be rapidly neutralised during the opening hours of a regional conflict involving Taiwan or the East China Sea.

For that reason, mobile missile batteries now occupy a central place within Japan’s emerging “counterstrike capability” strategy, which seeks to hold adversary ships and missile sites at risk from beyond enemy air-defence range.

The 25SSM reportedly combines improved stealth shaping, reduced radar visibility and advanced guidance systems intended to penetrate increasingly sophisticated Chinese naval and air-defence networks. Although designed principally as an anti-ship missile, the 25SSM also possesses limited land-attack capability, creating the politically sensitive possibility that Japan could strike hostile missile launchers or logistics sites if an attack against Japan had already begun.

Unlike the cruise-style 25SSM, the 25HGP uses a rocket booster to loft a glide vehicle into the upper atmosphere before it descends toward its target at extremely high speed along unpredictable manoeuvring trajectories.

That flight profile makes the system significantly more difficult to intercept than conventional ballistic or cruise missiles because enemy radars and missile-defence systems struggle to predict its final approach path, according to Defence Security Asia.

The US recently approved US$ 340 million in technical support for Japan’s continued Hyper Velocity Gliding Projectile (HGP) development, including missile testing assistance inside the United States.

Japan is now selling advanced naval ships to Australia. The Australian Defence Ministry said it will buy 11 of Japan’s Mogami-class frigates, which are stealthy, state-of-the-art surface combatants that analysts say are equal to, and in some respects better than – anything China or even the United States is putting in the water.
