The Supreme Court on Tuesday took up for hearing 67 petitions regarding the Bill on the proposed 22nd Amendment to the Constitution, which seeks to increase the retirement age of judges of the Superior Courts.

The determination on the Bill by the Supreme Court would be directly conveyed to the Speaker of Parliament within three weeks after its consideration,  and the Speaker in turn would make it public during a Parliamentary sitting.

Some of the 67 petitioners contended that the retirement age of current Supreme Court judges should not be increased by this Constitutional amendment. Instead,  it should only extend the age limit of the judges appointed to the court after this amendment comes into force. Their argument is that it is improper for the very judges who stand to benefit from the extension of retirement age to preside over the case deciding the Bill’s constitutionality. However, the government did not accept this view.

The opposition and the Bar Association of Sri Lanka (BASL) made several requests in respect of the composition of the bench hearing the petitions. A full bench comprising all available judges of the Supreme Court for the hearing is one of them. Another request was that the Chief Justice should recuse himself from the proceeding. These requests appear to have been made out of concern that certain judges might approve the amendment in consideration of their own interests rather than its legal validity.

They also submitted a request regarding the court’s ruling on the matter. That was the approval by the people through a referendum, in addition to obtaining a two-thirds majority in Parliament to pass the bill. However, a referendum cannot be held in the country simply because someone desires it. A referendum can only be conducted under Articles 83 and 85 of the Constitution.

According to those provisions, a referendum must be held to amend Article of the Constitution regarding the country’s name, Sri Lanka’s unitary state structure, the sovereignty of the people, the national flag, the national anthem, the national day, the place given to Buddhism, freedom of thought including freedom of religion, and protection against torture.

Furthermore, if the term of office of either the President or Parliament is to be extended beyond six years, public approval must be obtained through a referendum. In addition, amending Article 83 itself, which contains these matters, also requires a referendum.

Apart from these, Article 85 of the Constitution highlights circumstances for holding a referendum. According to it, a referendum must be conducted to pass a Bill or any provision of a Bill, if the Cabinet or the Supreme Court recommends to do so. The President also has the power to pass a Bill rejected by Parliament through a referendum, provided that the Bill is not related to the provisions of the Constitution.

Even though referendums cannot be held just because anyone wants one, they can be held on issues desired by the President. However, the Constitution states that such a referendum can be conducted on a matter that the President considers to be of national importance. In other words, even if a matter really lacks national importance, the President can hold a referendum, because the President can argue that a matter is deemed important. The law leaves no room for anyone to challenge this.

Although it is not relevant to the subject matter of this article, the law similarly allows the President room to act contrary to reality when appointing a Prime Minister as well. The President is expected to appoint the person who, in his opinion, is most likely to command the confidence of Parliament as the Prime Minister. However, unlike in countries like India and Pakistan, there is no way in Sri Lanka to verify whether the majority of MPs support that individual prior to the appointment.

Thus, in 2015, despite the United People’s Freedom Alliance holding nearly a two-thirds majority in Parliament, President Maithripala Sirisena appointed Ranil Wickremesinghe—who had the support of only around 55 MPs—as Prime Minister. Similarly, in 2024, President Anura Kumara Dissanayake appointed Dr. Harini Amarasuriya as Prime Minister when she had the support of only three members, including herself.

On those occasions, neither Wickremesinghe nor Dr. Amarasuriya was the person preferred by the majority in Parliament, as is well known. If others in Parliament had wanted to, they could have brought a no-confidence motion later to nullify those appointments. However, due to the political atmosphere at the time, no one dared to do so.

According to our understanding of the aforementioned provisions of the Constitution, the decision to hold a referendum on the 22nd Amendment now solely depends on the Supreme Court. It is the court that has to determine whether the matter comes under the sovereignty of the people, as petitioners opposing the Amendment argued. The President and the Cabinet who can decide on it do not seem to support the view.

Under Sri Lankan law, during a referendum, a single question is put before the people. In the current context, we can speculate that the question would be whether the retirement age of Superior Court judges should be extended. According to the Referendum Act, those answering “Yes” must mark a cross next to the Lamp symbol on the ballot paper, while those answering “No” must mark next to the Pot symbol. Hence, the highly rigged 1982 referendum for the extension of the term of the Parliament was called “Lampu-Kalagedi Sellama” (Lamp and Pot Game).

It is widely known that for the “Yes” side to win, the Lamp symbol must receive more than 50% of the valid votes cast. However, the Constitution specifies that if the total votes cast are less than two-thirds of the registered voters, the “Yes” side must secure at least one-third of the total registered voters to win. That means no matter how low the turnout is, “Yes” side must secure at least one-third of all registered voters.

During the 2024 general election, the total number of registered voters was approximately 17 million which by now, may have increased to 18 million. In that case, at least 6 million people would need to vote “Yes” to raise the retirement age of judges.

In the general election, the National People’s Power (NPP) obtained only around 6.9 million votes. During the local government elections held in May, 2025, the party lost about 2 million votes. The current situation remains unclear. However, it is doubtful whether people would show as much interest in a referendum as they do in elections. This is the rationale behind the government’s stance on the referendum for the passage of the 22nd Amendment.
