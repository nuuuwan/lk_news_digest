Their letter which was never meant for us

A group of activists hold anti-tobacco rally on ‘World no tobacco Day’

Somewhere in the American state of California, on the servers of the University of California, San Francisco, sits one of the world’s great archives of corporate secrets -- the largest of a handful of collections that have prised open the internal papers of the tobacco industry. It holds more than fourteen million of them: memos, letters, marketing plans, research the industry buried, strategies it hoped would never see daylight. Board minutes. Handwritten notes. The private correspondence of executives who assumed they were writing only to one another.

These documents are not there because the industry repented. They are there because it was caught. Through the 1990s, as the American states sued the tobacco companies to recover the public cost of treating dying smokers, the litigation forced the industry to open its filing cabinets. When the great settlement came in 1998, one of its terms required the companies to place their internal documents-tens of millions of pages- online, and to keep them there. A whistleblower’s leaked box had shown what was in those cabinets; the law then pried the rest of them open. What had been secret became, by court order, permanent public property. Thank you, America’s state attorneys general.

I was searching that archive for material to present at a conference recently, the way a person pans a river for one particular colour of stone. And there, among the millions of pages, I found a letter about us. About Sri Lanka. Written by one of our own.

The letter is a briefing on the “Code of Conduct” that the Ceylon Tobacco Company introduced for its marketing activities in Sri Lanka around the year 2000. It was written by the company’s Legal and Corporate Affairs Director to explain to colleagues, to superiors, to people inside the tobacco world exactly why the Code was being launched and exactly what it was meant to achieve.

It was never meant for me. It was never meant for you. It was certainly never meant for the mothers whose sons the company was recruiting, one advertisement at a time. It was an internal document, and internal documents have a quality that public relations type ones never can: they are honest. A press release is written to be believed. A private briefing is written to be understood by people who already know the real objective and simply need the plan. That is what makes this letter so rare and so valuable. And accurate. It is not an activist’s characterisation of the tobacco industry’s methods. It is not hearsay, or inference, or the suspicion or proposed theory of some anti-tobacco campaigner. It is the industry describing itself, in its own hand, to itself.

So for a few pages, we get to stand at a window that was never supposed to have glass we could see through. We get to watch the Ceylon Tobacco Company at work in the Sri Lanka of the late 1990s and early 2000s-and to read, in plain corporate English, precisely how a multinational company sets about frustrating a law meant to protect the public.

Let me set the scene as the letter itself sets it. In 1997, President Chandrika Bandaranaike Kumaratunga appointed a Presidential Task Force to formulate a national policy on alcohol, tobacco and illicit drugs. It reported in March 1998. The Cabinet agreed with its recommendations in principle. A bill was drafted. In May 1999, on the eve of World No Tobacco Day, the President was presented with the World Health Organization’s prestigious Tobacco Free World Award-the same honour given barely a fortnight earlier, that same month, to South Africa’s health minister Nkosazana Dlamini-Zuma, who had just driven her own country’s comprehensive tobacco laws through under the Nelson Mandela government. It is fateful that Nelson Mandela and Chandrika Kumartunga became Presidents of their respective countries in the same year, 1994. Across the Global South, governments were being decorated for standing up to tobacco.

Notice, before we go further, what this means. The Sri Lankan state was functioning. A president appointed a task force; the task force reported; the Cabinet approved; the legal drafters produced a bill and moved it toward Parliament. The institutions did their work and did it in a timely manner. If you went looking in this story for the corruptible Third World official of the familiar story-the one with his hand out-you would look in vain. The machinery of the Sri Lankan state, in these pages, behaves with speed and propriety.

So who is working to slow it down? Not a corrupt local functionary. But a respected multinational company, and its local arm, drawing on lawyers in London.

The letter records the campaign against the bill almost as a diary. The company wrote to the President and lodged “strong protests” on the grounds that its views had not been consulted, and that the report was therefore “biased”-the industry’s perennial demand that no law about tobacco may be considered legitimate unless the tobacco company itself helped to write it. In July 1998, the Managing Director and the Legal and Corporate Affairs Director met the President directly and protested again. She undertook to hear them out.

A second draft of the bill suddenly appeared after, according to the letter, “a lull” that lasted from the above protest in July 1998 to the date of “31st May 1999 when the President was awarded the WHO Tobacco Free Initiative award for action taken by her government. This led to the formulation of a 2nd draft of the bill.” So these awards do work, don’t they?

In September 1999, once they “were aware of the formulation of a 2nd draft bill”, three senior figures from the company went to see the President-and note who one of them was: a non-executive director who was also the Chairman of the Chamber of Commerce. A tobacco company, arriving at the highest office in the land wearing the borrowed respectability of general commerce pressuring the President to send the second draft of the bill to them for their scrutiny. The observations they then made on the bill were formulated, the letter tells us, “in liaison with BAT Lawyers and the Legal Department at the Centre”-the local company reaching back into the transnational legal muscle of British American Tobacco to fight a Sri Lankan public-health law. A local face that we see; a global machine behind it that we don’t.

Then the single most extraordinary sentence in the document. Despite all this, the bill was gazetted on 10 September 1999 and sent to Parliament on the 13th. And, in the company’s matter-of-fact telling: “On hearing this we faxed H.E and the draft bill was not placed on the order paper of Parliament.” A fax was sent. A bill stopped. Whatever the fuller story behind that sentence, the company records-with evident satisfaction, to its own people-that its intervention reached directly into the parliamentary process of our country and arrested it. This is not a small thing, right?

But the richest passage, perhaps the most entertaining, of course with dark humour, is the one where the company explains the Code of Conduct itself-the voluntary, public-spirited-looking set of rules it was about to announce with paid advertisements in every national newspaper, in all three languages. Why introduce a voluntary code at all? The letter does not leave us to guess. It lists the objectives. And because these are the company’s own words, written to its own people, they deserve to be read exactly as they were written.

“to demonstrate to the general public that [CTC] is sincere in its purpose... and is proactive in managing these issues”

“to demonstrate to government and the PTF that we are not seeking confrontation but sensible dialogue”

There it is, with no varnish. The voluntary code was not an alternative to harming the public; it was an instrument for delaying the law and managing public feeling. And a few lines later, the letter supplies the confession that gives the whole game away: “we need to offer substantial concessions in order to appear sincere.” Appear. You saw that word, right? The concessions were calibrated not for what they would protect but for how they would look. Don’t mistake the concessions for surrender, the letter is careful to add where the real line would be held: the company would “rigorously defend” on-store and in-store advertising-the retail visibility that actually moves the product, which can still be seen one way or another even today. Give away the periphery; guard the core; and call the whole performance sincerity.

Strip the letter down and what remains is something close to a mini playbook-a catalogue of the standard moves by which the tobacco industry fights governments world over simply trying to protect their people as they should. It is worth setting them out plainly, because once you have seen them here, in black and white, you will recognise them everywhere.

The tobacco industry’s playbook, as documented in this one letter:

Offer voluntary self-regulation — the “Code of Conduct” launched to head off looming legislation.

Delay effective control measure-in the company’s own words, “to delay the implementation of the Tobacco Bill.”

Manage public perception of the company-”to retain public empathy,” and concessions designed to appear sincere.

Concede something, retain the core-surrender much, but “rigorously defend” the point-of-sale advertising that drives sales.

Demand a hand in tobacco control-framing any law drafted without industry input as “biased.”

Cultivate top-level political access-a documented sequence of letters and meetings reaching to the highest office.

Borrow respectability-bringing the Chairman of the Chamber of Commerce to lend the tobacco company the face of ordinary business.

Deploy transnational legal power-local operations backed by “BAT Lawyers” and head-office counsel.

Frame public health law as extreme-the repeated branding of the bill as “draconian.”

Run a coordinated media campaign-advertisements in every paper, in all three languages, to define the story first.

Discredit critical journalism-dismissing the one dissenting editorial as the work of papers with which “the antis have a great rapport.”

Not one of these is my invention. Everyone is drawn from the four corners of a single letter that its author never dreamed a Sri Lankan reader would one day hold up to the light.

Two larger things surface when you sit with this document, and both are worth saying. The first is that it inverts the story we tell about corruption. We are trained to imagine the rot flowing upward from our own corruptible officials. Here it flows the other way. The Sri Lankan state, in this account, moves to protect its citizens- the President, the Task Force, the Cabinet, the Legal Department; it is a decent, respected, First-World-headquartered corporation and its local subsidiary that do the manoeuvring, the lobbying, the pressing, the stopping of bills by fax. The dirt, in this story, is imported-and it arrives in a suit, speaking the calm language of “sensible dialogue” and “sincere purpose” and “posh English.” This is the deepest lesson of the commercial determinants of health: that harm most often travels not as obvious vice but through the respectable, ordinary channels of commerce. That is why it is hard to see but easy to let through. The second is a matter I will state only as an observation, because Sri Lankans need no lecture on it. We have watched more than one government begin its life leaning toward the public and end it leaning toward the powerful. The first term earnest; the later years accommodating. This letter is a photograph, dated and precise, of the machinery by which that leaning is engineered — the access, the pressure, the flattery, the fax. It does not prove that any particular government changed sides. It shows us, once, with the curtain accidentally left open, how the changing of sides is attempted, patiently, on every government, in every term. The reader may decide for herself how the story tends to end.

The comforting thing, or the simple miracle, about this letter is that we have it at all. The uncomfortable thing is why we have it. We can read it today only because, in another country, a court forced an industry to dump its private confidential documents, and a single briefing about Sri Lanka happened to survive in that vast disclosure. It is a coincidence of another nation’s good work that we can see our own history this clearly. Which raises the question the letter cannot help but ask. The bill the company was working to slow in 1999 was no minor advertising rule; it was a comprehensive, Cabinet-approved tobacco control law, the forerunner of the lineage that Sri Lanka would not fully enact until the National Authority on Tobacco and Alcohol Act of 2006. Seven years, roughly, between the law’s approval in principle and its arrival in force. Seven years of advertising. Seven fresh cohorts of the young – our own children, recruited systematically.

And that was a quarter of a century ago — in an era when a strategy could still be typed into a letter and sent by fax, and so could still, one day, be found. The manoeuvring did not stop in 2000. It simply moved into rooms with no minutes, onto phones with no archive, into conversations that no court in California or anywhere else will ever pry open and hand to the public.

If the Ceylon Tobacco Company was doing this in 1999-and writing it down-what is being done now, by an industry that has learned, above all, never again to leave a letter behind?

That is the real value of the window at UCSF. Not that it lets us settle old scores, but that it teaches us what to look for in the dark. The methods in this letter are not history. They are a template, not only for Sri Lanka though. And the only reason we know it is a template for Sri Lanka is that, just once, somebody forgot to close the curtain. And you just had a peek.

The writer is a Consultant Psychiatrist and a Senior Lecturer in Psychological Medicine. He was also a Director of the Center for Combatting Tobacco (CCT).
