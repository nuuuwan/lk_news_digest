Why Are Our Politicians So Corrupt?

One can argue that it’s the accelerated Mahaveli Programme which opened the floodgates of mega political corruption in Sri Lanka

We have always known this truth: Corruption was not just rampant; it was endemic and looked ineradicable. The sarcastic saying aimed at Lankans – in Sri Lanka, it’s not what you know but whom you know that matters – was directly related to this corruption.

But getting rid of this destructive corruption element was not seen as a top political priority. Some people talked of Singapore, how Lee Kuan Yew eliminated it using draconian methods. Oh, but that’s Singapore, they said. This is Sri Lanka, and the merry making went on and on.

There was, of course, a bribery commission. It’s nothing new.  The Bribery Act No. 11 of 1954 was introduced just six years after independence to establish comprehensive written laws against bribery. The original Bribery Commission was founded in 1958 to handle bribery and corruption investigations. Even in the ‘good old days,’ when there was no established tradition of bribing politicians, the need for an anti-corruption body was felt.

Prime Minister S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike appointed the Parliamentary Bribery Commission led by Justice Walter Thalgodapitiya in 1959.The commission investigated allegations of bribery against members of parliament and government ministers. The final report was presented to Parliament on December 16, 1960, and  found  six  politicians guilty of receiving bribes or corrupt practices: But   the government  did not immediately punish those named in the 1960 report.

In 1965, Parliament passed the Imposition of Civic Disabilities (Special Provisions) Act (No. 14 of 1965).This act stripped the  accused politicians of their civic rights and voting rights for a period of seven years.

One may conclude from this that corruption among politicians was on a much lesser scale in the decades leading to the post-1977 Open Economy Pandora’s Box. The bribery commission was known mainly for netting  small fry from the bureaucracy and public services – a policeman or a public official who had taken a bribe of Rs. 500, 1000 of 5000 getting caught, paying fines, facing jail and losing jobs as a result.

That did not eradicate corruption. On the contrary, it only went to convince the ‘untouchables’ sitting in parliament or the plush chairs of top level bureaucracy that they could do as they please. One could also argue that high level corruption was less prior to 1977 simply because there was less money, and not due to the inherent decency or quality of those who held high office. But people of Dr. N. M. Perera’s quality ended their careers with reputations untarnished by allegations of corruption. There were ministers who travelled by bus to parliament. But the Mahaweli development programme changed all that. It was initiated in 1969 under Prime Minister Dudley Senanayake’s United UNP government and the first phase (the Polgolla Dam) was formally launched and commissioned by PM Dudley in February 1970, just before the 1970 general elections. This was meant to be a slow, 30 years programme. The subsequent United Front government (1970–1977) continued the initial construction phase (completing the Polgolla and Bowatenna headworks by 1976), but   J. R. Jayewardene after becoming president in 1977  accelerated the master plan in late 1977 to be completed  in just 6 years.

The total estimated capital cost for the initial 30-year programme, as formulated by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and the Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) in 1969, was Rs. 5,583 million (at 1968 prices). The initial estimated budget for the Accelerated Mahaweli Programme (AMP) in 1977 was a staggering Rs. 20,000 million (Rs. 20 billion) or approximately U.S. $610 million.

Corruption became rampant as everyone involved milked the Mahaveli cow. Top level politicians were suddenly said to own businesses in Australia, while technical officers, it was said, drove home in their own vans.  This allegedly established the tradition of high level corruption. But all that changed abruptly post-2024, after the NPP victory. Since then, there are almost daily arrests of high level officials, politicians and their relatives on corruption charges, and there have been unprecedented convictions, including 25-year jail sentences for two ex-ministers.

The anti-corruption drive is key to the NPP’s political survival. That is not to say it isn’t  political theatre. In a boiling socio-economic situation where one would expect mass protests to erupt, people are holding their peace because this judicial process answers a long-cherished need to see the corrupt getting their due – facing humiliating arrests and convictions. Whatever the opposition claims, these are not victims of trumped up charges and kangaroo courts. But it also allows the government to provide a type of therapy to an otherwise extremely frustrated population. It’s provides catharsis.

What remains to be seen is whether this anti-corruption process will continue and become an established tradition without being thrown overboard in the post-NPP era. Or are we going to be treated to a new political soap opera where, whoever from the opposition gains power will render impotent the prosecuting bodies, offer pardons to jailed allies and start prosecuting the key figures behind the current judicial process?

This is going to be the real test of Sri Lankan political culture.  The word ‘culture’ here is significant. One can argue that it’s the availability of previously unavailable wealth (or cash)  following economic liberalisation which unleashed the rush to become millionnaires (billionaires nowadays). But this phenomenon isn’t uniquely Sri Lankan. It can be observed in all countries which underwent similar socio-economic metamorphosis.

Take  Russia in the post-Soviet era, when it quickly became synonymous with oligarchs and mafias. It happened in China, too, in the post-Mao era, but China’s new generation of communist party leaders quickly took steps to bring runaway corruption under control with draconian measures. The family of anyone found guilty of high-level corruption and sentenced to be executed had to pay for the bullet. Therefore, we must believe in the sustainability and durability of the new anti-corruption culture. In this, however,  the institution is more important than the individual. Institutions are, of course, run by people. But they must be strong enough to withstand the inevitable change of faces and political ideologies. For that, the thinking has to be modern. That is where I fear that this experiment could fail. Historically, Sri Lanka’s 2000 years plus history of rule by kings illustrates this point well. Our ‘modern’ rulers mentally live in the past, and they want to keep their ‘subjects’ there. One President notoriously sat on a throne (replica of King Sri Wickrema Rajasinghe’s throne) after becoming president.

Mahinda Rajapakse was another propagandist for the importance of ancient royal traditions. During his presidency,  state-sponsored nationalistic narratives frequently invoked symbolic and genealogical connections to ancient monarchs to solidify his political legitimacy. After  defeating the LTTE in 2009, inscriptions on war monuments (such as the Jaffna war memorial) glorified Mahinda Rajapaksa as the “Lord of the Three Sinhala Countries” (Tri-Sinhala), a title historically reserved for ancient Sri Lankan monarchs who successfully unified the island.

Such people always believe that they are above all institutions. Ancient kings ruled with absolute authority. When the king was good, the rule was good, too. When the king was bad, things went bad. It’s like what Louis XIV of France said – ‘L’etat, c’est moi’ (The state, that’s me). We had benign kings and wise kings. Other were stupid, power-grabbing and corrupt. They wasted the royal treasury. Modern, democratic Sri Lanka, too, offers examples of this ‘the state, that’s me’ mindset.

Despite the current frenzy to net the corrupt, we are not out of troubled waters yet.
