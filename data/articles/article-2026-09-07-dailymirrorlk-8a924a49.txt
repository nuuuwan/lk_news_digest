School Meals to Nourish Free Education

The School Meal Programme  for the country as a whole is close to Rs. 33 billion or about 1% of all government expenditure.

Schools are central to communities. The next generation is socially and ideologically nourished in these spaces! However, nourishment is also a material process of eating and sustenance. The lack of it leads to malnourishment. I address here the provisioning of food through the School Meal Programme (SMP), which has hardly been discussed in the public sphere in recent times.

Over the last few months, I was given the opportunity to do a rapid study on the implementation of the SMP in the Northern Province. And I was intrigued by the large allocation for this programme in our budget for 2026. For the Northern Province alone the allocation was Rs. 2 billion, or close to 20% of the development component of the provincial budget. And for the country as a whole close to Rs. 33 billion or about 1% of all government expenditure. Over the last couple of years, the SMP has extended to all students from grade one to five, and in almost every school in the country. In other words, 1.4 million children, or one in sixteen citizens of this country eat one full meal provided through the schools on any given school day.

In this initiative to study the SMP in the Northern Province, I work with fellow academics from the Faculties of Arts and Medicine of the University of Jaffna and other social activists, supported by officials from the Education Department. We got a glimpse into the fascinating process of providing a healthy meal to every primary school child.

The meals consisting of rice, a protein (egg, fish, dry fish, chicken etc.), vegetables and a fruit, are to be chosen from a well-researched and assessed menu decided by the Ministries of Education and Health. The meal per student budgeted at Rs. 11 is an impressive logistical task and massive social responsibility that our schools are undertaking. Indeed, amidst a range of state programmes with short comings, the SMP is by far one of the most successful universal state programmes in the country. This is due to its local and decentralised character where the schools and their communities take ownership.

In this column, I provide some ideas from our rapid study to further strengthen the SMP; including connecting it with the communities and local livelihoods to nourish the next generation. I also discuss the need to extend it to older children in higher grades, particularly as we consider the dangers of a food crisis in the near future.

While school meals may not capture the imagination of the public when it is functioning smoothly, taking it away can at times earn the wrath of the masses. The mid-day school meal was first introduced in 1931 and was expanded over the next two decades. In this context, the Great Hartal of w1953 itself was triggered by the withdrawal of the rice subsidy and the mid-day school meal. For those of us who were not born or too young to remember that Hartal, the Aragalaya in response to the recent economic crisis, reminds us about the consequences of unavailability of essential goods in bringing about political changes.

In this context, free education and healthcare in their institutional forms have continued, even though they have been neglected by successive governments, which nevertheless were afraid to dismantle them. However, the same cannot be said of the universal food subsidy and the school meals, which have gone through many mutations, including the neoliberal logic of so-called targeting, as a way of whittling them down. It is in this context that the SMP’s revival as universal programme is poignant, and its crystallisation as an institution embedded in the local communities will be of historical significance.

There is something about children and their nutrition which can galvanise communities. The rapid study on the SMP brought together an enthusiastic group of researchers. We identified many aspects of school meals including the complexity of nutritional content, the challenges in the frugal preparation of the food, the delivery of inputs, and even agricultural production that is to ensure affordable and accessible produce. Indeed, nourishment and nutrition are only possible when there are working hands to ensure agricultural production and transform it into food.

There are four major questions that came from our research. First, the possibilities of augmenting the nutrition, including in schools and communities that may be vegetarian. Related, the kind of local varieties of food that can be introduced to ensure accessible and affordable supplies. Second, the importance of ensuring that the women who cook the food, particularly in the school kitchens, are adequately compensated. Third, the possibility of supplying the inputs from rice, eggs, fish and vegetables to firewood for cooking through co-operatives, which will also relieve the burden of procurement on the school, and particularly on the principals. Fourth, the problem of that sudden stop to the school meal, when children go from grade five to six.

Food security is a continuing concern, where countries may not be able to ensure enough grains or food due to disruption of global agricultural supplies, for example with the war on Iran – which has undermined the supply of fertilisers – and climate change. In many progressive quarters a new concept called food sovereignty is gaining ground. Food sovereignty is about ensuring self-sufficiency of appropriate food at the local level.

The great advantage of the SMP is that it creates a market with fixed needs at a certain price, while providing some flexibility in its menu to avoid food that may have momentarily fluctuated in price. This kind of market, which in the Northern Province amounting to Rs. 2 billion, can be a great boon for small producers, particularly if they are organised by co-operatives to supply healthy and decently priced food for their own communities. Next, if the programme is structured well, it can also provide a decent livelihood to those who cook the meals at or for the schools. The school canteens can also be avenues for the same cooks to create healthy foods for the children. In different ways, all the above are in practice today, but the challenge is to scale up these processes to reach most schools in the country.

While universal school meal programmes are being introduced in many countries, including in neighbouring India where it is now for all children up to grade eight, there is another reason why this is all the more important in Sri Lanka. Since the Covid pandemic and the long economic crisis that continues to this day, poverty and malnutrition have drastically risen in Sri Lanka.

At this crucial moment, I believe it is of utmost importance that the Government considers extending the SMP to grades one through nine. Otherwise, how can we expect our children to come to school when they are hungry, and what meaning would free education have, when our older children have to study with an empty stomach? Crises in the past have led to visionary social changes, and we as a country over the last decade have been pummelled by a long crisis. Extending the school meal to older children and the pre-schools, and perhaps even augmenting it with a glass of milk, are not luxuries, but the very essence of nourishing the next generation that can rebuild our country.
