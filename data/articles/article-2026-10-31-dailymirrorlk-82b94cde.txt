Why are investors fleeing? China SOE Exit From Colombo Logistical Hub

Regular abandonment of high-profile projects does not augur well for investment confidence in Sri Lanka

The recent exit of China Merchant Holdings from a  $392 million logistics hub in the Colombo Port City has yet again highlighted the usual script of high-profile investments announced with much fanfare collapsing under the weight of intractable regulatory bottlenecks.

The South Asia Commercial and Logistics Hub (SACHL), a joint venture between China Port Merchants, which held a 70% stake and Sri Lanka Port Authority, was announced in April 2023 and touted as a gesture of confidence in Colombo Port City while the country was under severe economic crisis.  In December the same year, the government officially gazetted it as a Strategic Development Project (SDP), effectively granting lucrative tax concessions including a 15-year total exemption from Corporate income tax, a 15-year exception on withholding taxes and dividend taxes for its shareholders and an exemption from secondary import levies during the construction period.

However, the project ran into troubled waters in 2024 when the IMF bailout conditions required the government to curb various tax concessions. The project, slated to be completed by the end of last year, has been stalled since then, while the government sought to bypass the IMF conditionalities by rerouting the project through the BOI.

However, in the end, the regular bureaucratic and regulatory bottlenecks and protracted inter-departmental wrangling ( On this occasion, between the Ministry of Finance, Port authorities and Inland Revenue Department) seemed to have hit the nail in the coffin.

China Merchants in a filing at the Hong Kong Stock Exchange stated that the company has decided to terminate the project “In view of changes in external circumstances, including adjustments to the SACLH project’s implementation environment and timetable as well as the respective commercial considerations of the parties.”

Following the abandonment of the project, the Sri Lanka Port Authority has announced plans to re-tender a 150,000-square-meter logistics hub under a new Public-Private Partnership.  One might argue the new PPP would offer a greater level playing field for international investors, and reposition the project towards more holistic international maritime supply chains, leveraging Colombo Port’s existing strength as a transhipment hub. Seventy per cent of Colombo Port’s transhipment volume is destined for India ( which accounts for 30 per cent of India’s transhipment volume). With the emergence of rival ports such as Vizhinjam, one might argue that the cautious approach would be to disentangle from potentially geopolitical considerations that might constrain Indian reliance on Colombo.

However, Sri Lankans who have a habit of reading too much into geopolitics fail to account for the primary contributory factor for recurrent deadlocks and abandonment of high-profile investment projects. They are all domestic: Institutional incompetence, regulatory bottlenecks and ad hoc policy changes have seriously dented Sri Lanka’s investment climate.

The list of high-profile victims includes the Japan-funded Colombo light railway project, the Adani wind farm in Mannar,  Sinopec’s $ 2.9 billion oil refinery in Hambantota, which had been deadlocked over similar regulatory bottlenecks and the under-utilised Hambantota Export Processing Zone, which was initially envisaged to be a 10,000-acre export processing zone feeding the Hambantota Port. Another is the $460 million US Millennium Challenge Corporation Grant, withdrawn by the funding institution due to the Gotabaya Rajapaksa administration’s politically instigated indifference to the offer.

Sri Lanka’s institutional structure is more attuned to finding excuses for not doing than contributing to growth. The same paralysis continues even after a project is formally abandoned. One striking example is the Adani Wind energy farm, which withdrew after the government sought to renegotiate the energy purchase contract. Leave aside the political narrative, the government’s decision for reappraisal may have its logic, later vindicated by tenders granted to two local producers at the purchase price of less than US$ 0.05 a unit against Adani’s $ 0.086.

However, since then the government has failed to follow up with a Swiss challenge tender or call new tenders for the abandoned Adani project. The bureaucracy in this country is more apt at procrastination than initiative. The opportunity cost is not just the delayed cheaper renewable energy. In a year or two, the project would be unviable due to local protests, often instigated by vested interests.

While the Port Authority plans for a PPP in place of the abandoned joint venture for a logistical hub, the authorities should make sure the project would not be a repetition of the recurrent institutional maladies.

China Port Merchants maintains a diversified portfolio, including the terminal in Colombo port. However, regular abandonment of high-profile projects does not augur well for investment confidence in Sri Lanka. The current stalemate of the Sinopec oil refinery, if it leads to its termination, would be one too many of such failures, and seriously hamper investor perception. That risks becoming NPP’s equivalent of Yahapalanaya’s unilateral suspension of the Colombo Port City project, which marked the end of Sri Lanka’s ten years of infrastructure-driven economic growth and the beginning of a lost decade, culminating in the economic crisis.

The general obsession to read geopolitics into this recurrent failure and procrastination of high-profile foreign projects is absurd is because they have affected projects with every major country — China, India, Japan and USA — that had sought to meaningfully engage economically with Sri Lanka. Similarly, it conceals the tremendous economic cost of the institutional incompetence and arcane regulatory framework. Until you recognise the principal malady, it is not possible to fix it.

Another major economic opportunity being pursued half-heartedly at the moment is the Japanese proposal for a Japan, Sri Lanka and India export- oriented economic corridor intended to integrate Sri Lanka with high- value Japanese supply chains and industrial hubs in India. The project, announced during President Anura Kumara Dissanayake’s visit to Japan and backed by Japan’s Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, aims to position Sri Lanka as a strategic manufacturing hub, and has the potential to increase Sri Lanka’s GDP by 9.3 per cent by 2030.

However, the project, like many of its predecessors, has faced the classic Sri Lankan ‘policy to implementation trap’.  A similar project in the past for strategic development in Trincomalee involving India, Japan and Sri Lanka had been a talk shop during much of Ranil Wickremesinghe’s administration and is now being rehashed into an energy hub involving Dubai, India and Sri Lanka, though on both occasions, there is little tangible progress on the ground.

Japan has insisted on legally binding guarantees for transparency, predictability and non-discrimination. The recent Investment Protection Act and the proposed Public Private Partnership Bill are intended to address some of the deep-rooted bureaucratic bottlenecks.

Tokyo has insisted that January next year, which marks the 75th anniversary of Japan-Sri Lanka relations, relations, be the deadline to finalise the triparty investment zones. The project offers Sri Lanka the opportunity to leapfrog in high value supply chains and gainful employment for the youth at a time Sri Lankan economy faces a crippling mismatch between the expectations of the labour force and available employment opportunities, which had seen last scale migration for foreign jobs, leading to a labour shortage at low end manufacturing and construction sector.

The NPP/ JVP government, when in opposition, blamed corruption for the flight of foreign investment and low economic growth. However, in power, it must be aware that economic growth is faltering under its watch, hitting 4.2 per cent in the second quarter. Its commitment to a complete overhaul of institutional and regulatory mechanisms for investment is praiseworthy. However, proof of pudding is in the eating, and as of now, it has delivered very little, other than managing the economic recovery, which, to give the devil its due, is Ranil Wickremesinghe’s achievement. That momentum is now faltering, and unless the government pushes forward with long-delayed economic reforms, Sri Lanka would likely head towards yet another period of suppressed economic growth, similar to the five years of Yahapalanaya.
