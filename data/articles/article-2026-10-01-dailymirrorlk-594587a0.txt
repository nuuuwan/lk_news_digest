Will any leader undo the 22A in future?

Despite opposition leader Sajith Premadasa’s promise, his party will need a two thirds majority to carry it out

The National People’s Power (NPP) government states that it is increasing the retirement age to retain the experience of senior judges for the judiciary for a little longer. The government may have other motives as well.

Opposition Leader Sajith Premadasa claims that he would repeal the recently adopted 22nd Constitutional Amendment once he takes office someday. Yet, we don’t believe he will do so.

Firstly, Premadasa’s party, the Samagi Jana Balawegaya (SJB) must have the two-thirds parliamentary majority to roll back this Constitutional Amendment. Yet, given the political antics staged by the Opposition parties -- including the SJB --  we cannot imagine a day when the SJB would secure a two-thirds parliamentary majority, or when it would come to power at all.

In Sri Lankan political history, a single party or a coalition has assumed office with a two-thirds majority on only three occasions - in the years of 1970, 1977, and 2024. The alliances led by Mahinda Rajapaksa won 144 and 145 seats respectively in 2010 and 2020, and secured a two-thirds majority only with the support of several minor political parties.

Therefore, it is difficult to believe that even if SJB comes to power, it will obtain a two-thirds majority capable of altering the constitution.

Besides, the 22nd Constitutional Amendment was introduced precisely to increase the retirement age of Supreme Court and Court of Appeal judges and it is generally believed that the appellate court judges also prefer to serve longer.  Therefore, if it is repealed in the future, it would cause significant dissatisfaction among the judiciary.

The National People’s Power (NPP) government states that it is increasing the retirement age to retain the experience of senior judges for the judiciary for a little longer. The government may have other motives as well. However, on what grounds would a future government reduce the retirement age of those senior judges? Will it claim that the experience of senior judges is not important?

The most critical question is why the 22nd Constitutional Amendment should be repealed at all. One could argue that this amendment will not expedite court trials in the manner the government claims. However, the opposition’s argument that increasing the retirement age of judges compromises the independence of the judiciary is completely baseless.

It is customary for politicians to declare, as Sajith Premadasa does, “We will come to power and change the course of history.” However, most of the time it is mere rhetoric. Only on a few rare occasions in history have politicians reversed the course of history. The undoing of 17th, 18th, 19th and the 20th Constitutional Amendments can be cited as examples of this.

Prior to 2001, the Presidents directly appointed whomever they wished to high state posts and as judges of the superior courts. They also removed individuals from those posts at will. Consequently, the courts and the state machinery operated under constant fear of the Executive.

To reverse this situation, the 17th Constitutional Amendment was introduced in 2001 during Chandrika Kumaratunga’s administration. In fact, Kumaratunga also kept delaying its passage. It was passed back then only because of pressure from the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), which wanted to get it done by supporting the Kumaratunga’s government when it had lost its parliamentary majority.

17th Amendment provided for the President to no longer directly appoint anyone to the high posts in the public service and the judiciary. The executive could only recommend names to the Constitutional Council which was introduced under the 17th Amendment, and only the names approved by the council could be appointed to such posts. Recently, the Constitutional Council rejected two names recommended by President Anura Kumara Dissanayake for the post of Auditor General.

The Constitutional Council is a body comprising members of parliament from both the ruling and opposition parties, as well as respected members of civil society. Through this mechanism, the absolute power of the President was somewhat checked.

In 2010, the United People’s Freedom Alliance led by Mahinda Rajapaksa assumed office after winning 144 parliamentary seats. With the support of other minor parties, Rajapaksa increased his parliamentary strength to a two-thirds majority and introduced the 18th Constitutional Amendment. Through this, the President was once again empowered to directly appoint whoever he wished to high state offices. The Constitutional Council was downgraded to a powerless Parliamentary Council. Besides, the 18th Amendment removed the rule limiting an individual to two presidential terms, allowing a person to contest for the presidency any number of times.

Following this, the Maithripala Sirisena-Ranil Wickremesinghe government came to power in 2015, promising to reintroduce checks on the presidency and to abolish the Executive Presidency. In April of that year, the 19th Constitutional Amendment was enacted, reviving the mechanism to check the President’s actions. The term of office for both the President and Parliament was reduced to five years. The legal provision restricting an individual to two presidential terms was also restored. Surprisingly, Rajapaksa loyalists who had backed the previous two opposing amendments, voted in favour of this one as well.

In 2019, Gotabaya Rajapaksa became the President, and his party, the Sri Lanka Podujana Peramuna (SLPP), won 145 seats. Like his brother, with the support of minor parties, he repealed the 19th Amendment in 2020 with the 20th Amendment. Through this, checks on the presidency were removed once again. Legal provisions were also included to allow his brother, Basil Rajapaksa, who held dual Sri Lankan-American citizenship, to contest elections.

When the economic crisis reached its peak, Gotabaya tried various means to win back public favour. Accordingly, in 2022, he himself came forward to repeal the 20th Amendment he had introduced and enacted the 22nd Amendment to restrict the powers of the presidency. The concession extended to dual citizens was also revoked.

In a striking display of unprincipled politics, Rajapaksa loyalists voted for every constitutional amendment from the 17th straight through to the 22nd, despite the fact that these laws alternately negated each other.

Apart from these shifts, no leader who claimed they would alter the course of political history actually did so. The best example of this is the repeated promise made by various parties to completely abolish the Executive Presidency. Chandrika Kumaratunga was the first to give such a promise in 1994. However, she did not possess the required two-thirds majority. Although the then United National Party (UNP) leader Gamini Dissanayake stated at one stage that he was ready to offer the opposition support to pass it, Chandrika did not follow through. To this day, that system of governance remains in practice.

In the 1980s, when the United National Party led by President J.R. Jayewardene introduced the open/liberal economic system, other political parties vehemently opposed it. Yet, even though many different political parties have held office in the country over the last 40 years, that economic system remains fully in force.

In Sri Lanka, political parties campaign during elections as if they are bitter ideological enemies of other parties. However, except for J.R. Jayewardene, who fundamentally altered the structure of the system of governance, all subsequent leaders have merely driven the same vehicle handed down to them without ever reversing the course of history.
