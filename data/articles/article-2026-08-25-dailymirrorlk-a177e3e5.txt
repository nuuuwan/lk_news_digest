Sri Lanka’s apparel sector flags double risk from EU-India trade deal

Sri Lanka’s apparel industry has warned that the recently concluded European Union (EU)-India Free Trade Agreement (FTA) could weaken its competitiveness in the European market by giving the Indian garments better tariff access while potentially complicating the use of the Indian-origin fabric by the local manufacturers.

The industry representatives raised the concerns during the discussions between the Export Development Board (EDB) and consultants appointed by the European Commission to assess the agreement’s implications for Sri Lanka.

The EU-India FTA, the negotiations for which concluded on January 27, is expected to grant duty-free access to several labour-intensive Indian exports, once it completes the necessary legal review, signature and ratification procedures. The Indian apparel would consequently become more price-competitive in the EU, one of Sri Lanka’s most important export markets.

The Sri Lankan manufacturers face a second potential disadvantage through the rules governing the origin of fabrics and other inputs sourced from India and used locally to produce the garments for export to the EU, under the GSP+ concession.

The industry representatives cautioned that any restriction on Sri Lanka’s ability to benefit from the cumulation arrangements involving the Indian inputs could further erode the sector’s competitiveness.

Cumulation allows the materials sourced from the designated partner countries to be treated as originating locally when determining whether a finished product qualifies for preferential tariff treatment.

The EDB said the government would engage with the EU to seek appropriate cumulation arrangements with India, aiming to protect Sri Lanka’s export competitiveness and support greater integration of regional supply chains.

However, the agency did not specify when the discussions would begin, the concessions Sri Lanka would seek or the value of apparel exports potentially exposed to the changes.

Joint Apparel Association Forum Secretary General Yohan Lawrence participated in the discussion and highlighted the challenges facing the garment industry once the agreement becomes operational.

The EU is Sri Lanka’s second-largest merchandise export destination after the United States and accounts for about 24 percent of the country’s goods exports, according to the EDB.

EDB Chairman Mangala Wijesinghe said that maintaining the preferential access through the EU’s GSP+ scheme would become increasingly important as the Indian products gain greater price competitiveness.

“We intend to position Sri Lanka as a reliable, sustainable and high-quality supplier to the global market, while encouraging the local enterprises to move towards higher-value, differentiated products and strengthen compliance with the EU standards,” Wijesinghe said.

European Commission consultant and international trade economist Paul Baker said that the economic modelling indicated that the agreement could substantially increase the Indian exports to the EU and strengthen the foreign direct investment flows into India.

He said the EU’s growing focus on sustainability, environmental performance, traceability and regulatory compliance could still provide opportunities for the Sri Lankan exporters capable of meeting those requirements.

The consultants said the concerns raised by the EDB and apparel industry would be considered in the final study submitted to the European Commission.
