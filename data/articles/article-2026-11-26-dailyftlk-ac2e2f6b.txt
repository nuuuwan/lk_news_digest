Men’s mental health: Battles we never see

“Who supports the man who is expected to support everyone else?”

Men are often expected to be strong, dependable and emotionally resilient. From a young age, many boys grow up hearing phrases such as “boys don’t cry” or “be strong”. While these words may be intended to encourage resilience, they can unintentionally teach men that expressing emotions is a sign of weakness.

As we observe Men’s Mental Health Awareness Month this November, it is important to recognise that behind a confident smile, a successful career or a seemingly happy family, a man may be silently struggling. In my field of work as a psychologist, I have observed that men often find it difficult to express emotional distress.

Instead of saying, “I am overwhelmed,” they may become irritable, withdrawn, unusually quiet or emotionally distant. Some immerse themselves in work, while others turn to alcohol, excessive screen time or other unhealthy coping mechanisms to escape what they are feeling.

Depression in men does not always look like sadness. Sometimes it appears as anger, frustration, exhaustion or a sudden lack of interest in relationships and activities they once enjoyed.

Society often measures a man’s worth by his ability to provide, protect and succeed. Financial pressures, relationship difficulties, career expectations and family responsibilities can leave men feeling that failure is simply not an option. Unfortunately, many suffer in silence because they fear being judged or perceived as weak.

True courage does not lie in denying these emotions, but in recognising them, understanding the mind and finding the strength to move forward. We must also recognise that men need emotional support just as much as women do. A husband needs to feel heard, a father needs reassurance, a son needs encouragement and a friend may simply need someone who listens without judgement.

Seeking psychological support is not a sign of weakness. It is an act of courage and responsibility towards oneself and one’s loved ones. This Men’s Mental Health Awareness Month, perhaps we should change the conversation. Instead of constantly reminding men to be strong, let us remind them that they are allowed to be human. After all, a man’s greatest strength lies not in how much pain he can carry in silence, but in knowing when he no longer has to carry it alone.
