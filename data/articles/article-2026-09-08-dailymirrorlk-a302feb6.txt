Paid leave essential to workers’ health and well-being

Colombo, Sep. 8 (Daily Mirror) - Sri Lanka’s workers are already entitled to a range of paid leave and holidays, but as workplaces become increasingly connected, a greater concern may be whether employees are actually able to switch off when they take time away from work.

Speaking to Daily Mirror, Head of the Environmental and Occupational Health Unit at the Ministry of Health, Dr Inoka Suraweera, said time away from work is important for workers’ health.

She said the health impact of work has to be considered alongside the amount of time people spend at their jobs. “If you just imagine a day, you spend at least around eight hours at the workplace, and you do this for 35-40 years,” Dr Suraweera said.

She noted that workers can be exposed to a range of occupational hazards depending on their jobs, including noise, radiation, extreme temperatures, chemicals, biological hazards, poor workplace ergonomics and unsafe machinery. Psychological hazards are also becoming increasingly important, she said, with workers facing risks such as occupational trauma, anxiety, depression, violence, bullying and burnout.

Within this context, paid leave provides workers with an opportunity to step away from workplace pressures, recover and spend time with their families and social networks.

Speaking about Sri Lanka’s leave arrangements, Dr Suraweera said the country’s leave situation is not a major concern, given the number of public holidays in addition to paid leave provided to employees. For instance, under the Shop and Office Employees (Regulation of Employment and Remuneration) Act, employees covered by the legislation are entitled to weekly holidays, annual leave, casual leave, statutory holidays, Full Moon Poya holidays and maternity leave.

The international picture, however, offers a broader perspective on the role of paid leave. According to an August 2026 report by the International Labour Organisation (ILO) on global legislative trends in paid annual leave, paid annual leave plays an important role in preventing excessive working hours, protecting physical and mental health, promoting work-life balance, supporting family cohesion and maintaining long-term productivity. The report also highlights significant differences in statutory leave entitlements across regions. Most countries in Europe and Africa provide more than three working weeks of statutory annual leave, while some countries provide considerably fewer days, including five days in the Philippines, six in Thailand and seven in Singapore. New Zealand provides employees with at least four weeks of paid annual holidays after 12 months of continuous employment, while full-time and part-time employees in Australia are entitled to four weeks of paid annual leave.

Meanwhile, the ILO also highlights a newer problem associated with digital technology, wherein workers remain connected to their jobs despite being on leave. The expansion of remote working has made it possible for employees to remain reachable even when they are physically away from their workplaces. In response, some countries have begun considering a “right to disconnect” aimed at protecting workers from work-related communications during their time off.

However, Dr Suraweera noted that the issue of workers being unable to disconnect from work while on leave has not yet emerged as a significant concern in Sri Lanka.

While Sri Lanka already provides workers with a range of leave entitlements, the growing use of digital technology and remote communication could increasingly raise questions about whether time away from work truly allows employees to rest and recover.
