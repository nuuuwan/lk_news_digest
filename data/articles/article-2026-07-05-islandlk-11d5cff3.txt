Why SL needs a National Talent and International Mobility Strategy

For more than five decades, Sri Lanka has been recognised internationally as a country that sends its people abroad. Labour migration has generated billions of dollars in foreign exchange, supported millions of families and made an invaluable contribution to the national economy. Successive governments have, therefore, focused considerable attention on promoting overseas employment and protecting Sri Lankan migrant workers.

The country is experiencing shortages of workers and specialised skills across many sectors of the economy. Hotels struggle to recruit experienced staff. The construction industry experiences delays due to labour shortages. Manufacturing companies report difficulties in finding skilled technicians. The information technology sector continues to seek highly qualified professionals, while universities, research institutions and healthcare providers face increasing challenges in attracting and retaining talent. At the same time, thousands of educated and experienced Sri Lankans continue to migrate overseas in search of better opportunities.

Can Sri Lanka achieve its economic aspirations by focusing only on sending workers abroad, or must it also become a country capable of attracting global talent, skills, knowledge and investment?

I believe the answer is clear. Sri Lanka must move beyond the narrow discussion of labour immigration and develop a comprehensive National Talent and International Mobility Strategy that supports national development while safeguarding national interests.

Labour shortages are no longer isolated problems affecting a few industries. They have become a structural constraint on economic growth.

Businesses report increasing difficulty in recruiting workers with the skills required for modern industries. Investors considering Sri Lanka increasingly examine not only tax incentives and infrastructure but also whether the country can provide access to skilled human resources. Innovation depends on researchers, engineers and entrepreneurs. Universities require internationally recognised academics. Tourism relies on skilled hospitality professionals. Without adequate human capital, economic growth inevitably slows.

Sri Lanka’s demographic trends further reinforce this reality. Declining birth rates, an ageing population and continued emigration of skilled professionals mean that domestic labour supply alone may not be sufficient to meet future economic needs.

Many countries facing similar circumstances have adopted proactive strategies to attract international talent. Singapore, Malaysia, South Korea, Australia, the United Arab Emirates and several European countries actively compete to attract skilled professionals, researchers, entrepreneurs and innovators. They recognise that talent has become one of the world’s most valuable economic resources.

Sri Lanka cannot afford to remain outside this global competition.

Public discussion in Sri Lanka often centres on whether foreign workers should be allowed into the country. This debate is understandable but incomplete. The real question is much broader.

What kind of people does Sri Lanka need to attract to achieve its national development goals?

*  Specialists capable of transferring knowledge and mentoring local workers.

Labour immigration is, therefore, only one component of a much broader national strategy for talent attraction and international mobility.

While discussing international talent, Sri Lanka must also recognise one of its greatest strengths, the global Sri Lankan diaspora.

More than three million Sri Lankans and persons of Sri Lankan origin live around the world. Among them are internationally recognised scientists, engineers, doctors, entrepreneurs, academics, technology experts, financiers, artists and development professionals. Many occupy senior leadership positions in multinational companies, universities, research institutions and international organisations.

For too long, national discussions about the diaspora have focused mainly on remittances.

The greatest contribution the diaspora can make is not only financial. It is their knowledge, expertise, innovation, professional networks, investment capacity and global experience.

Many members of the diaspora are willing to contribute to Sri Lanka if provided with structured, transparent and meaningful opportunities. Some may wish to mentor young entrepreneurs. Others may support research collaborations, provide technical advice, establish businesses, invest in emerging industries or participate in short-term assignments.

Sri Lanka should therefore develop a comprehensive Diaspora Talent Strategy supported by initiatives such as a National Skills Bank, an Idea Bank, professional exchange programmes and diaspora investment facilitation.

Our global diaspora should be regarded as an extension of Sri Lanka’s national talent pool.

Countries that have successfully addressed labour shortages have not simply opened their borders.

They have developed carefully designed systems that balance economic needs with national security, social cohesion and protection of local workers.

*  Protect employment opportunities for citizens through labour market testing.

*  Introduce specialised visa categories for investors, researchers and highly skilled professionals.

These measures demonstrate that attracting international talent is not incompatible with protecting national interests. On the contrary, effective governance strengthens both.

This conversation is not entirely new. In 2019, the National Human Resources Development Council, with technical support from the International Organisation for Migration (IOM), produced a comprehensive report recommending a whole-of-government approach to labour immigration. The report recognised that labour shortages were constraining economic growth and proposed important reforms, including manpower forecasting, labour market testing, work permits, qualification verification, employer responsibilities, biometric identification and an integrated single-window digital platform for processing foreign manpower.

More recently, in May 2026, the Cabinet appointed a committee to formulate policy recommendations on labour immigration.

However, Sri Lanka now has an opportunity to broaden this work beyond labour immigration and develop a forward-looking national strategy that addresses talent attraction, international mobility and diaspora engagement together.

Sri Lanka requires a long-term strategy built on several complementary pillars.

First, a national system should forecast future workforce requirements by sector, occupation and skills level so that policy decisions are guided by evidence rather than short-term pressures.

Second, immigration policies should facilitate the recruitment of highly skilled professionals where genuine shortages exist while protecting opportunities for Sri Lankan workers through transparent labour market testing.

Third, specialised visa pathways should be introduced for investors, entrepreneurs, researchers, academics, digital professionals and internationally recognised experts.

Fourth, a National Diaspora Skills Bank should connect the expertise of Sri Lankans living abroad with national priorities in research, education, innovation, entrepreneurship and public administration.

Fifth, Sri Lanka should position itself as an attractive destination for international students, researchers and knowledge partnerships, recognising that educational mobility often leads to long-term economic and innovation benefits.

Finally, modern digital systems, including biometric identity verification, integrated visa processing, qualification verification and secure information sharing should underpin all aspects of talent mobility.

Ultimately, this discussion is not really about immigration. It is about national competitiveness.

Countries that succeed in the 21st century will not necessarily be those with the largest populations or the lowest labour costs. They will be those that create attractive environments for talented people to live, work, invest, innovate and collaborate.

Sri Lanka possesses many natural advantages. It has an educated workforce, strategic geographic location, strong democratic institutions, rich cultural heritage and recognised strengths in tourism,

education and professional services. Combined with a globally successful diaspora and a carefully designed talent attraction strategy, these advantages can position Sri Lanka as a regional hub for knowledge, innovation and international mobility.

The question is no longer whether Sri Lanka should engage with global talent. The real question is whether we can afford not to.

The time has come to move beyond a narrow discussion of labour immigration and embrace a broader national vision, one that recognizes talent, knowledge and international mobility as strategic assets for sustainable development.

Sri Lanka’s geographical proximity to India presents a unique opportunity that few countries enjoy. India has emerged as one of the world’s leading sources of highly skilled professionals, entrepreneurs, researchers, technology specialists and innovators. As India’s economy continues to expand and bilateral economic cooperation deepens, opportunities for the movement of professionals, researchers, investors and knowledge workers between the two countries are likely to increase.

Ongoing discussions on strengthening the India–Sri Lanka economic partnership, including cooperation in services, further highlight the importance of establishing a modern national framework to facilitate the secure, transparent and well-managed movement of skilled professionals. Rather than viewing this solely through the lens of labour migration, Sri Lanka should develop policies that maximise knowledge transfer, technology collaboration, investment and innovation while protecting domestic labour market interests.

In the 21st century, countries will increasingly compete not only for investment but also for talent, knowledge and innovation. Sri Lanka’s future prosperity will depend on its ability to connect with the world’s best minds while mobilising the expertise of its own global diaspora. As one of the world’s fastest-growing knowledge economies and Sri Lanka’s closest neighbour, India represents a natural strategic partner in this journey. By developing a comprehensive National Talent and International Mobility Strategy today, Sri Lanka will be better positioned to harness these opportunities, strengthen regional cooperation and build a more competitive, innovative and resilient economy for the future.

If Sri Lanka is to become a competitive upper-middle-income economy, it must aspire not only to export talent to the world but also to attract the world’s talent to Sri Lanka.

Shantha Kulasekara is the Executive Director of the Institute of Migration Sri Lanka (IMSL), an independent, non-profit institution dedicated to advancing migration governance, international mobility and diaspora engagement. With over 30 years of national and international experience, he previously served with the International Organisation for Migration (IOM), the UN Migration Agency, as Head of Migration Governance in Sri Lanka and Head of Office in the Maldives.
