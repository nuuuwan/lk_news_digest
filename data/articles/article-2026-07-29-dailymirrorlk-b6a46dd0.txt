Prison overcrowding:  Revision of remand prisoners key to solution

The most practical is to first focus on remand prisoners who amount to around 70 percent of the total

The Department reported to Parliament that there was a backlog of 17,671 reports due from the Narcotics Division by end 2024

“The degree of civilisation of a society can be judged by entering its prisons.”

This aphorism is sometimes attributed to the Russian author Fyodor Dostoevsky and sometimes to the French philosopher Voltaire. Whoever said it, it rings true.

Describing the horrific overcrowding in our prisons is no longer necessary. The Negombo calamity has done that better than anyone could. What is now needed is quick action to prevent a recurrence and thereby drag our civilisation out from the ditch.

The most practical is to first focus on remand prisoners who amount to around 70 percent of the total. Just in Colombo, New Magazine and Colombo Remand Prisons held 29,745 unconvicted men in 2024, more than double the 12,592 housed in 2020. Women remandees were housed in Welikada: 2,692 in 2024 (more than double the number remanded in 2020). All these numbers are likely to be higher in 2026 because of the police wanting to score points under the President’s anti-drug campaign.

Remand prisoners are presumed innocent. There can be no debate about the need to treat remand prisoners in a humane manner. If effective measures can be taken to reduce the overcrowding in remand prisons, it will be easier to address problems affecting the minority who have been found guilty.   Sections 54 and 83

It is said that abuse by the police of Section 54 of the Poisons, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance of 1929, as amended, is one the main causes of overcrowding in remand prisons. The reason for the abuse is Section 83, an amendment introduced in 1984. It states that no person charged under Section 54 may be granted bail unless under exceptional circumstances by a High Court. This requires Lawyers, and money. The police use section 54 as an instrument to punish people without even the semblance of judicial oversight.

If the government acts quickly to repeal section 83, the inflow into the remand prisons can be stanched. Is this a complete solution? No. The Magistrate’s Courts should be better resourced, so that the judges can actually apply their minds to the question of whether a suspect should be given bail or not. It is not that all persons charged under section 54 will automatically be granted bail. If the police make a persuasive argument that a particular suspect must be remanded,  the Magistrate can do so. The police will no longer be able to overuse section 54 to take people they consider as bad guys “off the streets.”  Delays in Government Analyst’s reports

Proving a case filed under the Poisons, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance requires the Magistrate to see the scientific evidence on whether the substance that is the basis for the arrest is actually a prohibited substance, and even whether it meets the thresholds set under various sections. Until the report from the Government Analyst’s Department (GAD) is received, the suspects are kept in remand. T

The Department reported to Parliament that there was a backlog of 17,671 reports due from the Narcotics Division by end 2024. In 2020, the backlog was 5,873. The Department is generating more reports (27,127 in 2024 versus 16,928 in 2020), but the backlog is still increasing. The base problem is the rapidly increasing number of requests that the Department cannot keep up with.

Suspects must get justice. Therefore, reducing the requests for reports is not the answer (decriminalising minor drug possessions is, but that is politically difficult).

What is practical is a specialised and time-bound task force to examine the technical processes and work flows of the Department and also review the relevant legal provisions regarding, for example, the requirement that senior officials must be present in court to speak to the reports and the nature of the tests and procedures that are permitted. There are 91 officials at senior level in the GAD as against 222 in the Attorney General’s Department. Is this enough? Are the compensation packages for the scientific personnel adequate? Do the procedures allow them to focus on the science?

A comprehensive assessment of GAD followed by rapid implementation actions including amendments to the relevant legislation can cut the delays and thereby reduce the overcrowding of remand prisons.  House arrest and electronic anklets

Many countries, including Australia, Brazil, New Zealand, Thailand, and Viet Nam, are keeping unconvicted prisoners out of expensive remand custody using house arrest, complemented by electronic monitoring. I proposed this solution in August 2025 and was happy to see the Minister of Justice taking it up in February 2026.

In 2019, 60,347 devices were active in Brazil, which amounted to under 9 percent of the prison population in that country. Jair Bolsanaro, the former President of Brazil was restricted to his home wearing an electronic anklet while awaiting trial. According to The National Secretariat of Penal Policies, the cost of an electronic bracelet in Brazil was around R$200 (around Rs 10,800) per remandee. It is stated that the cost per monitoring a prisoner through this method is 70 percent lower than remanding that individual in prison.

The Brazilian law requires the monitored person to always wear the anklet and charge when it reaches 30 percent. If not, an alert is transmitted to the monitoring centre when it reaches 15 percent, and law enforcement is sent to the location. Those wearing anklets are required to stay at home or avoid pre-determined locations and must return home at pre-established times. If the conditions are not followed, the individual may be taken to prison.

From the above, it can be seen that actions such as a new act or amendments, the establishment of the necessary technical and human facilities at the police stations, the procurement of service providers must be taken. They should, ideally, be taken in parallel.

This solution can complement the legislative changes proposed above and the improvement of the Government Analyst’s functions. In combination, they will reduce the horrific overcrowding of remand prisons. They will not by themselves fix all that is wrong with our prisons. But they will create the space for retraining  prison and police officials, and for ensuring Sri Lankan prisons meet the UN’s Standard Minimum Rules for the Treatment of Prisoners (the Mandela Rules). By this means we will hopefully get prisons that reflect our aspirations to become more civilised.
