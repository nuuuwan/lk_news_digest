PERSONA Beyond the family name: Tilak de Zoysa’s journey in business

Success in business is rarely defined by a single qualification or an unbroken record of achievement. For corporate leader Tilak de Zoysa, a career spanning industry, corporate leadership, diplomacy and humanitarian service began with an unlikely chapter: failing his O/L examination twice.

A member of the prominent de Soysa family and nephew of businessman and philanthropist Sir Cyril de Zoysa, de Zoysa would eventually rise to become one of Sri Lanka’s longstanding corporate figures, leading some of the country’s best-known companies and business chambers while building an international profile through humanitarian and diplomatic work.

Yet, his early years offered few indications of the conventional academic path to corporate leadership.

At Royal College, Colombo, de Zoysa’s interests were largely outside the classroom. He excelled in sport, becoming Royal Primary Wrestling Champion and later playing cricket and rugby. Academically, however, he struggled. After failing his O/L examination twice, he passed on his third attempt with strong results.

Rather than pursuing university education, he chose a different route, one that would eventually shape his approach to management and leadership.

For seven and a half years, de Zoysa worked in the tea plantations of Maskeliya, gaining first-hand exposure to almost every aspect of the industry, from planting and maintaining tea bushes to plucking, weighing, processing and preparing tea for auction.

More significantly, plantation life exposed him to managing people and resolving difficult labour issues. Living in relative isolation and working closely with plantation employees taught him lessons in discipline, humility and the importance of respecting workers, principles that would later underpin his management philosophy.

In 1966, de Zoysa joined Commercial Engineering Company as a clerk on a monthly salary of Rs. 200. His responsibilities included travelling by bus to purchase steel, cement and other construction materials.

He later joined his brother’s refrigerator business, working in sales and after-sales service, and by 1971 had risen to become Sales and Service Manager.

A defining turn came in 1978, when his father asked him to return to the family-controlled Associated Motorways (AMW), which was then navigating a difficult period with around 1,400 employees and weak business conditions.

It was at AMW that de Zoysa would make his mark on Sri Lanka’s automobile industry.

Travelling to Japan with his father, he worked on developing relationships with Nissan and strengthening AMW’s international partnerships. He subsequently rose to become Deputy Chairman and Managing Director of the company.

Among the significant deals associated with his tenure was the introduction of CEAT tyres to Sri Lanka.

De Zoysa took CEAT’s Managing Director, during a visit to Sri Lanka, to AMW’s tyre rebuilding facility in Kalutara and later to the Kalutara Bodhiya, an institution closely associated with his family’s philanthropic legacy. The interaction helped establish confidence between the two sides and was followed within weeks by a visit to India to formalise the partnership. He was also involved in securing and developing relationships with international automotive brands including Maruti, Suzuki, Yamaha and Renault.

His corporate career subsequently expanded well beyond the automobile sector.

Over the years, de Zoysa chaired 11 companies, with leadership roles spanning businesses including Carson Cumberbatch, Associated CEAT, Dutch Lanka Trailers, Trinity Steel and Wipro International, among others.

His influence also extended into Sri Lanka’s organised private sector. He served as Chairman of both the Ceylon Chamber of Commerce and the National Chamber of Commerce, placing him among a relatively small group of business leaders to have headed both institutions.

He served on numerous corporate boards and was appointed to the Monetary Board of the Central Bank of Sri Lanka, while his diplomatic engagements included serving as Consul of Croatia.

Alongside his corporate career, humanitarian work became an important part of de Zoysa’s public life.

He served as Chairman of HelpAge Sri Lanka for 13 years before becoming the first Asian Chairman of HelpAge International in the United Kingdom and subsequently serving as its Global Ambassador.

Following the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, he was involved in overseeing the distribution of substantial international assistance, an experience that placed considerable emphasis on transparency and accountability in handling donor funds.

His international contributions were recognised by Japan in 2004 when he received the Order of the Rising Sun, Gold Rays with Neck Ribbon, an honour conferred on foreign nationals for distinguished contributions.

Despite a career spanning boardrooms, international organisations and diplomacy, de Zoysa’s approach to management remains grounded in lessons learnt much earlier in his working life.

He advocates listening to competing viewpoints before making decisions, remaining impartial, accepting responsibility and giving executives sufficient freedom to make decisions of their own.

For de Zoysa, leadership is not about creating employees who remain dependent on the person at the top. It is about developing people capable of thinking and acting independently, while ensuring that credit goes to those responsible for an achievement.

That principle perhaps best captures a career that did not follow the conventional route.

De Zoysa grew up surrounded by business success and inherited a prominent family name, yet his formative experiences were those of a plantation worker and a junior employee earning Rs. 200 a month. Academic setbacks did not prevent him from building a career across some of Sri Lanka’s most established corporate institutions.

His story also offers a broader lesson for a generation entering a business environment increasingly focused on qualifications and credentials: formal education can open doors, but it is not the only measure of future potential.

Experience, resilience, integrity and the ability to work with people can prove equally decisive.
