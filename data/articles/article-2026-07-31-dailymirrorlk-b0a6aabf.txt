Judicial reforms do not mean retirement age extension

The debate over extending the retirement age of superior court judges has brought into focus a much larger question that Sri Lanka has struggled with for decades: how to create a judicial system that delivers timely, accessible and credible justice. While the proposal to retain experienced judges beyond the current retirement age may appear to offer a quick solution to the mounting backlog of cases, it risks addressing only the symptoms rather than the underlying causes of judicial delays.

A strong judiciary is not measured by the number of years judges remain in office, but by the efficiency, independence and accessibility of the justice system as a whole. The challenge before Sri Lanka is therefore not simply about the retirement age of judges. It is about whether the entire judicial process is designed to deliver justice without unnecessary delays.

There is no doubt that experienced judges possess valuable institutional knowledge. Years spent interpreting the Constitution, developing legal principles and handling complex cases contribute significantly to the strength of the judiciary.  Therefore, any discussion on retirement age should recognise the importance of preserving judicial experience.

However, judicial efficiency cannot depend on extending the service period of a limited number of judges. If the system itself remains burdened by procedural delays, administrative weaknesses and inadequate resources, keeping judges in office for a longer period will provide only temporary relief.

Sri Lanka’s courts face a long-standing backlog of cases. A person seeking resolution of a property dispute, a criminal case, or a fundamental rights application cannot be expected to wait indefinitely while procedural obstacles continue to slow down the process.

The solution requires comprehensive judicial reform. This includes strengthening court administration, improving case management systems, increasing the use of digital technology, expanding alternative dispute resolution mechanisms and ensuring that courts have adequate human and physical resources.

One of the major issues affecting the judiciary is the outdated administrative structure surrounding courts. Modern judicial systems around the world increasingly rely on technology-based case tracking, electronic filing systems and digital records to reduce delays and improve transparency. Sri Lanka must accelerate similar reforms.

The appointment process and institutional independence of the judiciary are equally important. Any reform affecting judges’ tenure must be approached carefully because judicial independence is a fundamental pillar of democracy. Judges must not feel that their career progression, continuation in office or retirement arrangements are influenced by political considerations. Public confidence in the judiciary depends not only on actual independence but also on the perception that judicial institutions are free from external pressure.

Therefore, discussions about retirement age extensions must be conducted through a transparent and inclusive process involving legal experts, judges, civil society and other stakeholders. Constitutional changes affecting the judiciary should not be viewed merely as administrative adjustments. They have implications for the balance of power among state institutions.

There is also a broader question about whether extending the retirement age could unintentionally limit opportunities for younger judicial officers and reduce institutional renewal. A judiciary that preserves expertise while encouraging the growth of younger legal professionals will be stronger and more dynamic.

The government’s argument that experienced judges can help address the backlog deserves consideration. However, judicial delays are not caused by retirement alone. They are the result of decades of accumulated structural problems. Without addressing these deeper issues, extending retirement ages may become a convenient but incomplete response.

The objective should not simply be to keep judges longer in service; it should be to build a justice system where cases are resolved efficiently regardless of who occupies the bench. A modern republic requires institutions that function effectively through strong systems rather than dependence on individuals.

Sri Lanka’s judicial reform agenda must therefore move beyond short-term fixes. The country needs a comprehensive strategy that combines institutional independence, technological modernisation, administrative reform and adequate investment in the justice sector.

The credibility of democracy ultimately depends on the public’s faith that justice will be delivered fairly and without unreasonable delay. Achieving that goal requires courage to confront structural weaknesses rather than relying on limited measures that offer temporary solutions.
