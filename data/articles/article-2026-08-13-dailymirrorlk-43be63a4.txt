Why Sri Lanka needs to protect its remaining elephants

Sri Lanka’s elephant problem is far from over. With each passing day, the gentle giants of our jungles are facing a worrisome plight. Statistics indicate that over 350 elephants had succumbed to injuries as a result of the human-elephant conflict. But this conflict, if managed properly, could have been averted.

It is a known fact that farmers and people of yesteryear coexisted with nature. Farmers kept a portion of their harvest (known as Kurulu Paluwa) for birds and animals such as elephants. This was an unharvested outer strip or buffer zone of the paddy field left deliberately near forest boundaries. During the early 20th century, Sri Lanka was mostly covered by dense natural forests. These dense forests allowed large herds of elephants to roam around freely. Due to low population density, small, scattered rural settlements left wide, uninterrupted migratory pathways for elephant herds. Old agricultural practices used low-impact methods that didn’t permanently block elephant ranges.

Sri Lanka’s tank cascade system especially in the dry zone has been recognised by the Food and Agricultural Organisation (FAO) as a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage system. It included forest patches surrounding main reservoirs which collected rainwater to be used during the dry season. These forest patches and peripheral tree belts provided shade and the necessary food for elephants. But as the population grew, and people were in need of land, these forest patches were cleared.

During the post-independence era, large scale irrigation projects cleared vast dry zone habitats. As a result, habitats became fragmented and elephants, amongst many other wildlife species, were deprived of food and water. Eventually, this trapped elephants outside protected zones, increasing encounters with humans. On the other hand, traditional farming systems were replaced by modernised approaches and seasonal foraging routes used by elephants were replaced by permanent crop fields. This resulted in crop damage and the elephant eventually became a pest.

Around 1950s and 1960s, the then governments considered putting up electric fences to confine elephants to certain regions. In 1998, the first community-based elephant fence was erected by the Sri Lanka Wildlife Conservation Society in Pussellayaya. However, over the years, many of these fences were defunct  due to poor maintenance and management, and elephant fences weren’t identified as a feasible solution.

Today, people in rural areas consider elephants as a menace. They go to any length to keep elephants away. Illegal electric fences have taken away the lives of many elephants such as Barana, the iconic tusker that roamed around Kala Wewa. The use of fire crackers, elephant crackers, snaring have pushed elephants towards more vulnerabilities. Farmers are compelled to aim their firearms at elephants who come to their vegetable plots and as a result many wild elephants succumb to gunshot injuries.

Sri Lanka has witnessed numerous infrastructure developments that ultimately took a toll on the environment. Construction of road networks via elephant migratory routes, constructing commercial establishments and human settlements along elephant corridors have aggravated the conflict further. It was very much later that government officials realised the need to have wildlife underpasses at least as a pilot project to mitigate the conflict.

But mitigating Sri Lanka’s human-elephant conflict cannot be done inside A/C rooms, or from Colombo where people have no clue about an encounter with an elephant. There have been numerous discussions between government officials and rural communities about mitigating this conflict. But a feasible solution is yet to be found.

‘Bringing the world together to help elephants’ is the theme for World Elephant Day which was commemorated on Wednesday (August 12). World Elephant Day was co-founded on August 12, 2012 by Canadian Patricia Sims and the Elephant Reintroduction Foundation of Thailand, an initiative of HM Queen Sirikit of Thailand.

Sri Lanka is still trying to find middle ground to help her elephants, a species protected by the Fauna and Flora Protection Ordinance. But if this middle ground is not reached in the immediate future, Sri Lanka’s jungles will lose one of its endangered and iconic species.
