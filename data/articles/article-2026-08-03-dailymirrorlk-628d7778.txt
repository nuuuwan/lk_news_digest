Sri Lanka’s 17th IMF agreement that ends in March 2027 is no doubt the most consequential international agreement in the history of the country. It has drastically redrawn the workings of public finance and state institutions; from the Central Bank and the financial sector to the State Owned Enterprises (SOEs) and welfare programmes. The question before the country now is the post-IMF road ahead.

The 2020s marked by the Covid pandemic, tremendous balance of payment crisis followed by Sri Lanka’s first ever default in April 2022, and then the IMF’s austerity measures have contracted Sri Lanka’s economy. Can Sri Lanka come out of this economic depression towards an economic trajectory of growth with equity? What is to be done with the state institutions that had been run to the ground, including near liquidation, by successive governments since liberalisation in 1977, and are further suffocated by the ongoing IMF programme? How can our working people facing gruelling poverty and malnutrition be provided relief, but also sustainable livelihoods and employment?

The ongoing IMF programme has a much stronger hold on Sri Lanka than any previous agreement, because it came after a default. The IMF in its role as arbiter of debt restructuring could more or less hold the country to ransom. If Sri Lanka were to come out of its position of default with its international creditors, it had to accept the IMF’s Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) and the related stringent conditionalities. That the IMF for long has served the interests of Western creditors, and in Sri Lanka’s case the vultures of global financiers that hold International Sovereign Bonds (ISBs), is an open secret. How else would the IMF oversee a debt restructuring agreement signed by an unelected authoritarian president just two days before he lost the presidential election in September 2024?

When it comes to Sri Lanka’s external debt on which it defaulted close to 52% were commercial borrowing, inclusive of the 40% in ISBs. I have argued it is this high interest cost of ISBs borrowed extensively from 2007 to 2019 that was a major cause for debt distress and then default; a similar dynamic is also present in other countries like Ghana and Zambia. ISBs are sovereign debt borrowed in the international capital markets in New York and London, where five or ten year loans have to be paid back in foreign currency, usually USD. And in the case of countries like Sri Lanka at interest rates as high as 6 to 8 percent, over a ten year period.

The compounded interest itself could be equivalent to the principal, where such returns and particularly foreign currency earnings are unlikely, resulting in eventual default. However, the IMF programme has set a target of returning Sri Lanka to borrow ISBs again in 2027. Indeed, the IMF Report of June 2026 projects sustainability of Sri Lanka’s debt with external debt servicing of USD 2.417 billion covered through borrowing USD 1.5 billion in ISBs in 2027. This amounts to returning the sheep to the predatory wolves again.

While the President has clearly stated that this will be Sri Lanka’s last IMF programme, there are neoliberal propagandists from both national and international think tanks who are calling for another IMF programme and again floating ISBs. Not long ago, when some of us argued that the 17th IMF programme, like the previous sixteen programmes, is not the answer, many from the neoliberal establishment responded that this time it was different. Their call now for the 18th IMF programme is but a recognition of the failure of the 17th IMF programme, or betrays their interest to keep Sri Lanka under the stranglehold of the West.

When AKD was elected with great expectations two years ago, the NPP had just three members in parliament. The new president ahead of parliamentary elections was under tremendous pressure by the Western establishment to stick to the IMF programme signed eighteen months earlier and an unfair debt restructuring agreement signed two days prior to the elections.

Over the last two years since the presidential election, Sri Lanka has faced one shock after another; Trump’s tariffs, Cyclone Ditwah and the energy crisis with the US and Israel war on Iran. Managing such shocks even as the country attempts to emerge out of the worst economic crisis since Independence is no easy task. It has for months at a time pushed the government into a reactive corner.

In this context, four years is a long time in the political life of a government. Another IMF programme, shackling national policies to the priorities of global finance, will seal the fate of the government. Furthermore, coming out of an IMF programme alone will not be enough. The years of damage with the many laws and policies passed under the IMF programme have to be reviewed. The Central Bank Act, Public Finance Management Act and a whole range of other laws will have to be repealed or amended.

The stale policies of an export-led economy with trade liberalisation is now on the way to the dustbin; the World Trade Organisation itself dead as evident from Trump’s tariffs. The claims that tourism supported by infrastructure can bring steady inflows of foreign earnings are now shattered with the experience of the pandemic and the recent disruptions of international transport.

Nothing short of a fresh vision for our institutions, economy and society are necessary. The government alone cannot do this, and it will require not just much national debate and discussion, but also social mobilisation.

We are in uncharted economic waters. The formula for growth with development finance and international investors has become much talk with little substance. We are now forced to think about how we can use our domestic resources, whether it is our natural resources or our domestic finances, towards production, employment and the welfare of our people. Of course, every avenue to bring in foreign earnings should be pursued, but that will only be meaningful and sustainable if such foreign exchange is not squandered in luxurious or unproductive imports; how we spend our imports should be prioritised.

We need new or reformed institutions to develop our country at the crossroads, even as we draw on the lessons from the past. The Central Bank will have to play a developmental role. A twenty first century development bank is a must. Industrial and trade policies amidst a global economy coming apart are necessary. State institutions including SOEs have to be reformed and updated for our contemporary needs, and certainly not privatised and left to the whims of extractive financiers. Agricultural transformation is necessary to ensure a secure food system. Mobilisation of society with a new generation of co-operatives can be a way forward.

Such social mobilisation and the coordination of the state institutions will be necessary for economic growth with equity, including to provide relief to the people pummelled by a decade-long crisis and to revitalise their livelihoods. The budgetary allocation for the school meal programme – now provided to all children from Grade 1 to 5 – should be expanded to all school children, and a universal rice subsidy to address both the volatility in rice prices and to address malnutrition should be considered.

A robust housing scheme creating a multiplier of livelihoods for masons and wage workers, and backward linkages for industries and employment related to carpentry, roof tile making, metal works etc. could be considered for growth. Such an approach to the economy where the government takes the lead with investment, and in the process bringing in domestic private investment, is the need of the hour in world where we can expect little from external actors.

The NPP has an overwhelming mandate—like no other government in the history of the country. On a trying global pitch, the government has played a very cautious first innings over the last two years. The second innings cannot be the same, and it needs to muster the political will to mobilise state and society far beyond the boundaries set by the collapsing neoliberal order.
