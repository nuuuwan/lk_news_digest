Organised Crimes Bill erodes judicial powers - Romesh de Silva PC

Colombo, Nov. 5 (Daily Mirror) - The Supreme Court yesterday commenced hearing 26 Special Determination petitions challenging the constitutionality of the proposed Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill, with counsel who appeared on behalf of the petitioners raising concerns over provisions relating to detention orders, restrictions on bail, court reporting and the determination of membership in organised criminal groups.

The petitions were taken up before a three-judge bench comprising Chief Justice Preethi Padman Surasena and Justices Achala Wengappuli and Sampath Abayakoon.

Appearing for senior journalist Malinda Seneviratne, President’s Counsel Romesh de Silva submitted that Clause 21 of the proposed Bill effectively undermines judicial power by empowering police officers and a Ministry Secretary to make decisions relating to detention orders.

He argued that vesting such authority in police officers, without adequate judicial supervision, would undermine individual liberty and amount to an erosion of judicial power.

President’s Counsel de Silva also contended that the proposed legislation was inconsistent with the principles of the United Nations Convention against Transnational Organized Crime. He submitted that while the Convention provides for bail, the proposed Bill effectively denies bail for offences falling within its ambit.

Meanwhile, appearing for the Bar Association of Sri Lanka (BASL), President’s Counsel Saliya Pieris challenged Clause 14(3), which imposes restrictions on reporting judicial proceedings conducted under the proposed legislation without prior permission from the Court.

He submitted that the provision would prevent court journalists from reporting on proceedings under the proposed law unless they first obtained the Court’s approval.

He also drew the Court’s attention to Clause 4(2), raising concerns over what he described as the criminalisation of individuals based on their associations and appearance.

The provision sets out a non-exhaustive range of circumstances that a Court may consider when determining whether a person is a member of an organised criminal group. These include meetings with other individuals, identification by alleged members of such a group, styles of dress, tattoos, language, hand signals, associations with known members and visits to particular premises.
