PC Elections Phobia?  Only Cure, Face It

Governments often fear that an election held at an unfavourable time may lead to defeat

The continued postponement of Provincial Council (PC) elections in Sri Lanka has become a matter of serious concern for democratic governance and public representation. Provincial Councils were established to promote devolution of power and enable citizens to participate in decision-making at the regional level. When elections are repeatedly delayed, these institutions lose their democratic legitimacy, accountability is weakened, and public confidence in representative governance is undermined.

The history of postponing PC elections reflects a recurring pattern of political, legal, and administrative challenges. Since 2017–2018, elections have been repeatedly deferred due to changes to the electoral system, delimitation issues, legislative amendments, and related administrative difficulties. As a result, several PCs have remained without elected representatives for extended periods, with their functions being carried out by Governors and government officials rather than elected members. This prolonged absence of elected provincial administrations has been criticised by civil society organisations, constitutional experts, and opposition parties as a setback to democratic decentralisation and public participation in provincial governance.

An important aspect of this issue is that responsibility for the delays cannot be attributed to a single political party or administration. Successive governments representing different political groupings have postponed PC elections, often citing constitutional, legal, or practical obstacles. However, these postponements have also generated allegations that political considerations and concerns about possible electoral setbacks influenced decision-making. A recurring irony is that parties that supported or accepted delays while in government have often demanded immediate elections after moving into opposition. This has created the perception that commitment to democratic principles is sometimes shaped more by political advantage than by consistent respect for electoral rights.

Governments have argued that electoral reforms, legal amendments, and administrative preparations are necessary before conducting elections. Such concerns may have some validity; however, critics maintain that they cannot justify indefinite postponement of elections. In a functioning democracy, elections are the primary mechanism through which citizens renew or reject the mandate given to their representatives. Prolonged delays risk weakening democratic institutions, reducing public trust, and undermining confidence in the rule of law.

The fear of facing voters at an unfavourable political moment appears to be a recurring phenomenon among political leaders. Unlike common psychological fears, this political anxiety, sometimes described as “Election Phobia”, is rarely acknowledged openly, yet it has become increasingly visible in Sri Lanka’s political landscape. A phobia is generally understood as an intense and irrational fear that results in avoidance behaviour. The reluctance of political authorities to face the electorate at constitutionally determined intervals could reasonably be viewed as a political form of such avoidance.

Sri Lanka has experienced similar controversies in the past. In 1998, during the administration of President Chandrika Bandaranaike Kumaratunga, accusations were made that the government was attempting to avoid Provincial Council elections. The terms of several Provincial Councils, including those of Sabaragamuwa, North Central, Central, Uva, and Western Provinces, expired that year. The Elections Commissioner proceeded with the electoral process, accepted nominations, and fixed the polling date for  August 28, 1998. However, the government declared a state of emergency and introduced regulations that resulted in the cancellation of the scheduled election.

Several fundamental rights petitions were subsequently filed in the Supreme Court challenging the government’s actions. In its judgement delivered in January 1999, the Court examined the legality of the emergency declaration and the postponement of the election. Justice Mark Fernando, with Chief Justice G. P. S. de Silva and Justice Gunasekera concurring, criticized the failure to adequately protect the electoral process. The judgement emphasized that the Provincial Councils Elections Act was intended to ensure that elections would be held within a short period after dissolution,  and that cancellation of the election could not be justified under the Public Security Ordinance.

Justice Mark Fernando further held that the suspension of postal voting arrangements was unlawful, arbitrary, and not carried out in good faith. He stressed that the Elections Commissioner had been granted constitutional independence under Articles 103 and 104 to conduct elections according to law,  and to prevent improper interference or unnecessary delays. The Commissioner had both the authority and the responsibility to determine a new election date despite disputes surrounding emergency regulations.

The present situation reflects a similar political dilemma. Governments often fear that an election held at an unfavourable time may lead to defeat. Rising living costs, public dissatisfaction, allegations of corruption, and declining popularity can increase anxiety about facing voters. However, history suggests that attempts to delay elections through questionable methods frequently damage public trust and create greater political consequences in the long term.

The Buddhist principle of Avirodha, included in the concept of Dasaraja Dharma, emphasises respecting the wishes of the people and avoiding actions that obstruct public welfare. In a democracy, the will of citizens can only be determined through free, fair, and timely elections. Attempts to avoid elections are therefore inconsistent with democratic values and weaken the relationship between the government and the people.

Sri Lanka has witnessed similar debates in relation to national elections. In 1982, the government of President J. R. Jayewardene conducted a referendum to extend the life of Parliament instead of holding a general election. Critics argued that the move was intended to preserve the government’s strong parliamentary majority and avoid the uncertainty of an election under the newly introduced proportional representation system. The Liberal Party, led by Dr. Chanaka Amaratunga and Dr. Rajiva Wijesinghe, broke away from its alliance with the UNP and described the postponement as a threat to democratic rights.

Similarly, in 1975, the United Front government under Prime Minister Sirimavo Bandaranaike used its parliamentary majority to extend the life of Parliament by two years. When elections were eventually held in 1977, the government suffered a historic defeat. These examples demonstrate that postponing elections may provide temporary political relief but can also deepen public dissatisfaction and increase demands for change.

Unlike parliamentary elections, PC elections do not have a constitutional mechanism allowing indefinite postponement through a referendum or a special parliamentary majority. The Supreme Court has also recognised the importance of universal franchise as a fundamental democratic right. Therefore, prolonged delays in holding PC elections raise serious concerns regarding constitutional governance and democratic principles.

A defeat at a PC election may influence future presidential or parliamentary contests, but avoiding elections does not remove public dissatisfaction. Instead, it can strengthen opposition movements and increase distrust in government. Political leaders must recognise that authority maintained through avoidance rather than public consent is neither stable nor sustainable.

The most effective solution to “Election Phobia” is to face the electorate. Conducting PC elections on time, respecting the people’s mandate, and allowing democratic institutions to function are essential for restoring public confidence and safeguarding the long-term health of Sri Lanka’s democracy.

Breaking this cycle requires a genuine commitment from all political parties, whether in government or opposition. Respect for constitutional electoral timelines, transparent electoral reforms, and the timely conduct of PC elections are necessary to restore democratic legitimacy and ensure that provincial governance reflects the will of the people rather than political convenience.
