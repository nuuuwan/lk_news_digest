Conventional banks gain ground in Sri Lanka’s Islamic finance market

Sri Lanka’s conventional banks are expanding their footprint in Islamic finance, with assets held through their Islamic windows rising around 26 percent in the first half of 2026, as the industry’s total assets exceeded US$1 billion, according to Fitch Ratings.

The ratings agency expects the industry to grow over the medium term, supported by regulatory progress, rising political will and expanding conventional-bank participation. However, limited distribution channels, gaps in products and a weak operating environment are expected to constrain its reach.

“Interest in Islamic banking is rising among many Fitch-rated conventional banks, some of which have operated Islamic windows for over a decade, while others entered the segment recently,” Fitch said. Amana Bank, Sri Lanka’s only fully fledged Islamic bank, remained the dominant player, holding around 60 percent of Islamic banking assets at end-June 2026. Islamic windows operated by conventional banks accounted for the remainder.

The windows’ collective asset growth of around 26 percent was measured from the beginning of 2026. Amana Bank’s assets, measured year on year, increased by 7 percent to around Rs.215 billion at end-June, while deposits rose by 6.5 percent to Rs.180.3 billion.

Islamic banking is gradually increasing its share of the wider banking market, although its footprint remains small. It accounted for around 1.3 percent of banking system assets and 1.4 percent of deposits at end-June, up from 1.0 percent and 1.2 percent, respectively, in 2024.

Sri Lanka has one of the highest Islamic finance penetration rates among countries with Muslim-minority populations, according to Fitch. Muslims account for around 10 percent of the country’s population, a factor the agency identified as potentially constraining the industry’s mainstream relevance.

“However, the industry will likely remain niche due to the population mix, persistent demand-supply gaps, and a weak operating environment,” Fitch said.

Islamic banking accounted for about 91 percent of the industry’s assets, highlighting the relatively limited development of other segments. Takaful represented around 4 percent and non-bank financial institutions around 3 percent, while Islamic funds and sukuk each accounted for less than 1 percent.

A gap in sharia-compliant treasury instruments also limits Islamic banking’s liquidity-management options.

Amana Bank does not invest in government securities because of sharia restrictions and instead places excess liquidity in the interbank market. Fitch said these placements expose the bank to the sovereign’s credit profile through systemic linkages.

“The introduction of sharia-compliant alternatives to treasury instruments would further strengthen Islamic banking and provide additional options for liquidity management,” the agency said.

Amana’s institutional shareholders provide links to established Islamic finance markets. The Islamic Development Bank and IB Growth Fund together hold a 28.2 percent stake, while Bank Islam Malaysia Berhad is its fifth-largest shareholder. Fitch said these shareholders support knowledge transfer. Amana Bank is also covered by the Sri Lanka Deposit Insurance Scheme.

Growth beyond banking has been strong from a small base. The combined assets of Amana Takaful and Amana Takaful Life, the country’s only fully fledged takaful operators, rose around 32 percent year on year by end-2025, outpacing the insurance industry’s 18 percent growth.

Their combined presence nevertheless remained limited to just over 1 percent of insurance sector assets and about 2 percent of gross written premiums. Other insurers also offer takaful products through dedicated arms.

Islamic fund assets under management exceeded US$5.5 million in early August 2026, having increased around 35 percent from the beginning of the year.
