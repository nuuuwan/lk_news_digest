Easter Sunday Attack Verdict:  More a moment of reckoning than a closure

In the end,  suicide bombers struck after  local authorities disregarded multiple intelligence warnings by the Indian intelligence agencies

The judgement should provide closure to the survivors, the family members and the nation at large

On April 21, 2019, nine suicide bombers belonging to a home-grown Islamist extremist group, Jamathei Millathu Ibraheem (JMI), struck multiple churches and upscale hotels, killing 270 worshippers and tourists, and wounding five hundred people

At the time of this writing on Tuesday, a Trial-at-Bar of the Colombo High Court was expected to deliver the verdict on the landmark trial on the Easter Sunday terrorist attacks in 2019.

In one of the most exhaustive court cases,  the trial had 2,309 witnesses testify on behalf of the prosecution and a total of 23,270 charges filed against the 24 accused. One accused died in prison.

Among the accused were Mohammed Naufar alias Naufar Mavilavi, whom the FBI identified as the second emir of an Islamist cell, that pledged allegiance to the Islamic State, Mohamed Anwar Mohamed Riksan and Ahmed Milan Hayathu Mohamed. All three suspects were earlier indicted by the FBI for conspiring to provide material support to a designated foreign terrorist organisation.

The judgement should provide closure to the survivors, the family members and the nation at large. It should also be a moment of reckoning and retrospection, even more so now given that the tragedy had now been politicised to the extent that it threatens to blind the nation of repetition.

On April 21, 2019, nine suicide bombers belonging to a home-grown Islamist extremist group, Jamathei Millathu Ibraheem (JMI), struck multiple churches and upscale hotels, killing 270 worshippers and tourists, and wounding five hundred people.

The JMI, an obscure Islamist fundamentalist group mainly composed of affluent young Muslims such as the Ibrahim brothers who became Suicide bombers was an offshoot of a better-known Islamist outfit known as the National Thawheed Jammaath, which in turn was an offshoot of another well- established local outfit known as Sri Lanka Thawheed Jammaath (SLTJ).

The SLTJ, one of the earliest purveyors of Wahabisation of Sri Lanka, was first established in the country as a branch of Tamil Nadu Tawheed Jammaath.  The distinction between these groups  is merely semantic, except that JMI exercised a sense of urgency in terrorist action at home, while the others vacillated, instead preferring to commit violence abroad. All these groups operated in the open.

The first mosque of SLTJ was set up in Beruwela in 2002.  Mohammed Zahran Hashim, the ringleader of the Easter Sunday Attacks and the self-proclaimed local emir of Islamist State, split from SLTJ in 2012 and set up the National Tahweed Jammath (NTJ). In the same year, the first mosque of NTJ was set up in Kattankuddy. In the Parliamentary elections in 2015, every Muslim party in the East sought an audience with Zahran, who by then claimed to have a followership of 2000.

Easter Sunday attacks were not a political conspiracy, as some elements in the government, catholic church and compromised investigators try to project. They were a product of home-grown radicalisation that had been fostered by political inaction and cheap electoral calculations.

Extremism comes in two forms: Violent and non-violent. Not every extremist is a violent fanatic rampaging through a crowded market with a knife. Much of the radicalisation process goes through pathways of non-violent extremism, where individuals share ideological allegiance to extremist beliefs, but stop short of acting on them. Violent extremism and non-violent extremism cross-fertilise each other, and in fact the infrastructure of extremism — propagation, institutional capture and grassroots radicalisation — and its ideological façade is often provided by non-violent extremists. Hassan al-Banna’s Muslim Brotherhood, which claims to preach moderate political Islam and operate branches in much of the Muslim world (where it was banned from Egypt to recently in Tunisia) is the ideological fountainhead of Sunni Islamist fundamentalism, which was later adopted by al-Qaeda, the Islamic State and a number of local franchises of al-Qaeda and IS.

Radicalisation is a long-haul process which, for the most its part, is a below-the-threshold campaign of non-violent indoctrination.  It takes years or decades for non-violent extremism to mutate into violent manifestations. A decade before the July 7 2005 attacks in London, Islamists preached extremism in the East London mosques, and authorities looked the other way on the pretext of freedom of speech.

Non-violent extremism is not always invincible to the naked eye. Societies and subcultures often betray the radicalisation process that is under way through their newly adopted norms, values, dress codes, schooling and self-isolation from the dominant value system. The Arabisation of some quarters of Muslim society, wider adoption of the burqa, mushrooming Islamic International schools and Wahhabi mosques were all early warnings of the influence of an implanted ultra-conservative Wahhabism. The successive governments at the time ignored those warnings.

If those signs were not forceful enough, barely months before the attacks, in December 2018,  Buddhist statues in Mawanella were vandalised, and the police search for suspects led to a plantation in Wanathawilluwa, where a large cache of explosives and detonators were discovered. The Defence secretary acknowledged that around 50 Sri Lankans have gone to Islamic State-controlled territory in Syria. Ranil Wickremesinghe later famously claimed Sri Lanka did not have laws to punish the citizens who joined foreign terrorist groups.  In the end, the suicide bombers struck after  local authorities disregarded multiple intelligence warnings by the Indian intelligence agencies.

Then Police chief Pujitha Jayasundara and Secretary of Defence Hemasiri Fernando were later sentenced to death over the criminal negligence leading to the Easter Sunday massacre. However, the commander-in-chief and president Maithripala Sirisena, whose reluctance to take decisive action, owing to electoral calculations, was well documented in the presidential commission reports, got off with a slap on the wrist and just a fine.

Still, the Easter Sunday attacks were a premature violent manifestation of Islamic radicalisation. Despite the enormous tragedy it was, and the expanding tentacles of extremist ideology, Islamisation of the local Muslim society was still latent when  hurried terrorists blew up churches and hotels. Since then, public scrutiny and the outrage within the Muslim community have rolled back the Wahhabist expansion in Sri Lanka.

Recently, there is a growing fallacy that Islamist extremism has run out of steam. The proponents of this claim cite the defeat of Islamic State and the lull in the major terrorist attacks in the West. However, before the Islamic State, there was al-Qaeda, which momentarily fizzled away, driving followers to the hyper-extremist Islamic State. Ideologies, however warped they might be, do not just evaporate, especially if they entail primordial meanings. It would be a matter of years before the next phase of Islamist extremism announces its arrival, probably with a spectacular attack that galvanises its followers.

Muslim community suffered a massive public backlash after the Easter attack, which, while unfortunate, is a predictable social reaction. It took years for the mistrust to fade away. But that might also have reversed the runaway Arabisation within the community.

However, Sri Lanka has learnt wrong lessons from the Easter Sunday attack. Instead of building institutional capability to monitor and pre-emptively confront, and empowering moderate Sri Lankan Islam, and its grassroots organisations, the current government, at the behest of a section of the Catholic Church, has politicised the Easter attack.  The same misplaced politicised incentives that fostered radicalisation are in play under different political jargons.

The ongoing controversial detention of former military intelligence chief Suresh Salley and the politicised narrative of the complicity of intelligence agencies in the Easter Sunday attack – which any local defence correspondent worth their salt knows is bullocks –  weakens the intelligence apparatus and delegitimises them before the international media and agencies. Sri Lanka should take lessons from the Easter Sunday attack to avoid repetition. Instead, the country is repeating the same mistakes.
