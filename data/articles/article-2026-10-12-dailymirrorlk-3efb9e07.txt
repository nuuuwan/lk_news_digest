Catching the corrupt is not enough!

In recent weeks, Sri Lanka has witnessed a series of arrests and remand orders involving members of the Rajapaksa family, former ministers and former parliamentarians over allegations of fraud and corruption. The developments have attracted considerable public attention, with journalists and television crews gathering outside courts almost daily.

For a country that has suffered enormously from corruption, abuse of power and the misuse of public funds, these investigations matter. Those accused must face due process, and anyone found guilty must be held accountable, regardless of political connections or position. Nobody should be above the law.

However, while the Government of President Anura Kumara Dissanayake continues to emphasise its commitment to catching the corrupt, another question deserves equal attention. What is the Government doing to improve the lives of ordinary Sri Lankans who continue to struggle to make ends meet?

The National People’s Power came to power promising a break from the political culture that brought Sri Lanka to economic ruin. It pledged clean governance, accountability and a better future. Millions voted for that promise, hoping a new political leadership would finally put the interests of the people first.

But a government’s success cannot be measured solely by the number of politicians it arrests or investigations it initiates. It must also be measured by whether families can afford their groceries, patients can obtain essential medicines, young people can find jobs and businesses have the confidence to invest in Sri Lanka.

The cost of living continues to place enormous pressure on households. For ordinary families, the economic crisis is not an abstract discussion about debt restructuring. It is the price of rice, vegetables, milk powder, electricity, transport and school expenses.

A parent struggling to pay household bills is unlikely to find much comfort in hearing about another politician being remanded. The Government may argue that economic recovery takes time and that the damage caused by previous administrations cannot be reversed overnight. That is a reasonable argument.

But patience cannot be demanded indefinitely without visible progress. The public needs a clear plan, measurable results and meaningful relief. Economic recovery must eventually translate into something people can feel in their daily lives.

Equally troubling are concerns over the availability of essential medicines. For a patient battling cancer or another serious illness, a shortage is not merely an administrative failure. It can mean delays in treatment, anxiety and uncertainty for the entire family. Investigating corruption in medicine procurement is important, but so is ensuring that patients receive treatment without unnecessary disruption.

Unemployment and the departure of skilled workers present another challenge. Young Sri Lankans who have spent years acquiring qualifications want opportunities to build their futures at home. When suitable employment is unavailable, many look overseas, taking their skills and potential with them. This is a loss not only for individuals but also for the country. Sri Lanka cannot build a strong economy if its young people see migration as their only realistic path to a better life. Foreign direct investment is equally important. Sri Lanka needs investors who will establish businesses, create jobs, introduce technology and generate export revenue. Signing memoranda of understanding and announcing investment proposals may generate headlines, but what matters is whether they translate into actual investments, functioning businesses and employment.

Investors need policy consistency, efficient administration and confidence that regulations will not change unpredictably. Fighting corruption and improving the economy are not competing priorities. They should reinforce each other. Recovering stolen public assets, ending wasteful expenditure and preventing corrupt deals can protect national resources. But the benefits must ultimately reach the people.

The danger is that the Government could become so consumed by pursuing the failures of the past that it loses sight of the responsibilities of the present. President Dissanayake and his government must remember that the public did not elect them simply to investigate the previous regime. They were elected to govern a country of 22 million people whose expectations extend far beyond the courtroom.

The public will eventually judge this administration not only by who was arrested during its term, but also by whether life became more affordable, opportunities improved and the country moved forward.
