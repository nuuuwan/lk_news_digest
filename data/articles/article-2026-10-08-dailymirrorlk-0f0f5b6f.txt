Sri Lanka’s recovery brings limited relief to family budgets

From left: BCG Managing Director and Partner Anshuman Upadhyaya, BCG Managing Director and Senior Partner Nishant Gupta, BCG India Leader Marketing, Sales and Pricing, Managing Director and Partner Parul Bajaj (addresses media) and BCG Associate Director Devraj Bharati

Middle class accounted for 57% of households in 2026, down from 62% in 2020

Share in 2 lower-income categories rose to 39%, from 34%, while affluent households remained at 4%

Despite its reduced household share, middle class contributes about 70% of consumption

Sri Lanka’s households have lifted consumption to a record high while tightening their choices at the checkout, as a new Boston Consulting Group (BCG)–Ceylon Chamber survey revealed a smaller middle-class share and less room for purchases beyond the essentials.

The survey, presented to the editors in Colombo yesterday, highlighted an uneven recovery in the purchasing power.

The middle class’ share of households has fallen since 2020, while the consumers expecting to spend more substantially outnumber those anticipating higher incomes.

The households are protecting the spending on the necessities and becoming more selective elsewhere, with vehicles, furniture and jewellery among the purchases most likely to be postponed. The businesses face a market in which the strength of the overall consumption conceals significant differences in what the families can afford.

The report titled ‘The Changing Path to Purchase in Sri Lanka’, found that 77 percent of consumers expected the household expenditure to increase over the following six months. Just 38 percent expected their income to rise, while 35 percent anticipated no change and 27 percent expected a decline.

“The spending expectations that the Sri Lankan consumers have are running higher or running well ahead of income expectations,” BCG Associate Director Devraj Bharati told the briefing.

The findings draw on a nationally representative survey of 2,272 consumers across all nine provinces, conducted in June and July 2026 and complemented by focus group discussions. The expectations relate to the six months following the survey.

The household consumption reached Rs.8.9 trillion in 2025, at constant 2015 prices, exceeding the previous peak of Rs.8.2 trillion in 2019. Consumption has consistently accounted for 60-70 percent of GDP, making the household demand central to the country’s economic performance.

“If we go back about 10 years and look at how the overall Sri Lankan economy has evolved, we see that, in terms of household consumption, there have been three distinct phases,” BCG India Leader for Marketing, Sales and Pricing and Managing Director and Partner Parul Bajaj said.

The report puts the average annual real consumption growth at 5.5 percent between 2023 and 2025, following an average annual contraction of 0.8 percent between 2019 and 2023.

However, the recovery in aggregate expenditure has not been matched by a broad improvement in households’ income classifications.The middle class accounted for 57 percent of households in 2026, down from 62 percent in 2020. The share in the two lower-income categories rose to 39 percent, from 34 percent, while the affluent households remained at 4 percent.

BCG estimated that around 400,000 households shifted into the two lower-income categories. The income thresholds were adjusted for inflation to allow the comparison in real terms.

Under the study’s 2026 classifications, the middle-class households earn between Rs.75,000 and Rs.400,000 monthly. Those earning below Rs.75,000 fall into the two lower-income categories, while the affluent households earn above Rs.400,000.

Despite its reduced household share, the middle class contributes about 70 percent of consumption, according to the estimates derived from the reported spending across 32 categories.

The geographical distribution of demand adds another distinction. Rural Sri Lanka accounts for 56 percent of middle-class households, while the affluence is increasingly concentrated in the report’s urban-west segment.

“What we actually see is that affluence is sitting within the urban-west part of the country and it is rural which is anchoring the middle class,” Bajaj said.

The affluent households contribute 11 percent of consumption, despite accounting for just 4 percent of households. Their greater capacity to spend gives the businesses an opportunity alongside the larger, more budget-conscious middle-class market.

For the households overall, the necessities are absorbing more of the wallet. Food and beverages, healthcare and housing together accounted for 58 percent of expenditure in 2026, compared with 53 percent in 2020. Food and beverages alone rose to 32 percent, from 28 percent.

The consumers expected to spend more on essentials while maintaining expenditure on several everyday categories. Occasional purchases faced cutbacks and larger purchases were more likely to be deferred. The higher-income households showed greater spending resilience.

“While on the other side, if you are operating on the discretionary side of things, it is about unlocking the incremental demand because the consumer is slightly more cautious but you need to enable them to unlock this latent demand,” Bharati said.

The study also found that the consumers are considering more brands, while preference for one specific brand has weakened across several categories.

The single-brand preference among the packaged-food buyers fell to 17 percent, from 22 percent in 2020. For staples, it declined to 6 percent, from 14 percent, while personal care fell to 29 percent, from 39 percent.

“When I say brand consideration, it means people have a set of two, three, four brands in their mind when they are going to shop for a particular category,” Bharati said.

The digital channels increasingly influence which products enter those shortlists, although personal recommendations remain powerful.

“We went back and looked at what we found in 2020, when we did the last study and we actually saw that digital influence has moved from 32 percent in 2020 to 51 percent in 2026.”

Word of mouth remained the strongest influence at 66 percent, while YouTube and social media led digital discovery.

The survey found that 55 percent of consumers were aware of the technology and 18 percent had used it. About one-third of users employed it for brand and product research.

“So, we’re seeing that Gen AI is now becoming important not just as a productivity tool but also as an influencing mechanism for brand research, especially for the younger urban and affluent consumers,” Bajaj said.

Payment flexibility presents another opportunity for the businesses seeking to convert deferred purchases into sales. It ranked fourth among the factors influencing large-appliance purchases, cited by 45 percent of buyers.

Access to credit remained concentrated among higher-income consumers. Credit-card holding stood at 19 percent overall, compared with 33 percent among the households earning above Rs.150,000 monthly.

Digital banking showed a similar income divide. Only 25 percent of account holders used mobile or internet banking, ranging from 8 percent in the lowest-income category to 47 percent among the affluent households.

Among those who had adopted digital banking, mobile banking averaged 2.8 interactions a month, compared with 0.9 branch interactions.

The report recommended that consumer businesses adapt products, pack sizes, prices and distribution to the household spending power. The retailers were encouraged to consider payment flexibility where commercially appropriate, while the banks were urged to address access, familiarity and trust barriers to digital adoption.
