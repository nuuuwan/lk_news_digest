Is the Govt. trapped within its anti-corruption drive?

President Anura Kumara Dissanayake recently stated during a public meeting held in Kahatagasdigiliya that there are absolutely no boundaries or limits when it comes to arresting corrupt individuals.

The President made this statement while responding to a remark made by a former minister who had claimed that this government has no limits in arresting politicians. The President also emphasised that when making arrests in connection with corruption, statuses of individuals are irrelevant.

True; it is a fact that under the National People’s Power (NPP) government,  many individuals have been arrested regardless of their status, and many more are slated to be arrested. Namal Rajapaksa, the son of former President Mahinda Rajapaksa, was arrested on September 4 over allegations of receiving a bribe of Rs. 100 millionthrough the Airbus procurement deal that took place in 2012.

Besides, Mahinda Rajapaksa’s wife, Shiranthi Rajapaksa and Pradeep Kariyawasam, the husband of former Chief Justice Shirani Bandaranayake were arrested yesterday over an alleged fraud involving Siriliya Saviya Foundation which Shiranthi heads.

The prospect of a former President being arrested did not exist in the country before the NPP government took office. However, former President Ranil Wickremesinghe was arrested last year on charges of spending state funds for personal needs.

At the same time, we never expected that a former minister, regardless of their political party affiliation, would be handed a prison sentence. Nevertheless, former ministers Mahindananda Aluthgamage and Nalin Fernando were recently sentenced to 20 and 25 years in prison, respectively.

Although opposition parties label these incidents as political vendettas, it remains an undeniable fact that many politicians in the country have been involved in massive corruption scandals.

The bribery allegation involving the Airbus deal against Namal Rajapaksa has not been levelled in a vacuum. In a 2020 court case handled by the UK’s Serious Fraud Office (SFO), it was revealed that Airbus paid a bribe of $ 2 million to certain Sri Lankans, as  part of an aircraft purchase deal. Later Nimal Perera, a former close friend of the Rajapaksa family,   stated in an affidavit that his bank account received this money and he handed over Rs. 100 million to Namal Rajapaksa.

In another incident, it is alleged that the government suffered a loss of around Rs. 10 billion through the 2015 Central Bank bond scam. Consequently, Arjun Aloysius, the son-in-law of former Central Bank Governor Arjuna Mahendran, was arrested in 2018. Many also accuse Ranil Wickremesinghe and several others in connection with this matter.

Although the famous palatial house constructed on 16 acres of land in Malwana was alleged to be owned by Basil Rajapaksa, at the conclusion of a court trial, no one could legally prove its ownership. Yet, what really  happened is anybody’s guess. Yoshitha, another son of Mahinda Rajapaksa, and Yoshitha’s grandmother Daisy Forrest have also been indicted under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act.

It is not just them; except for a few parties and organisations like the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam, and the Frontline Socialist Party, almost everyone who entered or enters politics does so with the sole objective of making money. Most of the methods they use to acquire wealth are illegal. Even legally, they have mechanisms to amass vast amounts of money. For instance, they can import vehicles duty-free, which would otherwise require millions of rupees in customs duties, and later sell them at a price inclusive of the duty value.

The general belief is that if someone serves as a Member of Parliament for five years—especially as a member of the ruling party—the financial problems of even their future generations will be permanently solved. In fact, it is often enough just to be a member of a local Pradeshiya Sabha, let alone a member of  parliament.

A few years ago, over a hundred chairs were imported for the chamber of the Western Provincial Council at a cost of Rs. 600,000 each. Anyone can guess what actually took place there. In another incident, a report by the Auditor General a few years ago noted that a type of vaccine worth 250 rupees per vial was imported at a cost of 40,000 rupees per vial. On another occasion, the Land Reform Commission had sold a piece of land under its purview at a rate of Rs. 370 per acre.

Some directors of the CEB have their own power generation companies. They were the ones controlling electricity pricing. They are also the ones who discouraged low-cost solar power generation for a long time. The reason  why ministers turned a blind eye to all  this warrants in-depth investigation.

Hence, it is not just politicians; the majority of high-ranking government officials are also corrupt. Former President Maithripala Sirisena stated at a public gathering in October 2024 that ministers cannot steal without the assistance of the secretary and the chief accountant of the ministry. Recent news reports have also revealed that politicians extort millions of rupees from underworld leaders. It has been alleged that even presidential pardons from death sentences have been on sale.

Bribery is an issue that occurs in almost every government office in the country. High-ranking officials take bribes on a massive scale, sometimes in tens of millions, while lower-level officials and employees accept smaller amounts. The question remains as to whether one can find a single person who obtained a driving license without paying a bribe. Recently, even a court registrar was arrested.

Therefore, if a serious anti-corruption initiative functions systematically, thousands more could be arrested. However, the Bribery Commission and the CID lack the necessary manpower and resources to take action even on the complaints already received.

Nevertheless, the core issue is whether a country can achieve development solely by curbing corruption, even though anti-corruption measures are absolutely essential for economic development. Economic analysts, and the International Monetary Fund (IMF), have commended the NPP government’s fiscal performance from a macroeconomic standpoint. Last year, the country’s economic growth stood at 5%. Because Sri Lanka’s Gross National Income grew beyond the World Bank’s threshold, the country advanced last July from the status of a lower-middle-income country to an upper-middle-income country.

Yet, this does not mean the country has achieved the desired outcome or is on track to do so. Even though the total national income has increased, the nation’s wealth remains concentrated in the hands of a few. The plight of the poor has not changed. The economy frequently suffers shocks due to global impacts. Therefore, economic growth must continue, and it must expand toward rural areas.

Although foreign investment is an essential part of progress, a recent report by the U.S. State Department noted that past barriers to foreign investment are still at work. And without increasing foreign reserves, incentivising savings and diversifying exports, it would be difficult to withstand international economic shocks.

If these structural reforms are not executed rapidly and in parallel with curbing corruption,  the government risks becoming a prisoner of its own anti-corruption campaign.
