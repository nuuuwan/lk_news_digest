Are Sri Lanka’s wildlife laws enough to stop the destruction of medicinal plants?

Sri Lanka is preparing to mark World Wildlife Day 2026 amid growing concerns over the exploitation of medicinal and aromatic plants, raising questions over whether existing laws and enforcement are strong enough to prevent the country’s valuable biodiversity from being depleted.

This year’s World Wildlife Day theme, “Medicinal and Aromatic Plants: Conserving Health, Heritage and Livelihoods,” highlights the increasing threat faced by species that are commercially exploited for medicinal and cosmetic products.

The Department of Wildlife Conservation says millions of organisms are used to meet human demand, while several medicinal and aromatic species in Sri Lanka are increasingly threatened because of their high commercial value and excessive use.

The issue raises a difficult question: If Sri Lanka already has a strong legal framework for protecting wildlife, why are threatened species continuing to face pressure from commercial exploitation and illegal international trade?

Sri Lanka became a party to the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) in 1978, with the Department of Wildlife Conservation responsible for implementing the convention.

Wildlife officers conduct raids and enforce the Fauna and Flora Protection Ordinance to prevent the illegal exploitation of protected species. However, the continued international demand for valuable wildlife resources makes enforcement a constant challenge.

The concerns extend beyond animals. Rare and threatened plants are also vulnerable to exploitation, particularly when their commercial value increases.

Conservationists and authorities therefore face the challenge of balancing human demand for traditional medicines and other products with the need to ensure that valuable plant species are not driven towards extinction.

The controversy also raises questions about whether public awareness programmes alone can adequately address a problem driven by commercial demand.

Sri Lanka will mark World Wildlife Day on March 3 at the Lunugamvehera area of Yala National Park. The event will include awards for winners of wildlife-related school and open competitions, recognition of the best tourist bungalow in a national park and the declaration of the Thalawila coral reef area as the “Thalawila–Kalpitiya Sanctuary.”

The national celebration will be held under the patronage of Environment Minister Dr. Dammika Patabendi, with Professor Sagarika Kannangara of the University of Kelaniya delivering the keynote address on “Wildlife Conservation Ensures Human Survival.”

But as Sri Lanka celebrates its rich biodiversity, the central question remains: Can the country protect its wildlife and medicinal plant heritage from growing commercial pressure before some species become too valuable—or too rare—to save?
