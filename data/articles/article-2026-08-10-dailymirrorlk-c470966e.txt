How significant are the provincial councils today?

It has been almost 12 years since the last provincial council election was held

The leaders of the newly formed Tamil-speaking political parties’ platform have requested President Anura Kumara Dissanayake to hold the long-delayed provincial council elections, as soon as possible.

They made this request during their first media briefing following the launch of their platform on July 14 and on August 3 when they met the President. However, they got a response that had to be expected.

President Dissanayake had told them that elections for all nine provincial councils would be held once the Parliamentary Select Committee on electoral reforms recommends an appropriate electoral system for those councils. It was reported that the request was also made when the Indian Foreign Secretary Vikram Misri met the President on August 5, during his official visit to the island.

It has been almost 12 years since the last provincial council election was held. After a staggered series of elections, the final election took place for the Uva Provincial Council on September 20, 2014. The tenure of councils so elected in that year and the previous year ended in 2018 and 2019. Therefore, these councils are being run by the Governors, without the elected people’s representatives, at least for seven years.

However, it is not clear if the people of the country, especially those who are living in the Northern and Eastern Provinces, for which these councils were originally meant for, seriously felt the absence of elected members, in terms of administration and development.  In fact, Provincial councils were brought in under the 13th Amendment to the Constitution which was imposed on the country by the Indian leaders, using even military pressure and the resultant Indo-Lanka Accord of 1987.

These councils were meant for the resolution of the ethnic problem and hence originally planned to be established in the Tamil dominated Northern and Eastern Provinces. They were established later in other provinces as well, with a view to pacifying the majority Sinhalese who were furious at the idea of the devolution of power, at that time.

Nevertheless, with the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE) having rejected this mode of power sharing, all other Tamil groups who renounced violence to accept the provincial councils and moderate Tamil parties too,  started to find a fresh solution to the ethnic problem. The thousands of hours spent by leaders of Sinhalese, Tamil and Muslim communities in deliberations at various committees, Parliamentary select committees, All-party conferences and parleys mediated or facilitated by international players in search of a lasting solution have gone down the drain since the early eighties. Intransigency born out of the fear on the part of these leaders to be branded as traitors due to  intracommunity rivalry is the main culprit in sabotaging these peaceful efforts. Lack of seriousness and political will among the leaders of successive ruling parties is another reason.

We cannot see a single major achievement—economically or socially—by the provincial councils in the north or south in their nearly four-decade-long history. Even the first and only merged North-Eastern provincial council, and subsequent council elected for the north separately were highly politicised. The Sunday Times once in 2018 reported that “the NPC had adopted 415 resolutions during its five-year tenure, seven resolutions a day it had met, but many resolutions adopted had been related to issues mostly do not come within the purview of the NPC.”

In fact, provincial councils are a launching pad for most south-based politicians to start their sons’ and daughters’ political voyages. They currently demand the elections not because they are interested in power sharing minority communities, but on the premise that they would be able to defeat the NPP at those elections, jointly as an Opposition bloc.

The Indian government is apparently no longer committed to the Indo-Lanka Accord, yet ritualistically raises the issue during mutual visits by  leaders of both  countries.

However, provincial councils are the only administrative structure the Tamil community gained after sacrificing tens of thousands of lives, over a period of four decades, in the process of seeking a solution to the national question. Any effort to solve the national question must consider all these facts and push for clear strategies.
