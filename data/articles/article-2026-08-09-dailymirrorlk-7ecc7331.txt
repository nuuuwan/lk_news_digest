Sri Lanka’s elephant problem  is really a  fence-placement issue

Community fences remain the best approach to coexistence between humans and elephants in Sri Lanka

Seventy per cent of the country’s elephant population ranges outside protected areas and these areas are at or near their carrying capacity

Casualties of HEC reduced in 2024 largely due to nearly 300 community-built electric fences made to protect paddy tracts

In 2025, HEC figures rose again, as the government stepped back from the coexistence model recommended by the 2020 NAP

For 65 years, Sri Lanka’s human-elephant conflict (HEC) policy has rested on a single premise: that elephants can be confined inside Protected Areas (PAs). They cannot. Seventy per cent of the country’s elephant population ranges outside PAs, and the PAs themselves are at or near their carrying capacity. The 2020 National Action Plan for the Mitigation of human-elephant conflict (NAP) recognised this and set out a different framework: manage elephants inside and outside PAs and use fences to separate people from elephants rather than to imprison the animals.

In 2024, Sri Lanka’s human-elephant conflict began to ease. Elephant deaths fell by 20% and human deaths by 15%, driven largely by around 300 community-built electric fences to protect paddy tracts. In 2025, both figures rose again, as the government stepped back from the coexistence model recommended by the 2020 NAP.

Fencing works when it follows ecological and development boundaries. Where it does, it tends to protect villagers and elephants at once, and at a lower cost than the alternatives Sri Lanka currently funds.

NAP distinguishes between two types of electric fencing: linear fences erected by the Department of Wildlife Conservation (DWC), and community-based permanent fences around villages and seasonal paddy fields. DWC has erected 5,500 km of linear fences island-wide, but around 65% are along administrative and not ecological boundaries. NAP calls for these fences to be moved to elephant habitat boundaries which elephants relate to. However, villagers resist relocation and pressurise politicians as fences limit encroachment on state land. Communities also have little incentive to maintain these fences as they are funded and erected by the government.

In contrast, community-based permanent village fences and seasonal paddy field fences are located along human perimeters. This prevents crop raids on home gardens and paddy fields, limiting retaliatory action on elephants.  Villagers erect, partially fund, and maintain these fences, instilling a sense of ownership.

NAP’s recommendation to facilitate coexistence through community fences is based on research and pilots conducted by the Centre for Conservation and Research, Sri Lanka (CCRSL). CCRSL has piloted approximately 30 paddy and 50 village fences in the North-Central, North-West and Southern Provinces, many of which have been in operation successfully for over 15 years.  Together with CCRSL, the Department of Agrarian Development also erected around 300 paddy field fences in the North-Central and North-West Provinces in 2024. Conservationists attribute the fall in elephant deaths from 488 in 2023 to 388 in 2024 primarily to the success of this coexistence approach.

Village and paddy field fences can only succeed with active community participation and continuous maintenance. CCRSL’s demand-driven model facilitates long-term commitment by villagers. Communities build these fences and contribute seed money, which is channelled into a village-managed fence maintenance fund. This approach provides a sense of ownership and incentive to maintain fences in the long run. Communities must also compete to meet CCRSL’s criteria for eligibility and continuity. Competition drives the success of the CCRSL model. This contrasts with the pre-selection approach used by most governments, leading to politicisation.

NAP notes that community fencing requires long-term commitment by villagers and implementation/monitoring agencies. It recommends that Divisional Secretariats (DSs) are best placed to work on village fences given the close working relationship between the DSs’ offices and villagers. NAP further proposes that the Department of Agrarian Development (DAD) and the Mahaweli Authority of Sri Lanka (MASL) collaborate with villagers to install paddy field fences on land under their jurisdiction, given their stake in increasing crop yield. CCRSL also partners with these agencies and village/farmer organisations, providing free training and monitoring.

CCRSL’s research, also incorporated into NAP, shows that paddy field fences should be seasonal, not permanent. Between the Maha and Yala harvests, paddy land lies fallow for two to three months, and the crop residue is a key source of nutrients for elephants. Additionally, the fallow grounds serve an important function as a foraging area for elephants. Permanent fences require maintenance even during the fallow period, imposing additional costs on villagers with elephants inevitably breaking unmanned fences heightening HEC. In contrast, seasonal paddy field fences are built when cultivation begins, removed after harvest and stored by villagers until the next cropping period, allowing elephants access to crop residue. This is what coexistence looks like in practice.

Community fences remain the best approach to coexistence Sri Lanka has tested. What has been missing is not evidence, but political commitment to implementing NAP at scale.

In 2025, that commitment collapsed further. The government resumed the long-distance drives, in effect renewing a strategy that has failed for 65 years because PAs cannot hold the population that confining elephants would require. Community fence expansion stalled. NAP’s proposed Presidential Task Force, meant to coordinate implementing agencies and monitor progress, has still not been appointed.

A durable end to Sri Lanka’s human-elephant conflict is unlikely without sustained state commitment. But even partial, disciplined implementation of NAP’s fencing model has already shown it can save lives.
