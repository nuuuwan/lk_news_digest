Meat, morals, and the carbon footprint

Recently, a Buddhist monk whom I deeply respect for his understanding of the Buddha’s teaching stated with absolute conviction that there is nothing wrong with eating meat. He argued that no rule binds the laity in this matter and that meat consumption is not an unwholesome act, or papa karma. If his point was that eating meat has no proven consequence for a future life, I can accept that position, because no empirical evidence can settle such a claim.

But karma is not merely a theory about the afterlife; at its heart, it is a discipline of cause and effect. Seen in that light, it is difficult to claim that eating meat has no consequence at all. Whatever one may believe about future lives, and regardless of religious affiliation, the effects of meat consumption are visible here and now: in the climate, the land, public health, animal suffering, and the moral choices we make.

Animal farming is often discussed as a matter of food, culture, or personal preference. Yet when we look more closely, it is also a matter of consequence. Every system of production creates effects, and animal agriculture creates effects that reach far beyond the dinner plate. It affects the climate, land, water, public health, the lives of animals, and the moral habits of human beings. Whether one speaks in religious, ethical, or scientific language, the principle is the same: actions have results, and meat consumption is no exception.

One of the most visible consequences of animal farming is environmental damage. Livestock systems produce greenhouse gases, including methane, nitrous oxide, and carbon dioxide. Methane from cattle digestion and manure is especially powerful in warming the atmosphere. In this sense, meat production is not merely a private act of consumption; it is part of a global pattern that contributes to climate change.

Chart: Greenhouse Gas Emissions, Measured as Kilograms of CO2-Equivalent per 100 Grams of Protein

Available comparative data show that beef from beef herds produces about 50 kilograms of CO2-equivalent emissions per 100 grams of protein, far higher than most other protein sources. This figure is not the same as carbon dioxide from burning fuel; it is a standard climate metric that converts methane, nitrous oxide, and carbon dioxide into a common warming equivalent.

By comparison, many plant proteins produce only a small fraction of that footprint. Recent estimates also place livestock emissions at roughly 12% of global human-caused greenhouse gas emissions, while the wider agrifood system accounts for about one-third. For that reason, one practical contribution individuals can make is not necessarily to become vegetarian overnight, but to reduce high-impact meat consumption and choose lower-carbon sources of protein whenever possible.

The consequences of animal farming are also found in water and soil. Large farms produce substantial amounts of manure, and when this waste is poorly managed, it can contaminate rivers, lakes, and groundwater. Nutrients from manure and fertilizer can flow into waterways, causing algal blooms and oxygen-depleted zones where aquatic life cannot survive. Water is also used directly for animals and indirectly for growing their feed. When the full chain of production is considered, many animal products require far more water and land than plant-based foods.

A significant share of the world’s grain supply is also directed to animal feed rather than direct human consumption, making animal agriculture an inefficient way to convert land, water, and crops into calories and protein. Livestock production uses large areas of agricultural land, including pasture and land for feed crops, and continued expansion can contribute to deforestation and habitat loss.

Intensive farming also keeps large numbers of animals in confined conditions, which can increase the risk of infectious disease. Some diseases can move between animals and humans, creating risks beyond the farm itself. Antibiotics are sometimes used to prevent outbreaks and accelerate animal growth. However, when antimicrobials are overused, bacteria can become resistant, making infections in both animals and humans harder to treat.

As an island nation, Sri Lanka has access to naturally sourced or wild-harvested fish and seafood; the carbon emissions associated with this process are generally limited to fishing boats, refrigeration, and transportation. Taken together, these impacts are usually lower than those associated with farmed or imported fish. Yet national fish consumption has reportedly declined in recent years, largely because of inadequate refrigeration and preservation, both at sea and on land, and weak inland distribution infrastructure, which raises the costs and limits access.

Beyond the environment and public health lies the direct suffering of animals. Modern farming often treats living beings as units of production. Animals may be confined, separated from natural behaviors, transported long distances, and slaughtered at a fraction of their natural lifespan. Even if one argues that eating meat is not forbidden by religious rule, the question remains whether unnecessary suffering can be ignored. Compassion asks us to look not only at the final product, but also at the life behind it.

Many consumers assume that meat bought from the market is morally harmless because the animal was not slaughtered specifically for them. This resembles the sentiment expressed by the Buddhist monk mentioned earlier. But demand is part of the chain of causation: if consumers did not buy meat, the industry would not produce it at the same scale. For that reason, the First Precept remains relevant not only as a formal rule against killing, but also as a daily reminder to examine how ordinary habits could harm the planet.

To say that eating meat has no consequence is to overlook the chain of causes that makes meat possible. Whether we approach the issue through science, ethics, or the teachings of the Buddha, one truth remains clear: our choices matter. This does not mean that everyone must become vegetarian; that remains a personal decision. But anyone concerned about the future of the planet can begin with practical, mindful steps: eat less high-impact meat, replace some meals with plant proteins, choose lower-carbon animal proteins when animal protein is desired, and avoid waste.

As the chart above suggests, some protein choices carry only a fraction of the carbon footprint of others. Developing the fishing industry is an obvious choice. In the internet age, information on sustainability and nutritional value of any food item is readily available. What is needed now is broader public awareness of the economic, environmental, ethical, and health consequences of animal protein consumption—awareness that can help shape a more sustainable future, for our own sake. The reality is that world is running out of land and water to support both increasing human populations and farm animals.
