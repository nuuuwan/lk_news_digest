Those who read my previous week’s article in this newspaper, titled ‘Bird’s Eye View of the Economy: “Ok, Stable and Growing”; Worm’s Eye, “Not That Rosy”’, will find a timely companion in the newly released annual flagship report by the Institute of Policy Studies (IPS).

This publication skillfully mirrors that dual perspective, initialising its coverage with a four-chapter macroeconomic analysis—a high-level evaluation of national economic metrics like inflation, total output, and trade balances—before guiding readers through a much grimmer reality across the remaining ten chapters. This extensive second section serves as a “worm’s-eye” analysis, focusing on microeconomic conditions to explain how individual households, small businesses, and workers are actually faring on the ground.

Established in the late 1980s as an independent State apparatus, IPS was specifically created to provide autonomous policy advice, filling critical gaps in the less comprehensive reporting historically produced by the Central Bank and the Ministry of Finance. For nearly four decades, the institute has published this State of the Economy report every November, delivering crucial data just in time to assist the Ministry of Finance with its upcoming budget planning.

Because the official annual economic reviews from the Central Bank and the Finance Ministry for 2026 will not see the light of day until April and June 2027, respectively, this IPS report acts as the quintessential early bird catching the worm. Crucially, the 2026 report zeroes in on structural policy choices required to achieve durable, long-term growth—a pressing challenge for Sri Lanka as the nation prepares to exit the protective shield of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and its Extended Fund Facility (EFF), a structured loan program designed to stabilise bankrupt economies, which concludes in March 2027.

In the opening chapter, titled ‘Policy Perspectives’, IPS synthesises its institutional wisdom on how Sri Lanka should craft strategic policies to sustain economic growth once the safety net of the IMF is gone. However, the report candidly admits that the State does not possess total freedom to design a completely independent policy package just yet. It explicitly notes that “growth under the guardrails is the operating reality of 2026 and will remain so for the duration of the IMF program and beyond.” This means that the strict fiscal limits and structural benchmarks agreed with international lenders will dictate national budgeting for years to come.

Crucially, IPS reminds policymakers that an IMF program is essentially an emergency toolkit designed strictly to achieve macroeconomic stability—the stabilisation of national balances, such as curbing hyperinflation and steadying the currency. True, durable growth relies on an entirely different set of foundations. According to IPS as outlined in the State of the Economy Report for 2026, long-term economic expansion requires boosting the economy’s potential output through advanced technology, a more highly skilled workforce, and robust capital investment. It also demands careful mitigation of environmental damage, a steady elevation of living standards, and deep social inclusion—a development model ensuring that vulnerable or marginalised groups are not left behind but are given equal access to economic opportunities and fruits of economic advancement.

Thus, the IPS rejects the outdated philosophy of chasing growth for growth’s sake. Instead, it advises the Government to balance development with the genuine wellbeing of ordinary citizens. There is ultimately little virtue in economic growth unless it preserves the environment for posterity and invites every citizen to become a partner in national prosperity. In stark contrast, any policy framework that fosters social exclusion—allowing a select elite to hoard the fruits of growth—inevitably triggers severe social, political, and economic disorder.

Sri Lanka learnt this lesson at a catastrophic cost in 2022. On that occasion, ordinary people, pushed to the absolute brink by unendurable financial burdens, revolted against the State, culminating in the incumbent President fleeing the country under the cover of darkness and paving the way for a brand-new administration to set in. Fresh with the trauma of that hazardous memory, IPS is understandably desperate to avoid a repeat of history. It is precisely this fear of renewed instability that drives the institute to advocate for social inclusion as an indispensable goal of modern economic policy. It ends the chapter with a wise counsel in this regard. It says that Sri Lanka can and must grow, but it must grow differently to achieve its ambitious socio-economic targets.

While Sri Lanka has undoubtedly engineered an economic recovery from the absolute nadir of the 2022 crisis, IPS explicitly warns against the danger of complacency. The nation must not get stuck savouring this initial survival; instead, it must urgently transition from mere recovery to long-term economic resilience—the structural strength required to absorb, adapt to, and withstand sudden international financial shocks without collapsing. If this deeper resilience is not built, Sri Lanka risks becoming a defenceless victim to future global crises, leaving the economy damaged beyond redemption.

Achieving this safety net demands tough structural changes, including comprehensive tariff and labour reforms to boost competitiveness, alongside deep investment in modern infrastructure. Crucially, IPS stresses that this does not just mean pouring concrete into traditional physical projects like highways, but rather building digital infrastructure to modernise transactions, governance, and trade. National economic policies must be firmly geared toward this modernisation, yet the hurdles remain monumental.

The report reveals a grim reality when looking at individual sectors: between 2015 and 2025, Sri Lanka’s agriculture completely stagnated, suffering a below-zero compound annual growth rate (CAGR)—a metric that measures the smoothed, steady annual growth rate of an industry over a specific period, showing that real farming output actually shrank over the decade. Over the same 10-year period, industrial growth averaged a miniscule 0.5%,while the services sector averaged just 2%. These low numbers, as revealed by IPS, contributed to a very low real economic growth over that entire decade hovering barely above zero.

What this means is that the size of the cake that determines the real prosperity of people has remained almost the same. But when the inequitable income distribution is reckoned, the poor have become poorer, while the rich richer demonstrating the telltale signs of an exclusive economic growth. Thus, the sobering conclusion of IPS is that it is precisely from this gloomy, fragile baseline that Sri Lanka must now design its blueprint for truly durable growth.

Under the strict guidance of IMF, Sri Lanka’s fiscal policy—the way the Government manages its public taxation and spending—has focused intensely on achieving immediate fiscal stabilisation. However, IPS warns that while balancing the books is an absolute necessity to create a stable economic environment, it is not enough on its own to drive genuine national development. Instead of simply squeezing more taxes out of the economy, the institute highlights a critical need to maintain tax revenues while simultaneously rationalising, or streamlining, State spending.

This is known as a balanced fiscal stance, an approach that carefully balances incoming revenue against outgoing expenses to ensure the Government’s financial position actively supports national progress. In contrast, the standard IMF blueprint prescribes a rigid strategy of revenue-based fiscal consolidation, which means narrowing the budget deficit purely by driving up Government income through higher taxes and duties.

Although successive administrations have followed this tax-first strategy to the absolute letter, independent economists have heavily critiqued it, arguing that it suffocates local businesses and squeezes households. These experts echo the IPS view: Sri Lanka must instead pursue a balanced consolidation that cuts out wasteful expenditure while optimising tax revenue. The IPS report insists that this refined financial strategy must aim to build real prosperity for ordinary citizens through a growth-oriented reallocation of public money.

This is broken down into a fivefold plan: directing State investment into highly productive economic zones, channeling significant resources into human capital development—which means upgrading the skills, health, and knowledge of the workforce through better education and healthcare services—and building physical infrastructure strong enough to withstand severe climate shocks.

It also demands aggressive funding for technological innovation and digital transformations, alongside actions to improve productivity across every single sector. Crucially, this modern framework has absolutely no room for inflating recurrent expenditure—the everyday, ongoing costs of running the Government—such as expanding expensive fuel subsidies or maintaining a bloated, inefficient public service. These are exceptionally bold policy recommendations that challenge long-standing political habits.

With this comprehensive overview complete, IPS shifts its focus to a detailed “worm’s-eye” analysis, examining the ground realities faced by ordinary households. At first glance, the data appears promising: Sri Lanka’s unemployment rate has fallen to approximately 4%, returning to the baseline observed before the 2022 economic collapse.

However, the report warns that this figure is no reason for complacency. The drop in unemployment is not driven by the creation of new, productive jobs, but rather by a worrying trend of citizens completely leaving the labour force—the total pool of people who are either actively working or looking for work. This retreat is most alarming among women, whose participation rate has shrunk from roughly 35% in 2019 to just 30% in 2024. When fewer family members participate in the workforce, household income drops, directly damaging the overall welfare of dependents. For children, lower family income stunts parental investment in talent and skill development; for the elderly, it translates to reduced access to healthcare and a higher vulnerability to disease.

Making matters worse, this low participation is exacerbated by a severe brain drain—the mass emigration of highly educated and skilled professionals seeking better opportunities abroad. As IPS bluntly notes, this flight has removed the country’s most mobile, skilled workers, making unskilled workers to occupy jobs which demand high levels of skills. At the same time, a collapse in the supply of skilled workers needed by the information and communications technology (ICT) and professional service sectors has pushed those industries to hire less qualified workers to fill the vacancies. The result of this unwarranted development is the loss of competitiveness, on one side, and low growth in productivity, on the other.

The IPS report turns a compassionate yet analytical lens onto the plight of Sri Lanka’s rapidly ageing population, often referred to as the silvering people. While popular misconception often paints senior citizens as an economic burden who merely consume the goods produced by the younger workforce, the report vigorously corrects this bias, documenting the highly productive contributions they continue to make. In countless households, older family members look after the young, transfer invaluable traditional and professional knowledge to the next generation, and actively engage in trade and community services essential to a functioning society. Consequently, a vast market known as the silver economy has emerged around them.

This specialised sector encompasses two main branches: silver consumption goods tailored for active older adults, and silver-care products, such as targeted healthcare and mobility aids. The IPS report emphasises that a forward-thinking economy must deliberately allocate national resources to produce these essential goods and services.

These products are increasingly classified as age-brands, and the specialised field dedicated to developing them is formally known as gerontechnology—or more colloquially, age-tech—which uses technological solutions to improve the health, independence, and daily lives of older individuals. With Sri Lanka’s demographic shift accelerating rapidly, ensuring the wellbeing of this massive group through dedicated economic planning must become a core national priority.

In my view, the IPS ‘State of the Economy’ report is far too precious a document to be ignored by those entrusted with shaping the economic future of this country. It is an indispensable roadmap, yet it suffers from one persistent, recurring vulnerability: it continues to cater almost exclusively to technically competent academics and data analysts. Not every decisionmaker, particularly within the volatile political arena, possesses the specialised training required to instantly digest its complex macroeconomic messaging and dense policy equations.

To bridge this gap, while enthusiastically recommending this report to all policymakers, I strongly suggest that future editions should carry as addenda to them simple explanatory materials rich with easily understandable infographics—visual representations of information, data, or knowledge designed to make complex statistics quickly and easily digestible. Translating these dense chapters into scannable charts, diagrams, and visual breakdowns will democratise this vital knowledge, allowing ordinary citizens and politicians alike to fully grasp the urgent structural choices facing Sri Lanka.

My final recommendation is to Parliamentarians: Study this report from cover to cover, and if necessary, get IPS experts to explain complex matters they have presented, and use it productively for policymaking. Otherwise, it will simply remain a report, as it has happened in the past, consumed only by technical experts.
