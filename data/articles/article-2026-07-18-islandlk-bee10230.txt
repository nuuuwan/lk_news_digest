Erdoğan’s New Republic

Soon after dominating global headlines by hosting one of NATO’s most consequential summits in recent years, Recep Tayyip Erdoðan once again ensured that the closing images were unmistakably Turkish. Each visiting leader left Ankara carrying an engraved Gümüþay .357 Magnum revolver produced by the state-owned defence manufacturer MKE, complete with six cartridges, personalised stationery and a biography celebrating Erdoðan’s political journey. Erdoðan, however, had already achieved what mattered. The gift signalled that Turkey no longer wished to be seen merely as NATO’s south-eastern flank. It sought recognition as an independent military power capable of manufacturing weapons, shaping alliances and dictating conversations.

Days later, another story placed Erdoðan at the centre of regional geopolitics. An investigation published by Haaretz reported claims by the former Mossad chief, David Barnea, that a conversation between Donald Trump and Erdoðan influenced American reluctance to support a proposed Kurdish operation during the recent conflict with Iran. The report also included sharp disagreement from former senior Israeli intelligence officials, one dismissing Barnea’s explanation as a convenient attempt to explain operational failure. What matters is that Turkey’s president now features in discussions of covert regional strategy not as an observer but as a leader whose views are regarded as consequential by allies and adversaries alike.

Ten years after the failed coup attempt of 15 July 2016, Erdoðan stands as one of the most durable political figures of the twenty-first century. Few leaders have experienced a military uprising, survived it, transformed it into the defining narrative of national politics and then used its aftermath to redesign the architecture of the state. It emerged with a fundamentally different political order.

The coup itself remains one of modern Turkey’s most consequential events. More than 250 people were killed as sections of the armed forces attempted to overthrow the elected government. Fighter aircraft bombed Parliament, military units seized strategic infrastructure, and civilians answered Erdoðan’s televised appeal to confront tanks in the streets. Yet there is little dispute over what followed. Emergency rule enabled the most sweeping restructuring of the Turkish state since the founding of the republic.

One of the enduring ironies of modern Turkish politics is that the movement later accused of masterminding the coup had once been one of the governing coalition’s most valuable partners. During the Justice and Development Party’s (AK Party) first decade in power, followers of the exiled cleric Fethullah Gülen expanded their influence across the police, judiciary and sections of the bureaucracy while helping to weaken the military’s long-standing political dominance through the Ergenekon and Sledgehammer investigations. The alliance collapsed spectacularly after the 2013 corruption investigations targeting ministers and figures close to the presidency, transforming former partners into bitter enemies. The failed coup completed that rupture and furnished the political legitimacy for a purge on a scale unprecedented in the history of the Turkish Republic.

More than 150,000 public officials were dismissed or suspended, while well over 100,000 people faced detention or imprisonment on allegations of links to the Gülen movement, which Ankara holds responsible for the coup. Entire sections of the judiciary, military, universities and civil service were rebuilt. Critics argued that the purges rapidly extended far beyond those directly implicated in the coup and became a vehicle for replacing institutional pluralism with political loyalty.

The constitutional referendum held in 2017 completed the transformation. Turkey abandoned its parliamentary model in favour of an executive presidency that concentrated powers previously divided among different institutions. An office once designed largely as an arbiter became the dominant executive authority. Parliamentary oversight weakened. The bureaucracy increasingly revolved around the presidency rather than autonomous institutions.

Soner Çaðaptay, in The New Sultan, describes Erdoðan as an “anti-Atatürk Atatürk”. The phrase captures the central paradox of his rule. Mustafa Kemal Atatürk built a secular republic through centralised state power. Erdoðan has dismantled much of that secular order by deploying the same instruments of centralised authority. The ideological destination changed. The governing method remained remarkably familiar.

His first decade in government explains why that narrative proved so resilient. Turkey experienced rapid economic expansion, improved healthcare, modernised infrastructure and a substantial reduction in poverty. As Çaðaptay acknowledges, Erdoðan’s record “has many positive elements”, including significant improvements in living standards. These achievements created enduring electoral loyalty among millions of Turks whose lives genuinely improved under AK Party governments.

The relationship between prosperity and political dominance became increasingly reciprocal. Electoral victories strengthened executive authority. Executive authority facilitated greater influence over institutions. Institutions, in turn, reinforced electoral advantage. Scholars increasingly describe Turkey as a competitive authoritarian system rather than a conventional liberal democracy. Elections remain meaningful and opposition parties continue to govern major municipalities, yet the political playing field has become progressively more uneven.

The fate of Erdoðan’s principal opponents illustrates this evolution. Selahattin Demirtaþ has remained imprisoned since 2016 despite repeated calls from the European Court of Human Rights for his release. Ekrem Ýmamoðlu, the Istanbul mayor widely regarded as Erdoðan’s most formidable electoral challenger, faces legal proceedings that critics view as politically motivated, while the government insists the judiciary is acting independently. The Republican People’s Party has meanwhile become embroiled in internal disputes and court proceedings that opponents argue have weakened its capacity to challenge the presidency. Each case is different. Together they contribute to a political landscape in which Erdoðan confronts fragmented opponents rather than a unified alternative.

His foreign policy has displayed similar adaptability. Erdoðan purchased Russian S-400 missile systems while supplying armed drones to Ukraine, criticised Israel in uncompromising language while maintaining channels of communication with Washington, repaired relations with Gulf monarchies after years of confrontation, and expanded Turkey’s defence exports, military footprint and diplomatic influence across Africa, the Caucasus and the eastern Mediterranean. Few contemporary leaders have demonstrated such willingness to occupy apparently contradictory positions when doing so advances national leverage.

His rapport with Donald Trump reflects the same pragmatism. During Trump’s first presidency, sanctions threatened Turkey over the detention of an American pastor and Ankara’s acquisition of Russian missile systems. Yet both leaders repeatedly emphasised their personal relationship, allowing dialogue to continue even during periods of acute disagreement. If the recent  Haaretz investigation accurately reflects internal Israeli discussions, Erdoðan’s conversations with Trump remain sufficiently significant to feature in assessments of regional security planning. Turkey’s president is increasingly regarded as a strategic actor whose calculations cannot easily be ignored.

The greatest misunderstanding about Erdoðan is to imagine that his longevity rests primarily on repression. Coercion matters, but it is only one component of a much broader political formula. He built a loyal electoral coalition before accumulating extraordinary constitutional powers. He invested heavily in domestic defence production long before presenting personalised revolvers to NATO leaders. He cultivated religious conservatives, Anatolian entrepreneurs and nationalist voters long before many of his opponents grasped how profoundly Turkish politics had changed. His success has rested on combining electoral legitimacy, economic transformation, institutional redesign and relentless political adaptability. Ironically, it was also shaped by repeated Western miscalculations over Turkey’s trajectory, misjudgements that ultimately came at the expense of what was once the region’s most vibrant secular democracy bridging Europe and the Middle East.
