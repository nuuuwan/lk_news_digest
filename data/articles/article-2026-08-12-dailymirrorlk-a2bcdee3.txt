Forensic audit looms as former state utility pays millions of dollars in equity returns for emergency power

The legacy of state power monopoly has funneled millions of dollars in equity returns to private power producer Ace Power Embilipitiya (Pvt) Ltd over nearly a decade, exposing severe flaws in Sri Lanka’s long-term generation planning and an expensive reliance on emergency procurements.

Details revealed in Parliamentary Publication Series No. 396, containing the energy minister’s observations on the Committee on Public Enterprises investigations, show that the former Ceylon Electricity Board paid approximately 10.39 million dollars in return on equity charges alone to the plant operator between 2018 and 2024. These payments were made despite the original ten-year build-own-operate power purchase agreement for the 100-megawatt furnace oil plant expiring in April 2015.

Following the expiration of the original agreement, the state utility repeatedly sought and secured cabinet approvals to extend power purchases from the plant. The extensions were routinely justified on emergency grounds, citing delays in constructing planned least-cost power plants, severe transmission line bottlenecks in the southern grid, and prolonged droughts that depleted key hydroelectric reservoirs such as Samanalawewa.

Between 2016 and 2023, the legacy utility executed five separate contract extensions and short-term agreements. During the first one-year extension from April 2016 to April 2017, the state paid 4.16 million dollars in non-escalable capacity charges, which included 3.16 million dollars in equity returns. A second extension in 2017 drew another 4.37 million dollars in non-escalable charges, including 3.32 million dollars in equity returns. A broader three-year extension from 2018 to 2021 resulted in 13.17 million dollars in non-escalable payments, of which 10.02 million dollars represented equity returns to the owners.

Further short-term procurements were executed during the 2022 economic crisis to prevent widespread blackouts, and again in late 2023 to manage severe hydro deficits in the southern region when water was released from Samanalawewa for downstream agricultural needs. Across these temporary arrangements, the state continuously paid non-escalable capacity components that guaranteed profit margins for the retired independent power producer.

These continuous extensions occurred despite repeated objections from the energy sector regulator, the Public Utilities Commission of Sri Lanka. The regulator repeatedly informed the state utility and the Ministry of Energy that extending expired independent power producer contracts violated Section 43 of the Sri Lanka Electricity Act, which mandates competitive tendering for new generation capacity. However, ministry officials bypassed these regulatory hurdles by invoking emergency provisions under cabinet directives to maintain uninterrupted national supply.

The high financial cost of these extensions stands in stark contrast to failed state efforts to purchase the power plant outright. In March 2016, the cabinet initially approved negotiating the purchase of the Embilipitiya plant to secure long-term grid stability at lower costs. While Ace Power Embilipitiya submitted a purchase offer of 17 million dollars, the Chief Government Valuer subsequently appraised the facility at Rs. 2.37 billion in July 2017.  Negotiations collapsed when the company refused to lower its price, citing major capital spent on plant maintenance and overhauls. Consequently, the state abandoned the acquisition and resumed short-term capacity payments, eventually paying out far more in operational extensions than the outright valuation of the asset.

Bringing a definitive end to the long-running controversy, the newly unbundled state utility formally notified the management of Ace Power Embilipitiya on January 30, 2025, that power purchases from the plant would no longer be required. Following a pivotal Committee on Public Enterprises hearing on September 24, 2025, parliamentary oversight members recommended a full forensic audit into the emergency energy procurements and capacity charges paid to the facility, aiming to establish accountability for the systematic reliance on emergency thermal power that drained state coffers for years.
