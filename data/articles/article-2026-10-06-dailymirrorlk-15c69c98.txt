Sri Lanka’s BRICS strategy must begin at home

Practical cooperation, stronger institutions and diverse partnerships should guide the country’s approach to a changing world order

Sri Lanka’s approach to BRICS should begin with a simple question: What can engagement deliver for its people and economy?

Debates about international groupings often revolve around membership, diplomatic recognition and the balance of global power. For Sri Lanka, however, the more pressing concerns are practical: opening markets, attracting useful expertise, strengthening institutions and preserving the freedom to make independent decisions.

A seat at an international table can create opportunities. Turning those opportunities into results requires preparation at home.

These questions emerged during Spark Inbox’s discussion, After BRICS 2026: The Global South, Europe, Asia and the United States in a Changing World Order. Contributions from Imran Furkan and Dr. Bipin Ghimire offered different perspectives on international cooperation, but pointed towards a shared concern: a country’s external influence depends significantly on its domestic capabilities.

Furkan challenged the idea of the Global South as a unified political actor, emphasising that countries ultimately pursue their own interests. His argument raises an important consideration for Sri Lanka. Shared identities and diplomatic language may help bring countries together, but they do not guarantee common priorities or support when interests diverge.

Ghimire highlighted the demand for greater representation and the value of collective bargaining. Countries can find common ground even when their interests are not identical. Cooperation on specific issues can give smaller states opportunities that would be harder to secure individually.

Sri Lanka should take both arguments seriously. It can participate in collective initiatives while carefully assessing the interests of each partner and the benefits of each proposal.

The practical test is whether engagement improves the country’s ability to address its development needs.

That requires a clearer distinction between diplomatic activity and economic achievement. Meetings, declarations and expressions of interest are useful steps, but their value depends on what follows. A resolved certification problem for exporters, a functioning research partnership or a completed project offers stronger evidence of progress than an announcement alone.

The same discipline should guide any consideration of formal BRICS participation. Sri Lanka should establish what additional opportunities a proposed relationship would provide, which are already available through existing channels, and what financial and administrative obligations would follow.

Political engagement, bilateral cooperation and development financing also require separate assessments. Access to a diplomatic forum should never be presented as an automatic route to funding. Any financing proposal needs scrutiny of its costs, repayment implications, implementation requirements and long-term value.

Furkan emphasised functioning institutions and economic development. Ghimire similarly linked international influence to economic strength, political stability and the ability to make useful contributions. Dr. Suresh Marcandan’s intervention added the importance of diplomatic credibility and capable representation.

For Sri Lanka, this means investing in the work that makes international agreements effective: preparing credible proposals, negotiating clear terms, assigning responsibility and following through on commitments.

A focused programme over the next twelve months could translate these principles into action.

The first step should be a 90-day assessment of Sri Lanka’s objectives, existing commitments and available cooperation channels. This would establish where new engagement could add value and where existing relationships already offer workable solutions.

Alongside that assessment, Sri Lanka could prepare three modest, costed proposals.

One could address a specific obstacle faced by exporters or service providers, such as documentation, certification or access to buyers. Another could connect a local climate resilience need with international expertise. A third could support skills development, research or digital services tied to demonstrated demand.

Each proposal should identify its intended beneficiaries, implementing institution, funding needs and measurable results. Up to two feasible pilots could then proceed, with quarterly reviews and a public assessment after twelve months.

These are recommendations arising from the policy analysis, rather than commitments made by the discussion’s participants or public authorities.

Diversification must also be treated seriously. Maintaining several international relationships is valuable when those relationships create usable alternatives in markets, finance, technology and expertise. Signing more agreements does little to reduce vulnerability if the country remains dependent on the same underlying sources.

Strategic autonomy therefore means having the practical capacity to make choices and sustain them. It requires credible alternatives abroad and capable institutions at home.

Sri Lanka should keep its options open, engage constructively with BRICS countries and maintain strong relationships across regions. Every initiative should face the same test: does it strengthen the country’s development prospects and capacity to act independently?

The measure of success will be what Sri Lanka can deliver through those relationships—and whether its citizens experience the benefits.
