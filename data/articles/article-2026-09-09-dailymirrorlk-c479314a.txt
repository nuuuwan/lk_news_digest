Why India and Sri Lanka need a new fisheries partnership

For India and Sri Lanka, the sea has never merely been geography. It has been livelihood, memory and civilisation. Fishing communities on either side of the Palk Strait have for generations inhabited a maritime world older than the political boundaries that now divide it.

Fish has nourished households, sustained coastal economies and created cultures whose relationship with the ocean cannot easily be reduced to coordinates on a map. The economic story is equally compelling. India today produces nearly 20 million metric tonnes of fish annually, supports the livelihoods of roughly 30 million fishers and fish farmers, and is the world's second-largest fish-producing country.

Sri Lanka, despite its vastly smaller size, produces over 410,000 metric tonnes annually, with fisheries supporting hundreds of thousands of livelihoods and remaining integral to its coastal economy and food system.

Yet there is an unmistakable irony. An ocean that historically connected the two societies has increasingly become a site of contestation. The fishermen dispute, bottom trawling, depleted coastal stocks and recurring arrests in the Palk Bay have made fisheries a persistent irritant in bilateral relations. But behind the language of territorial waters and maritime violations lies a human cost too easily forgotten. A fisherman at sea is not an abstraction of nationality. On both shores are families dependent upon an uncertain catch and households for whom a seized boat or interrupted fishing season can become an economic crisis.

Indian fishermen face arrest, detention and the loss of boats and livelihoods. Sri Lankan fishermen confront incursions, competition for stressed resources and the ecological consequences of destructive fishing practices. Economic anxiety on one shore cannot be solved by transferring it to the other.

The ecological stakes are equally serious. Intensive trawling can disturb seabed habitats, destroy juveniles and generate substantial bycatch. Severely damaged marine ecosystems may take years, and vulnerable habitats far longer, to recover. Climate change compounds this pressure by altering ocean temperatures, habitats and the distribution of fish. Political boundaries are fixed; fish and marine ecosystems are not. What India and Sri Lanka now require is something more ambitious: a reimagining of the entire India-Sri Lanka fisheries relationship as an economic, ecological and social partnership.

The starting point should be joint marine resource management. The two countries could undertake joint stock assessments and regularly exchange information on fish availability, spawning grounds, ocean conditions and ecosystem health.

Science-based seasonal restrictions or catch quotas for appropriate shared stocks could reduce overfishing while providing greater predictability to fishing communities. There is room to think even more ambitiously. Could carefully designed joint fishing zones become symbols of cooperation rather than contested access? Any such arrangement must respect sovereignty and establish clear areas, seasons, eligible vessels, sustainable gear and science-based limits. Carefully negotiated pilot zones could demonstrate that shared marine resources can be governed through rules and reciprocity rather than a recurring cycle of intrusion and arrest.

The institutional foundations already exist through the Joint Working Group on Fisheries and cooperation between maritime authorities. They could be strengthened through coordinated patrols, real-time communication, satellite-enabled vessel monitoring and faster exchange of information on illegal fishing, shifting the emphasis from reacting to violations to preventing them. Bottom trawling must remain central to this transition. Sri Lanka has prohibited mechanised bottom trawling, while India has been supporting alternatives through deep-sea fishing, restrictions on destructive practices, vessel modernisation, mariculture and seaweed cultivation. But ecological reform will endure only if fishing families are given economically viable alternatives.

Economics provides another opportunity. India brings scale in aquaculture, infrastructure, digital systems, cold chains and a vast market. Sri Lanka offers expertise India can learn from, particularly its experience in deep-sea tuna fishing, multi-day vessels, monitoring, sustainability compliance and demanding export markets. Partnership does not require one country to permanently be the teacher and the other the pupil. The social dimension deserves equal attention. Fisheries policy often sees the fisherman but misses the woman behind the value chain. Women across both countries are central to processing, trading, drying, shellfish collection and increasingly aquaculture. Connecting women-led cooperatives in seaweed cultivation, processing and marine entrepreneurship could turn seaweed diplomacy into household prosperity, combining livelihood diversification with a climate-resilient marine industry. Fish must also be understood not simply as an export commodity but as food security. In an era of climate uncertainty, disrupted supply chains and volatile food prices, affordable aquatic protein is strategic infrastructure.

This is why the next India-Sri Lanka economic corridor need not only run through ports, power cables or shipping lanes. It could be underwater: connecting joint marine resource management, aquaculture, tuna, seaweed, marine biotechnology, ocean research, cold chains and sustainable fisheries. Such a corridor would be more than commerce. It would recognise that the fisherman from Rameswaram and the fisherman from Jaffna may carry different flags, but ultimately depend upon the health of the same sea.

For too long, the Palk Strait has been discussed as a line separating fishermen. The more consequential question is whether one of the most difficult elements of the bilateral relationship can become one of its most innovative. The future cannot simply be about deciding who fishes where. It must also be about deciding what kind of ocean the next generation inherits. The sea between India and Sri Lanka need not become smaller. Their imagination of it must become larger.

Author Malika Pandey is a public policy expert with years of experience working across key ministries of the Government of India.
