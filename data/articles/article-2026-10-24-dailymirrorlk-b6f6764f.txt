Thilina Weerasinghe and the Dark Side  of Sri Lanka’s Smiling Face

Sri Lanka has two faces – the sunny, smiling face of welcome so highly valued by foreign visitors, and the grim, unsmiling one feared by the country’s own citizens. Thilina Weerasinghe’s ‘Maranaya Asala Nisalawa’ mirrors that dark side with unflinching honesty.

‘Maranaya Asala Nisalawa’ can be translated as ‘Deathly Still in Face of Death’ or ‘Unflinching Besides Death.’ But I don’t know if either of these translations captures the sombre mood of the original. Writing of this nature won’t tag with popular taste. People want to forget and move on. Instead of mass murders, the public finds the daily deluge of  horrific accidents on social media is vastly entertaining.

‘Maranaya Asala Nisalawa’ is an account of a writer-activist’s progress from her schoolgirl days to a mature, uncompromising activist

‘Maranaya Asala Nisalawa’ is more than an autobiography. Rather, it is an account of a writer-activist’s progress from her schoolgirl days to a mature, uncompromising activist who remains a fierce critic of a flawed, flayed system to this day.

To call her a feminist would be limiting. One may call her a woman’s rights activist who knows how to work with men. This is an unholy pilgrim’s progress through decades of ethnic riots, civil war, insurrection and bloody suppression.

What sets Thilina’s outlook apart from  run-of-the-mill political activism is her  unflinching commitment to intellectual honesty. But such a point of view is not always appreciated in a sphere where  views are often partisan,  coloured by personal loyalties and ties.

Times change, and priorities shift accordingly. Despite massive socio-economic upheaval, the past decade has not seen politically-motivated kidnappings, disappearances, death squads and ‘white van’ culture which haunted Sri Lanka  since the 1980s. Government death squads have been replaced by mafia killers, an unending source of social media entertainment. Mass graves are getting excavated, but people’s attention is elsewhere. The killing of majestic tusker Kawantissa is bigger news than a mass grave.

That is neither the fault of Kawantissa nor those who love Sri Lanka’s endangered elephants. Just as the police can’t be expected to protect citizens from extra-judicial murder, the Wild Life Department can’t be expected to protect elephants. The present director general’s statement following Kawantissa’s death, that it must be seen against the context of the human-elephant conflict, is proof of lacklustre official attitudes. This is supposed to be a people-first government (other forms of life second) with more and more people finding it hard to have three meals a day.

These are contradictions. The author has left out wild life, but she is pre-occupied with other contradictions. After a tsunami-like economic crash, people need to move on with new priorities. The country is counting on tourism for an upturn, and elephants are tourist premium. Tourists say (in general) what a beautiful country it is, and how genuinely friendly the people are. That’s the smiling face everyone wants to promote, the sunny side.

But ‘Maranaya Asala Nisalawa’ is a word portrait of the unsmiling, dark side. Thilina Weerasinghe is the sort of writer you either love or hate. There is no middle ground. People who can’t stomach this depressing narrative can fall back on sunnier accounts. The sunny side is real, not a fabrication. But the smiling face is the poster boy for Sri Lanka, not the dark side. That’s  something  that many would now rather forget.

Her narrative begins with two childhood memories – how fellow villagers caught an elderly couple caught stealing manioc from someone’s garden, and beat them up until the terrified woman defecated in her clothes.

This goes directly against the popular, established narrative of ‘Gami sundarathwaya’ (serenity of the villager). That narrative isn’t false. But it has been used to suppress the other, darker narratives as exemplified by Thilina’s childhood memory of harsh village justice for two hungry people.

Her other childhood memory is the story of Premawathi Manamperi, the 22-year-old beauty contest winner from Kataragama in the deep south who was raped, humiliated and murdered by some members of the army contingent sent to the deal with the first JVP uprising of April 1971.

In Premawathi’s case, the accused were arrested, tried and sentenced at a time when people had only a vague notion of human rights. But, as Thilina’s harrowing narrative demonstrates, such abuses didn’t stop there. Tamil schoolgirl Krishanthi Kumaraswami became a rape-murder victim at a northern military checkpoint in Jaffna in 1997.

Her narrative provides interesting, forgotten statistics – In 1977, when J. R. Jayewardene was elected as president, almost 25% of the population were at dire poverty levels, earning Rs. 50 a month on average. He was elected with a huge majority and high hopes. But his liberal economy created huge rich-poor gaps, forcing thousands into menial Middle East jobs, facing exploitation and abuse.

The author’s first memory of ethnic hatred came when she was just ten years old. She and her parents were travelling in a car driven by her father’s friend when they were stopped by armed thugs near Dematagoda railway station, looking for Tamils to beat up.

As a young woman about to enter university, she faced the same harrowing experience when thugs boarded the bus she was in, looking for Tamils.

But racism isn’t only looting, arson and murder. The author recalls how, while returning from Batticaloa with a Tamil friend and her daughter, a woman soldier who boarded the bus abusively threw away the peacock feather the little girl was playing with.

In July 1980, the government crushed a strike launched by twenty five trade unions who asked for a Rs. 300 per month salary raise. 40,000 workers lost their jobs.

She chose journalism as her profession, leading one prominent Sinhala newspaper editor to comment: “Look after your skin before anything else.”

A natural risk taker, Thilina often ignored this advice. Censorship blocked her article when she wrote about mothers lamenting their disappeared children. After one article, the editor of the women’s weekly Vanitha Withthi received a typed, unsigned note warning Thilina: “It’s people like you who should be burnt on tyres.”

It isn’t just official, state censorship she had to face. Major publishers and even some booksellers refused to promote her fiction and non-fiction as ‘immoral writing.’ One publisher asked her scathingly if she had a father.

One evening during the dark days of ‘terror’ in 1989, she received a parcel from a prominent photojournalist. It contained pictures of dead bodies on the road taken by him. The army was checking his neighbourhood. Panicking, he had sent the pictures to her, putting her life in danger.

But this book isn’t a record of personal betrayals. It’s a portrait of a larger betrayal – of the people by the rulers, who maintain power through rigid laws and the poverty-inducing economics.  But Thilina’s critical voice is equally harsh on everyone who try to impose their arbitrary laws on their fellow travellers – for example, her condemnation of university ragging and a group of raggers who caught a pair of fellow students having sex and punished them by pouring human excrement on them. She is equally critical of sexual repression and hypocrisy as she is of the political version.

In Jaffna, she secretly meets a soldier who admires her writing. The result is an outpouring of emotion which goes directly counter to the stone-faced ‘ranaviru’ concept that Sri Lankans  were weaned on during the civil war, especially during the final two decades.

This is not a book for the squeamish. It exposes a dark side which most people now want to forget.
