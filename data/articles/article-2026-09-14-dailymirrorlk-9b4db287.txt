Time to flush black money out of the system

Colombo, Sep. 14 (Daily Mirror) - Start with a managed demonetization and withdrawal of the Rs. 5,000 note, make it harder to hoard illicit cash, conduct anonymous transactions and keep money beyond the reach of the formal economy. Bring hidden cash into the banking system, widen the tax net, curb bribery and illicit transactions and strike at Sri Lanka’s shadow economy. This is not an untested experiment. Our big brother India and several other countries have already travelled this road, giving Sri Lanka a tested international playbook and the lessons to phase out its highest-value note.

Sri Lanka stands at a rare inflection point. Having clawed its way back from the worst economic collapse in its post-independence history, the country is now under intense scrutiny — from the IMF, from credit rating agencies, and from its own citizens — to prove that the reforms of the past two years were not merely a survival exercise, but the foundation of a fundamentally more honest economy. It is in this context that policymakers should seriously examine a measure that has quietly worked in other jurisdictions.

Withdrawing the Rs. 5,000 note, the highest denomination in circulation.

The Hidden Cost of High-Value Cash. Every economy has a shadow twin the undeclared, undocumented, untaxed transactions that run parallel to the formal one. In Sri Lanka, this shadow economy has long been sustained by the convenience of high-denomination currency. Large notes are compact, anonymous, and untraceable — precisely the qualities that make them ideal for hoarding unaccounted wealth, facilitating smuggling, and greasing informal channels such as undiyal-style money transfers that bypass the banking system entirely.

The Rs. 5,000 note, introduced for convenience, has arguably become a convenience for the wrong reasons. When a single note carries such purchasing power, it becomes the currency of choice not for the ordinary citizen buying groceries, but for those seeking to move or store large sums without a trace.

First, the IMF program demands it. Sri Lanka’s Extended Fund Facility is explicitly tied to governance and revenue-mobilization benchmarks. A tax-to-GDP ratio that has historically lagged regional peers cannot be fixed through new taxes alone if the base itself remains hidden in cash. Withdrawing the highest note is a structural nudge toward formalization one that costs the government nothing to implement and could meaningfully widen the tax net.

Second, public trust demands it. A visible, credible move against unaccounted wealth would resonate as substance, not symbolism, in a political climate still hungry for accountability.

Third, the digital rails already exist. The scaffolding for a smooth transition is more developed today than it was for comparable reforms elsewhere.

India has run the experiment twice, with sharply different designs and sharply different results.

2016: the shock therapy model. When India abruptly demonetized its Rs. 500 and Rs. 1,000 notes overnight, roughly 86% of currency in circulation by value vanished from legal use with almost no warning.

The chaos that followed cash shortages, long bank queues, disruption to daily-wage and small-business activity is now a textbook case in what not to do. More tellingly for the “black money” objective ie; nearly all of the demonetized currency eventually came back into the banking system, which many economists took as evidence that hoarders found ways to launder rather than lose their cash. The core lesson was not that currency reform doesn’t work, it was that a surprise, blanket shock imposes enormous short-term pain on ordinary citizens while giving well-resourced evaders time and incentive to find workarounds.

2023: the gradual withdrawal model. India’s approach to phasing out its Rs. 2,000 note was the opposite in design. The Reserve Bank of India announced the withdrawal in May 2023 but kept the note as legal tender, gave the public a long window to deposit or exchange notes at bank branches, and kept RBI issue offices open for exchange well beyond the initial deadline even accepting notes by post. The result was an orderly, low-drama transition: by early 2026, roughly 98% of the withdrawn currency had been returned to the banking system, with no queues, no liquidity panic, and no measurable disruption to economic activity.

Notably, this was also a case where the note being withdrawn was itself a high-value denomination introduced to remonetize the economy quickly after 2016, meaning India effectively used its own history to correct course, reducing the very anonymity-friendly denomination that shock therapy had reintroduced.

The takeaway for Sri Lanka is unambiguous. If the goal is to curb unaccounted cash without repeating India’s 2016 trauma, the design should borrow directly from India’s 2023 playbook a clear withdrawal announcement, an extended and well-publicized exchange window, continued legal-tender status during the transition, multiple deposit/exchange channels (including for those without easy access to bank branches), and no sudden invalidation of the note’s value. The objective is not to punish cash holders overnight it is to make holding large sums outside the banking system progressively less convenient, while giving legitimate holders every reasonable opportunity to formalize their money.

A carefully managed withdrawal of the Rs. 5,000 note could deliver benefits far beyond simply changing the currency in people’s wallets. By encouraging large cash holdings to move into the banking system, it could bring previously invisible money into the formal economy, widen the tax base, improve tax compliance and strengthen government revenue without imposing yet another new tax on those already paying their fair share. The impact could extend well beyond taxation. Bribery and corruption, under-the-table commissions, undeclared business transactions, underworld payments, illegal gambling, smuggling, illicit trade, money laundering, tax evasion, informal foreign-exchange dealings and other cash-based criminal activity could become significantly more difficult when large amounts of money can no longer be conveniently stored, transported or exchanged in high-denomination notes.

It would not eliminate such activities altogether, but it could substantially increase the inconvenience, risk and physical volume involved in conducting large illicit cash transactions, while pushing more legitimate economic activity towards traceable banking and digital channels.

The benefits could also extend to the external economy. Greater use of regulated channels could help redirect remittances and foreign currency away from informal networks and into the official banking system, supporting foreign-exchange liquidity and reserves.

Increased bank deposits could strengthen financial-system liquidity and potentially expand the pool of funds available for productive lending, business expansion and investment. At the same time, greater adoption of digital payments would create clearer transaction trails, improve financial transparency and accelerate Sri Lanka’s transition towards a modern, less cash-dependent economy.

If implemented gradually, intelligently and without disrupting legitimate commerce, withdrawing the Rs. 5,000 note could become part of a broader national drive to formalize the economy, attack corruption and illicit finance, widen the tax net, strengthen foreign-exchange flows, improve financial transparency and ensure that the burden of rebuilding Sri Lanka is shared more fairly.

Several countries have already moved to eliminate or reduce their highest-value banknotes as part of broader efforts to modernize their payment systems and make large anonymous cash transactions less convenient. Denmark offers one of the most relevant recent examples. In 2023, it announced the withdrawal of its 1,000-kroner note its highest denomination. Danish authorities specifically pointed to the disproportionate use of the note in financial crime.

The European Central Bank similarly stopped issuing the €500 note because of concerns that it could facilitate illicit activity, while Canada has removed its CAN$1,000 bill from legal-tender circulation. Nigeria’s Naira 2022–23 currency exercise also sought to tackle the enormous volume of cash being hoarded outside the banking system. This will protect the ordinary citizens and the legitimate business and minimize the financial crime in the future.

If Sri Lanka is serious about fighting corruption, black money, tax evasion, underworld cash and illicit transactions, take away one of their greatest convenience.

“Don’t keep taxing the people already inside the system and the genuine citizens” Go after the hidden economy instead of repeatedly squeezing compliant taxpayers.
