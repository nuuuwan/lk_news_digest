Can Sri Lankan Police arrest you for not carrying an NIC?

Colombo, Aug. 5 (Daily Mirror) - A widespread belief among the public is that failing to carry a National Identity Card (NIC) in public is an offense that automatically leads to arrest. However, according to Attorney-at-Law Inspector of Police P. Amitha Abeysekara Silva from the Police Legal Division, simply not possessing an ID card is not an independent criminal offense under Sri Lankan law.

Under the Registration of Persons Act No. 32 of 1968 and the Police Ordinance, police officers hold the legal authority to question a person's identity whenever necessary for official duties, such as preventing a crime or conducting an investigation. While the lack of an ID on its own is not a crime, if an officer already has reasonable suspicion regarding an individual under specific circumstances, the inability to produce identification may reinforce that suspicion and provide legitimate grounds for arrest.

Police also said that under Section 33 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, an officer may arrest a person without a warrant if they refuse to disclose their name and residential address or intentionally provide false information when accused of or suspected of committing a non-cognizable offense.

They said that the purpose of such an arrest is solely to verify the individual's identity. Once their name and place of residence are verified, the police are required by law to release the individual on bail.

Frequently Asked Questions: Carrying a National Identity Card in Sri Lanka

1. Is it illegal to go out without carrying a National Identity Card in Sri Lanka?

No. Simply failing to carry a National Identity Card (NIC) in public is not an independent criminal offence under Sri Lankan law.

2. Can the police arrest someone solely for not carrying an NIC?

No. A person cannot be arrested solely because they do not have their NIC with them. However, the absence of identification may become relevant if the police already have reasonable grounds to suspect the person of an offence.

3. Can police officers ask a person to prove their identity?

Yes. Under the Registration of Persons Act No. 32 of 1968 and the Police Ordinance, police officers may question a person’s identity when necessary for an official duty, such as preventing a crime or conducting an investigation.

If a police officer already has reasonable suspicion about a person based on specific circumstances, that person’s inability to provide identification may reinforce the suspicion and contribute to lawful grounds for arrest.

5. Must a person provide their name and address to the police?

A person accused or suspected of committing a non-cognizable offence must provide their correct name and residential address when requested by the police.

6. Can someone be arrested for refusing to provide their name and address?

Yes. Under Section 33 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, a police officer may arrest without a warrant a person accused or suspected of a non-cognizable offence if they refuse to disclose their name and residential address.

7. What happens if a person gives the police false identity information?

A police officer may arrest the person without a warrant if they intentionally provide a false name or residential address while accused or suspected of committing a non-cognizable offence.

8. Why can the police arrest someone who refuses to identify themselves?

The purpose of such an arrest is to verify the person’s identity and place of residence. It is not a punishment for failing to carry an NIC.

9. What happens after the police verify the person’s identity?

Once the person’s name and residential address have been verified, the police are legally required to release the individual on bail.

10. Does every request for identification mean that a person is suspected of a crime?

No. Police officers may request identity information while carrying out legitimate official duties. Whether an arrest is lawful depends on the circumstances and the existence of legally recognised grounds.

11. What is the key difference between not carrying an NIC and refusing to identify yourself?

Not carrying an NIC is not, by itself, a criminal offence. Refusing to provide your name and address, or deliberately giving false information in circumstances covered by Section 33 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, may result in arrest for identity verification.

Sri Lankan law does not make the mere failure to carry an NIC an independent criminal offence. Nevertheless, carrying valid identification may make it easier to confirm your identity if questioned by the police during a lawful inquiry.
