A cancer patient’s struggle

There is something  cruel about asking a cancer patient to come back tomorrow when the medicine needed to keep that person alive is not available today.

For many Sri Lankans battling cancer, the journey to a government hospital begins long before sunrise. Patients and their families travel from distant areas, often after spending money they can barely afford on bus or train fares.

They stand in queues. They sit on hospital floors. They wait outside pharmacies and clinics, sometimes for hours. And after all that waiting, some are told that the essential drug they need is simply not available.

What are they expected to do? Go home and wait? Find the money to buy it privately? Or simply hope that their illness will wait until the State can provide the medicine?

Cancer does not wait. It does not understand shortages, procurement delays, tenders or bureaucratic paperwork. But a poor patient has nowhere else to turn. This is why the shortage of essential cancer medicines cannot be treated as just another problem within Sri Lanka’s health sector. Sri Lanka once took pride in having a healthcare system where a person’s ability to pay did not determine whether he or she received treatment. Free healthcare has been one of the country’s greatest achievements. Yet every time a patient walks into a hospital and walks out without an essential medicine, that promise is weakened.

There are also legitimate concerns about the quality of medicines entering the country. When shortages become acute, the temptation can be to find supplies wherever they are available and as quickly as possible. But speed cannot come at the expense of safety. Every medicine entering Sri Lanka must meet the highest possible standards.

This is particularly important when medicines are sourced from countries where manufacturing standards and regulatory systems may vary between suppliers. The issue should never be about a country. It should be about whether the medicine is safe, effective and of the required quality. Sri Lanka must be uncompromising when it comes to medicines.

At the same time, perhaps the bigger lesson is that we cannot forever depend almost entirely on others for medicines needed to treat our most vulnerable citizens. We have  talented scientists, pharmacists, entrepreneurs and professionals. We have universities, research institutions and a growing private sector. What we have lacked for years is a consistent, serious and long-term national policy to build a strong pharmaceutical manufacturing industry.

The present Government now has an opportunity to do what its predecessors failed to do. It should identify the medicines that are most critical to the country, encourage local production, provide incentives for credible pharmaceutical manufacturers and create partnerships between the State, universities and the private sector.

If Sri Lanka can produce  a significant proportion of its essential medicines locally, it would reduce our vulnerability to international supply disruptions, foreign exchange shortages and global price fluctuations. We have seen what happens when healthcare is treated as an afterthought. The period under former Health Minister Keheliya Rambukwella remains a particularly painful chapter in Sri Lanka’s recent health sector history. Sri Lanka cannot afford to repeat those failures. The Government must understand that healthcare is not merely another ministry, another budget line or another administrative file.

Behind every shortage is a human being. It is the mother waiting for chemotherapy. The father who has travelled from a village hoping to collect his medication. The daughter who quietly counts the money in her purse because she knows her family cannot afford to buy the medicine privately.

And the elderly patient who returns home after spending an entire day at a hospital -- these people are not asking for luxury. They are asking for medicine. A government that wants to earn the trust of its people must understand that healthcare is one of the clearest measures of whether the State truly cares.

The Government must act now: secure essential supplies, strengthen quality control, investigate shortages, punish those responsible for malpractice and build a pharmaceutical industry capable of producing critical medicines locally.

And a cancer patient should never have to leave a hospital empty-handed simply because the system failed to provide what was needed.
