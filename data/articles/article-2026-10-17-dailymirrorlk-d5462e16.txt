Exporters push back against separate freight charges

Urge authorities against amending or rescinding Gazette No. 2334/26 of March 31, 2023

Sri Lanka’s exporters have urged the government to preserve the existing freight pricing safeguards, warning that a return to separate terminal handling charges (THC) would burden trade with additional costs and expose the consumers to higher prices.

The National Chamber of Exporters of Sri Lanka (NCE) yesterday called for the retention of the all-inclusive freight framework, insisting that the container handling costs should remain within the rate negotiated with the shipping lines rather than be imposed as an additional local charge.

The NCE specifically urged the authorities against amending or rescinding Gazette No. 2334/26 of March 31, 2023, which it said remains in force.

The appeal highlights the stakes for the exporters competing in the overseas markets and the importers whose shipping expenses feed into the cost of goods sold locally. An additional charge outside the agreed freight rate would raise the costs for the businesses, with the burden eventually reaching the consumers, the NCE warned. Under the existing framework, the container handling costs must be incorporated into a single freight rate agreed between the shipping line and party contracting for the service. The THC covers the handling of containers at a port, including their movement on and off a vessel.

The NCE’s concern is that a separately imposed THC would reopen the door to the fragmented charging system that prevailed before 2013, weakening the businesses’ ability to negotiate and establish their full shipping costs in advance.

According to the NCE, the importers and exporters previously faced nearly 50 separate charges, many of which varied and were imposed on the parties with little influence over how they were determined.

The all-inclusive framework was introduced in December 2013 and strengthened in 2017. However, the subsequent attempts to revise the arrangements have kept the freight pricing a contentious issue.

The NCE said the tariff changes introduced in October 2022, at the request of the local service providers, prompted legal challenges from the stakeholders. Following further changes and public pressure, the protection was restored through the March 2023 gazette.

The NCE also drew attention to the treatment of handling the expenses under the Sri Lanka Ports Authority’s own tariff.

It said the tariff does not use the term “Terminal Handling Charge” and requires the vessel stevedoring costs to be paid directly to the port by the shipping line. Those costs should be incorporated into the all-inclusive freight payment, instead of being recovered from the exporters and importers through a separate charge, the NCE argued.

The NCE stressed that the framework does not prevent the service providers from collecting the legitimate payments. It requires those expenses to be covered within the agreed freight arrangement, giving the contracting parties the opportunity to negotiate the price and terms of the service.

For the businesses, the distinction determines whether the negotiated freight rate represents the full cost or leaves room for additional local demands.

The NCE maintained that retaining the existing safeguards would support transparent freight pricing, greater cost certainty for trade and protection against additional expenses passing through the supply chains to the consumers.
