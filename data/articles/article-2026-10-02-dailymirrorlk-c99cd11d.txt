The age of longevity: Is Sri Lanka ready for longer lives?

As Sri Lankans live longer, the country faces a question that goes far beyond healthcare and pensions: How can we ensure that the additional years of life are lived with health, security, independence and dignity?

“The Age of Longevity: Rethinking Systems for Longer Lives” is the international theme of the 2026 International Day of Older Persons. For Sri Lanka, the theme comes at a significant moment.

According to the 2024 Census, people aged 60 and above account for 18% of Sri Lanka’s population, while children below 15 account for 20.7%. There are now 87 older persons for every 100 children.

These figures point to a profound demographic transformation. As the number of children declines and the older population increases, relationships between generations are changing, both within families and across society.

This transformation affects almost every aspect of daily life — from healthcare and employment to housing, transport, family responsibilities and community life.

The challenge is therefore not simply to help people live longer. It is to make longer lives better lives.

Older people are often treated as though they form a single group with identical needs. They do not.

Many older people remain independent, make their own decisions and contribute actively to their families and communities. Some need assistance with particular activities, while others require regular or intensive care.

Growing older should not mean losing the freedom to maintain friendships, pursue interests, learn new skills or manage one’s own affairs. Accessible transport, health information, opportunities for learning and practical guidance on finances and public services can all help older people maintain their independence.

Support, when required, should empower older people rather than take control of their lives.

Active ageing should also not be misunderstood as an expectation that everyone must remain employed or constantly occupied. For one person, continuing to work may be important. For another, it may be gardening, volunteering, spending time with grandchildren, engaging in religious or spiritual activities, learning something new — or simply enjoying rest.

The relationship between older parents and their adult children remains one of Sri Lanka’s greatest sources of social support.

A conversation, a visit, sharing a meal or involving parents in family decisions can provide meaningful companionship and reassurance.

Many households have fewer children. Adult children may live in another district or overseas. In other families, both spouses work while raising children and supporting ageing parents. Middle-aged adults may consequently find themselves caring for two generations at the same time.

These pressures should be recognised rather than interpreted as a failure of family responsibility.

A son or daughter may genuinely want to accompany a parent to every medical appointment but be unable to take repeated leave from work. A family may want an older parent to remain at home but struggle to arrange assistance during working hours.

Reliable home and community care services could help families manage these challenges while allowing them to spend more meaningful time together.

At the same time, support cannot depend entirely on having children or relatives nearby.

There are older people who have no children, whose children live abroad or far away, or whose families are unable to provide regular assistance. As a society, Sri Lanka must ensure that their access to essential support does not depend solely on their family circumstances, income or place of residence.

The 2024 Census recorded more than 370,000 people aged 60 and above living alone, with women accounting for the majority.

Living alone does not necessarily mean being lonely. Many older people value their independence and are comfortable living by themselves.

The more important question is whether they have someone to turn to when they become ill, whether they maintain regular contact with others and whether they can participate in community life.

Loneliness can also affect older people who live with their families.

An older person may spend most of the day alone while other household members are at work or school. The death of a spouse or close friends can further reduce social contact. Difficulties with mobility or transport may prevent people from participating in activities they once enjoyed.

Neighbours, community organisations, religious institutions and local authorities can play an important role. Regular visits, telephone calls, accessible community gatherings and transport assistance can make a significant difference.

Older people should also be given opportunities to shape community activities and contribute their knowledge, experience and skills.

Improving day centres for older persons is one practical way Sri Lanka could strengthen community-based support.

A day centre should be more than a place where an older person spends the day. It should be a place where people want to meet others, learn, participate and feel valued.

Older people themselves should have a say in what these centres provide.

Activities could include music, reading, exercise, gardening, crafts, shared meals, digital learning and social gatherings. Centres could also provide health information and create opportunities for older and younger generations to interact.

Such facilities could also provide reassurance to working family members who worry about an elderly parent spending long hours alone.

But accessibility is critical. Older people with difficulties in walking, hearing or seeing should also be able to participate. In some areas, safe and affordable transport may be necessary.

Ultimately, the success of a day centre should be measured not simply by the number of people who attend, but by whether older people feel respected and want to return.

Longer life does not necessarily mean a life free from illness.

The 2024 Census found that more than half of Sri Lankans aged 60 and above have at least one chronic disease.

Many older people live with several conditions simultaneously and may also experience changes in mobility, hearing, vision or memory.

This requires a healthcare system that supports people continuously rather than one that focuses only on treating individual illnesses when they arise.

Strong primary healthcare, regular medication reviews, rehabilitation, mental health support, hearing and vision services, nutritional advice and assistance with daily functioning can help people remain independent for longer.

Healthcare providers should ask not only, “What illness do you have?” but also, “What do you want to continue doing?”

With appropriate treatment, assistive devices and supportive environments, many people can continue living active and fulfilling lives while managing chronic conditions.

For some people, ageing eventually brings a need for assistance with bathing, dressing, mobility, medicines or meals.

Home and community care services require trained workers, proper supervision and clear standards. Family members who provide intensive unpaid care also need information, guidance and occasional relief.

Most importantly, care arrangements should involve the older person whenever possible.

What kind of assistance do they want? What can they still do independently? Where would they prefer to receive care?

Needing assistance should never mean losing one’s dignity or one’s voice in decisions about one’s own life.

Longer lives also require us to reconsider traditional ideas about employment and retirement.

Some older Sri Lankans wish to continue working because they enjoy their professions and want to use their experience. Others continue working because they need an income. Some prefer to leave paid employment but remain active through volunteering, teaching, family responsibilities or community work.

Those who wish to continue working should have fair opportunities, suitable working conditions and opportunities to update their skills. Those who cannot work need adequate income security.

We must also recognise years of unpaid work spent caring for children, relatives and households — work that may not have resulted in wages or a pension.

The circumstances of older women require particular attention. Some have had fewer opportunities to accumulate savings and may live alone following the death of a spouse.

The Census shows a much higher proportion of widowhood among women than men aged 65 and above. Their circumstances must therefore be considered when planning income support, housing, care and community services.

Independence is influenced not only by a person’s health but also by the environment in which they live.

Can an older person safely walk to a shop or clinic? Is there somewhere to sit while waiting for a bus? Can they cross a busy road safely? Are public buildings and toilets accessible?

These may seem like small issues, but they determine whether older people can continue participating in everyday life.

Homes may also need simple modifications. Stairs, inadequate lighting and slippery bathrooms can turn a familiar home into a hazardous environment.

Planning safer homes can help people remain in their own communities for longer.

Online appointments, payments and government services may be convenient for many people, including some older users. Others may require assistance or a simple alternative to access services in person.

A service is truly accessible only when the people who need it can actually use it.

Younger and older people may have different expectations about employment, family life, technology and independence. Misunderstandings can grow when there are few opportunities for generations to interact.

Schools, universities, community organisations and day centres can help bridge this gap.

Young people can share digital knowledge, while older people can contribute professional experience, local history, languages, crafts and practical knowledge.

Such interactions reinforce an important message: older people are not simply recipients of care. They are also contributors to society.

The International Day of Older Persons should be an opportunity not only to celebrate older people, but also to listen to them.

Older people should have a meaningful role in identifying problems and designing solutions. Their needs vary according to health, income, gender, location and family circumstances.

A programme that works in Colombo may require a different approach in a rural community.

Sri Lanka can begin with practical measures — asking older residents what they need, supporting those who wish to remain independent, identifying people with little regular support, improving day centres according to users’ preferences, strengthening home and community care, and making healthcare, transport, housing and public services easier to use.

Government institutions, local authorities, healthcare providers, employers, community organisations and families all have a role to play.

Sri Lanka has long valued strong family bonds. The task ahead is not to replace those bonds, but to strengthen them with dependable systems of support.

A longer life should give every person the opportunity to make choices, maintain relationships, receive assistance when needed and live with dignity.
