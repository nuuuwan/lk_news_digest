Mild punishments may not break the cycle of sadistic university ragging

By 2019, around 16 university students from different universities had taken their own lives as a result of ragging

The dream to pursue higher education at a state university has been challenged due to the prevalence of the sadistic rag culture. What began as a practice for socialising purposes with mostly humour and pranks quickly worsened to severe forms of bullying, harassment, sexual and gender-based violence and torture. As a result, some students,  or rather victims,  became permanently crippled or took their own lives as they couldn’t bear the trauma and humiliation.

By 2019, around 16 university students from different universities had taken their own lives as a result of ragging. Some died of kidney failure caused by extreme ragging. A student from Hardy Technical Institute died from kidney failure due to extreme exercise and being forced to consume excessive quantities of liquor. Studies indicate that as many as 2000 students, a substantial number,  dropout annually due to extreme forms of ragging in state universities.

Charith Dilshan from Sabaragamuwa university faced a similar plight in April 2023. A few hours after the university Avurudu ceremony, Charith became a victim of bullying, harassment and torture. His batch mates revealed how he was stripped naked in public and how the perpetrators kept banging his head on a wall. He was pricked using a safety pin and was ultimately thrown inside a cupboard. Several other batch mates had faced a similar situation and had lodged a complaint with the police.

Three years later, following prolonged investigations, 11 perpetrators of this crime were expelled from the university on Wednesday. The earliest recorded incident of ragging was reported from the then Vidyalankara Pirivena or the Kelaniya University in 1974 where a trainee mathematics teachers was subjected to ragging. According to media reports, 12 undergraduates were expelled and four officials were penalised for failing to take appropriate action.

It is clear that expelling and penalising officials alone won’t resolve this horrible act of bonding and socialising. Stricter regulations and enforcement of the law is required to discourage brutal forms of ragging in state universities. Some even suggest that perpetrators should be named and shamed and they should never be able to find a job placement for the crime that they have done.

It is unfortunate to note that ragging continues in state universities despite it being recognised as a serious criminal offense under the Prohibition of Ragging and Other Forms of Violence in Educational Institutions Act No. 20 of 1998. The law applies both inside and outside of school and university property. In 2010 the University Grants Commission (UGC) released Circular No. 919 with guidelines to curb the ragging menace in universities and higher education institutes. In 2016, the UGC established the Centre for Gender Equity/ Equality (CGEE) and Prevention of Sexual and Gender-Based Violence (SGBV) and Ragging. In 2017, a 24-hour helpline was set up by the UGC as a complaining mechanism to assist students who are in distress or actively facing violence. The helpline received a considerable total of 557 complaints between 2017-2018.

Findings of a 2022 study on prevalence of ragging and sexual and gender-based violence in Sri Lankan universities revealed that the perpetrators have traits such as belonging to low income households coming from rural backgrounds, staying largely in university dormitories, and having had a troubled upbringing. A common notion is that ragging is part of the university subculture making the campus a space for ‘social leveling.’ Findings of the study also revealed that university subcultures are made up of rural and urban class divisions that have resulted in the formation of two subcultures, with urban students opposing the subculture that uses ragging as the main tool to transmit a particular subculture.

Researchers observe an element of class jealousy on the part of seniors who engage in ragging in Sri Lankan universities, reflecting social deviancy. Another hypothesis regarding ragging in Sri Lankan universities is that it is associated with university student politics.

Ragging has taken away many innocent lives and it has discouraged many bright students from pursuing higher education and fulfilling their dreams. Sadistic forms of ragging should be banned from state universities if Sri Lanka wants to produce more intellectuals from her own soil.
