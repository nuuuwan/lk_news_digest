Saudi  execution of Bangladeshi couple sparks global outrage

Visitors need to respect the laws of any given  country they visit or work in,  just as its own citizens do

Bangladeshi government says it wasn’t given prior notice of the executions and learnt of the event via news reports

A major row has erupted over the execution of two Bangladeshi nationals, a man and a woman, in Saudi Arabia on October 14. Saudi Arabia’s interior ministry said they were executed for committing an ‘immoral act’ inside a mosque.

No details of this ‘immoral act’ have been made clear. Some unconfirmed reports say it was kissing, while others claim it was adultery.

Bangladeshi government says it wasn’t given prior notice of the executions and learnt of the event via news reports.

What is clear is that they were sentenced to death under an Islamic Law called Ta’zir, under which the judge has the discretion to decide on both offence and sentence.

It isn’t even clear if this is a married couple. There has been a deluge of social media misinformation which makes it very hard to get a clear picture. Claims that it was a honeymoon couple have been refuted. Bogus social media pictures of an Egyptian couple – the husband kissing his pregnant wife’s belly inside what looks like a mosque – are circulating, claiming to be images of the executed Bangladeshis.

Now it looks as if the two, Nazrul Islam and Siti Akhtar, have been working in Saudi Arabia for some time. Their ages and marital status has not been confirmed. What is confirmed is that both were executed on Oct. 14 under Islamic law for committing an unspecified ‘immoral act’ inside a mosque. The general consensus is that it was a kiss.

Bangladesh has demanded an explanation. But the country has over three million workers in Saudi Arabia, and is not in a position to take any strong action.

What is clear is that Bangladesh was not given the time to take any diplomatic action against the execution order, unlike in Sri Lanka’s case when Sri Lankan worker Anojan Sivarasa who was sentenced to death by the Saudi appellate court for alleged blasphemy committed by a  social media post. He was sentenced to death, but the sentence was commuted after a strenuous diplomatic effort by Sri Lanka, and now his case is back in the initial court where he was earlier sentenced to five years in prison.

In most countries, adultery is a case for civil courts and does not carry a death sentence (except for several Islamic countries). In the US, however, A few states (such as Wisconsin, Michigan, Minnesota, and Idaho) still have active laws  that classify adultery as a misdemeanor or felony, though actual criminal prosecution is extremely rare.

Kissing is not a legal offense around the world, including Sri Lanka. But police here use antiquated vagrancy laws to prosecute couples for ‘immoral behaviour’ in public places. Except for several Islamic countries such as Saudi Arabia, conditions wary widely – In China, according to custom, public displays of affection (PDAs) are not the norm. At the University of Zimbabwe, students can be expelled for PDA. In Indonesia, usually a liberal country, kissing or even handholding between couples in public is not accepted.

Thailand is famous for its sex industry, but it is a conservative country and kissing in public is not allowed. In the Middle East, PDAs including kissing in public between opposite genders is now allowed, though family members and  friends can kiss on the cheeks as a gesture of affection.

But any PDA inside a place of worship is usually not allowed, and this is not limited to Islam. Kissing the bride during a Christian wedding ceremony is allowed in Western countries, but all religions expect the sanctity of their places of worship to be respected. Imagine a couple caught kissing or in any other ‘immoral act’ inside a Buddhist temple in Sri Lanka. They will be prosecuted under civil laws. In extreme cases, monks and their faithful may even take the law into their hands and  shame the couple.

But the question the world is asking now – why were the two Bangladeshis executed, even if their actions inside the mosque clearly amounted to sacrilege? Isn’t that too harsh? They could have been fined, jailed, or deported,.

Visitors need to respect the laws of any given  country they visit or work in,  just as its own citizens do. But outsiders have the right to question the severity of the punishment in proportion to the crime committed.

Saudi Arabia has executed more than 1000 people since 2020, with annual totals fluctuating due to temporary moratoriums followed by a major surge in recent years.

On December 21, 2025, the European Saudi Organisation for Human Rights (ESOHR) reported that Saudi Arabia carried out 347 executions in 2025,  after a record-breaking 345 executions in 2024. The majority (79%) of executions in 2025 were for crimes failing to meet the international legal threshold of a “most serious crime” and predominately included executions for drug-related charges (69% of all executions).

Due to a lack of transparency, the numbers reported by ESOHR are minimum counts. ESOHR noted that Saudi Arabia’s continued expanded use of the death penalty contradicts previous promises made by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman  to limit its use in the Kingdom.

Fifty Seven per cent of all those executed in 2025 were foreigners.  Of the 202 foreigners executed in Saudi Arabia in 2025, 94% were convicted of non-lethal drug-related offenses. According to ESOHR, their trials often featured due process violations. Reporting by The Guardian UK in November 2025 shed light on some of these violations for a group of Egyptian migrants held in the “death wing” of Tabuk prison in northern Saudi Arabia.

Their families said  confessions were given under torture, and the accused often could not afford legal representation. Previously, prisoners would receive notice of their imminent execution and be afforded the opportunity to call their families, but recently, some families have reported only being notified of a loved one’s execution after the fact. This is precisely what happened in the case of the two executed Bangladeshis. Even the government was not given prior notice.

With Dubai losing its lustre as a top Mideastern tourist destination, Saudi Arabia is investing heavily to lure millions of tourists to the Red Sea. The Red Sea Destination is a massive, luxury regenerative tourism megaproject along the country’s west coast.

But is Saudi Arabia ready to apply the same unforgiving laws to those wealthy tourists who would be mainly from the West? Or is it going to apply a different standard? Executing two poor Bangladeshis from a nation struggling to develop is one thing – doing that for millionaire types throwing around money into Saudi coffers would be another.
