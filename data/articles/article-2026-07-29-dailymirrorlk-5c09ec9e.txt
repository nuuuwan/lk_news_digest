Reclaiming the Republic: Why True Judicial Efficiency Demands Systemic Reform Over Retirement Age Extensions

A fundamental mistake in political thinking is confusing a raw democratic majority with a true constitutional republic. Securing a two-thirds majority in Parliament grants a government the legal authority to pass legislation, but it does not give it the moral authority to rewrite the rules of governance at will.

In a pure democracy, majoritarian power can quickly become numbers-driven control, where 66% of lawmakers can override fundamental principles simply because they have the votes. In contrast, republicanism is built on the principle that certain values—such as the rule of law, the separation of powers, and institutional checks and balances—are higher than any majority. The judiciary exists specifically to protect individual rights and the Constitution from majoritarian overreach. When a government uses its raw parliamentary numbers to alter judicial structures mid-stream, it elevates majoritarian force above republican values.

A constitution is meant to be a stable framework, not a draft document that changes whenever a new administration takes office. For a constitutional amendment to be legitimate, it must follow two essential principles:

1. Prospective Application: Any change to judicial service conditions or retirement ages must apply only to future appointees who take office after the law is passed. Applying extensions to sitting judges creates an unavoidable appearance of political patronage.

2. The "Veil of Ignorance": Sound governance requires lawmakers to write structural rules as if they do not know whether they will be in power or in opposition when those rules take effect.

If an extension applies to current judges, it changes the rules mid-game. Even if done with no ill intent, it creates a subtle incentive structure where judges approaching retirement could be perceived as aligning with the executive to secure an extension.

Sri Lanka’s political history offers a clear, repeated warning about tailoring constitutional rules to suit immediate circumstances. Successive governments have used their parliamentary majorities to pass ad-hoc amendments to solve short-term administrative or political challenges:

• The 18th Amendment (2010) removed presidential term limits to consolidate executive control.

• The 20th Amendment (2020) recentralized power over independent commissions and the judiciary.

History shows a consistent outcome: every regime that tampered with constitutional safeguards to entrench its authority ultimately suffered severe public backlash and lost power. The electorate inevitably rejects governments that manipulate state institutions for political convenience. Short-term institutional fixes routinely lead to long-term political failure.

Democratic legitimacy does not come from parliamentary strength alone; it comes from adherence to foundational principles. The independence of the judiciary is not an abstract concept—it relies entirely on public trust.

For justice to function, the courts must not only be independent; they must be seen to be independent. When a government uses its majority to alter judicial tenures, it weakens public trust in the neutral administration of justice. Once public confidence in the courts is eroded, no parliamentary majority can restore it. Enduring institutional values must always take precedence over temporary political power.

Reforms driven by administrative efficiency can still lead to damaging consequences if core institutional boundaries are crossed. A government may argue that extending judges' terms retains experienced legal minds or helps clear court backlogs.

However, when a good intention is executed by departing from constitutional norms, the systemic damage far outweighs the short-term benefits. This can create a precedent that future administrations can exploit. Good intentions cannot cure a flawed process.

When controversial amendments are introduced, much of the political opposition does not stem from a principled commitment to the rule of law. Often, opposition figures attack the proposal purely for political advantage despite having enacted similar or worse constitutional manipulations when they were in power.

The public recognizes this hypocrisy. When politicians who previously weakened judicial independence suddenly present themselves as guardians of the Constitution, their arguments carry little weight. This creates a dangerous cynicism where citizens distrust both the government proposing the change and the opposition criticizing it.

The current administration has an opportunity to break this historical cycle and avoid setting precedents that it would object to if placed in opposition in the future. If a policy decision is made to raise the retirement age of judges, the state can still enact this extension provided it applies strictly to future appointees. True judicial efficiency is not achieved through tenure extensions for sitting judges, but through deliberate, foundational steps: building widespread public legal awareness, strengthening legal education, encouraging Public Interest Litigation (PIL), expanding the legal aid system, depoliticizing key state institutions including the police, and allocating significantly more funds toward these priorities in the national budget. By prioritizing these structural efforts, the government can modernize the legal system while safeguarding the foundational integrity of the Constitution.
