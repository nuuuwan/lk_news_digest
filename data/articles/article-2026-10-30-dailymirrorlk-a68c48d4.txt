Israel election ends in deadlock as rival blocs fall short of majority

Jerusalem – Israel is facing weeks, and potentially months, of political uncertainty after final election results confirmed that neither of the two main political blocs had secured the parliamentary majority needed to form a government.

The bloc led by Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s right-wing Likud party and the opposition alliance led by his main rival, Gadi Eisenkot of the centrist Yashar party, have both fallen short of the 61 seats required for a majority in Israel’s 120-member parliament, the Knesset.

The results have set the stage for difficult coalition negotiations, with deep political and ideological divisions making it challenging for either side to assemble a government.

Although a coalition remains possible, any agreement could prove fragile and struggle to survive beyond a year. If party leaders fail to reach an agreement within the 12-week period available to them, Israelis could be called back to the polls.

One of the opposition’s main routes to removing Netanyahu from power depends on cooperation with Arab political parties – a prospect that has emerged as one of the biggest obstacles to forming a new government.

Two prominent right-wing leaders within the opposition bloc, former Prime Minister Naftali Bennett and former Defence Minister Avigdor Liberman, have rejected the possibility of joining a government that relies on the support of the two Arab parties in parliament – the Joint List and Raam.

Their opposition extends to both direct participation in a governing coalition and providing outside support to a minority government.

Both leaders have hardened their positions following the Hamas-led attacks on Israel on 7 October 2023.

Arab party participation in an Israeli government remains rare. Raam was part of an eight-party coalition that ended Netanyahu’s 12-year period in power in 2021. However, that government collapsed after just over a year.

Even without forming a government, opposition parties could still cooperate in parliament to replace the Knesset speaker and secure other key parliamentary positions.

Such an arrangement could limit the influence of Netanyahu’s caretaker government, although it would not necessarily remove him from the premiership.

Another possibility is the collapse of the two rival blocs and the formation of a national unity government.

Such an arrangement would likely involve Netanyahu’s Likud party, some of its existing coalition partners and at least one party from the opposition alliance.

Netanyahu is believed to be prepared to part ways with far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir as part of a potential agreement. However, he is not expected to abandon far-right Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich or the ultra-Orthodox parties.

Eisenkot, meanwhile, has ruled out joining a government led by Netanyahu.

Any reversal of that position could trigger a backlash among Israelis who have campaigned for Netanyahu’s departure from office and could be viewed by sections of the opposition as a betrayal.

A further possibility would be for Netanyahu to encourage individual lawmakers or members of opposition parties to defect and support his government.

However, all four leaders of the opposition bloc – Eisenkot, Bennett, Liberman and Yair Golan – have publicly ruled out joining a Netanyahu-led administration.

With Netanyahu’s bloc eight seats short of a parliamentary majority, securing the support of eight opposition lawmakers would be a considerable challenge.

As political negotiations continue behind the scenes, Israeli President Isaac Herzog is expected to begin formal consultations with the leaders of parties that have secured seats in the Knesset.

After hearing their positions, Herzog will appoint one political leader to attempt to form a government.

Arab parties are expected to support Eisenkot, potentially making the Yashar leader the first candidate to receive the mandate.

The candidate appointed by the president will have 28 days to assemble a coalition, with the possibility of a 14-day extension.

If the first attempt fails, the president can give another political leader 28 days to form a government.

Should that effort also collapse, a majority of Knesset members can request that the president appoint a third candidate, who will then have 14 days to secure the necessary parliamentary support.

If all three attempts fail, the country will have to hold another general election.

Netanyahu's position as caretaker prime minister means he can remain in office while the political parties attempt to resolve the deadlock.

If Eisenkot receives the first mandate but fails to form a government, the opportunity could subsequently pass to Netanyahu.

Should Netanyahu also fail, the political impasse could leave him continuing as caretaker prime minister for months until a new government is established.

Another election, which could take place no earlier than next spring, is therefore a distinct possibility.

The latest results threaten to return Israel to prolonged political paralysis, raising the prospect of a seventh general election in eight years.
