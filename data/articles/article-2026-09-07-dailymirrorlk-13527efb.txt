Could Sri Lanka face climate catastrophes similar to Nepal ?

Aftermath of a landslide in Aranayaka in November 2025. Following Ditwah, climate scientists identified around 16,000 families living in landslide-prone areas. Image courtesy of Bandara Jayaratne

The catastrophic Nepal floods is a stark reminder about people’s struggle for survival amidst rapid climate change events. The floods were triggered on Wednesday (August 26) after an ice-rock avalanche and glacial collapse blocked the flow of the Lhende tributary of the Bhote Koshi River. The resulting surge struck Timure near Nepal’s border with Tibet, causing widespread destruction downstream. The debris flow and flooding travelled nearly 100 kilometres and impacted populated areas along the Lende Kholā and Trishuli Kholā rivers.

According to Nepal’s national disaster authority, the death toll had risen to over 1300 by Sunday (September 6) while over 4000 individuals have been reported missing. Climate researchers observe that climate change is rapidly shrinking glaciers, thawing permafrost and intensifying natural disasters across Himalayas. Journalists reporting from the ground explained how entire villages were swept away and how layers of mud and silt have made commuting unthinkable. Survivors recount horrifying tales, awaiting the return of their loved ones and still trying to recover from shock.

The latest World Bank data shows that Nepal produces around 0.1% of total global greenhouse gas emissions. In other words, the World Bank describes Nepal as a ‘negligible contributor of climate change.’  But on the contrary, this country seems to be at the frontlines of a rapidly changing climate highlighting the realities of climate injustice.

Other developing nations impacted by extreme climate change events such as famines, multi-year droughts, eroding coastlines, destructive floods and public health emergencies such as malaria include Chad, Somalia, Haiti, Democratic Republic of Congo and Bangladesh.

In November 2025, Sri Lanka experienced one of its worst natural disasters in recent history during cyclone Ditwah. A geospatial analysis done by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) indicated that the cyclone triggered around 1200 landslides in the hilly regions of the country.

Sri Lanka has a history of floods and flash floods dating back to the 1900s. The events range from rare, high-impact disasters to frequent and island-wide floods in recent years.

In his comments to the Daily Mirror, Senior Climate Scientist Dr. Ranjith Punyawardena said that even though Sri Lanka may not experience Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOFs) similarly to Nepal, Sri Lanka is likely to experience extreme rainfall events. “These events maybe damaging, there could be heavy rainfall within a short period of time and our drainage systems may not be able to accommodate such a massive amount of excess water. If such events occur in Central Highlands for instance, it would lead to landslides and flash floods,” he cautioned.

Citing future predictions, Dr. Punyawardena said that many parts of the country are likely to experience heavy rainfall during October-November. “With the activation of the second inter monsoon season, the wet zone including Central Highlands, Kandy, Nuwara Eliya, Badulla, Matara, Kalutara districts will become vulnerable to floods and landslides. The dry zone will experience floods as there are no slopes in this region,” he added.

Sri Lanka has deep valleys in Central Highlands which could be vulnerable to landslides and flash floods during peak rainfall, said Geology Expert and former Head of the Geology Department at the Peradeniya University Prof. Atula Senaratne.

Explaining the chain of events, Prof. Senaratne said that loose rocks and thick layers of soil along these slopes could collapse during heavy rainfall. “At the same, heavy rain increases the speed and volume of water in a river. When debris collapse, they block the water flow in rivers and would form a temporary dam. Behind this dam, a reservoir forms. Once the force exceeds in capacity, the dam would burst releasing mud, water and debris in the form of a flash flood within a short period of time which would have a drastic impact on downstream populations,” he told Daily Mirror.

Slope regions in Nawalapitiya, Hatton, Haputale, Bandarawela and Upper elevations of Ella have been identified as vulnerable regions in this case. He recalled a similar event that had occurred in Sarasavigama, Kandy during the height of cyclone Ditwah. “A landslide blocked a stream in the area and once the floodwaters were released in the form of a flash flood, it killed around 30 people living downstream. During our field visits we identified a similar location in Matale as well,” he said.

According to Prof. Senaratne, the way forward is to map such vulnerable locations. “These maps could carry information about landslides and probabilities of rivers being blocked. These maps could be done similarly to landslide hazard zonation mapping. Such tools would allow vulnerable populations to evacuate prior to a disaster. Cartography is a very important approach and the National Building Research Organisation (NBRO) is in the process of carrying out these assessments,” he added.

According to Dr. Wasantha Senadheera, Senior scientist at the National Building Research Organisation (NBRO), Sri Lanka has many natural river basins. “In 2017, a major landslide in Athweltota in the Kalutara District triggered by unprecedented rainfall claimed many lives and caused severe damage to infrastructure. That is one of the most recent examples. If Sri Lanka had any kind of vulnerability similarly to the Nepal floods, it could have been triggered during Ditwah,” he told the Daily Mirror.

However, Dr. Senadheera said that following a 15-year assessment, around 15,000 families living in vulnerable areas have been identified. “But following cyclone Ditwah-which was one single event, we identified around 16,000 families living in landslide-prone areas. This too is an estimate because this figure was derived from reported incidents. We do not know how many incidents have not been reported and how many more vulnerable areas are yet to be identified,” he added.
