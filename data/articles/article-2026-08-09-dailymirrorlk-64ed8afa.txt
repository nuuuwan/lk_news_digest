Half-staffed and stonewalled,  procurement watchdog struggles to go digital

NPC operated with 50% vacancy rate — 30 of 60 approved posts vacant

48 of 135 procurement complaints remained unresolved at year-end as line ministries withheld

Severe staffing shortages, resistance from state entities, and the sluggish adoption of a new digital monitoring system are heavily undermining efforts to establish transparency and accountability in public procurement, according to the National Procurement Commission’s performance report for 2025.

The state oversight body is currently operating at half its intended capacity, with a 50 percent vacancy rate crippling its day-to-day operations. Out of an approved cadre of 60 positions, 30 remain vacant, including critical executive roles.

The commission highlighted that the inability to recruit essential personnel has directly restricted its capacity to execute monitoring, investigation, and policy development functions, while completely halting the establishment of its planned research and development unit.

As explicitly stated in the report, “This shortfall is one of the major challenges hindering the performance of the Commission,” National Procurement Commission Chairperson Sudharma Karunaratne noted that while the commission achieved notable milestones during the year, this work was carried out “despite limited human and physical resources.”

Compounding these internal administrative constraints, official investigations into alleged procurement irregularities face systematic delays due to state ministries failing to cooperate. During the year under review, the commission handled 135 complaints regarding procurements conducted outside established procedures. While fact-finding investigations were completed for 87 cases, 48 cases remained pending at the end of the year. The commission revealed that incomplete documentation and prolonged delays in obtaining essential information from line ministries have stalled final decisions, with several outstanding inquiries dating back to 2024.

Institutional inertia has also hindered the broader digital transition intended to modernise public sector purchasing and reporting. Separately, the widespread failure of procuring entities to establish dedicated Procurement Management Divisions, as mandated under updated guidelines, has created a severe bottleneck in the rollout of the Electronic Procurement Monitoring System. The monitoring division’s primary focus shifted during the year from manual-based activities to the operationalisation and pilot implementation of this digital platform. Despite launching the pilot in September across 24 ministries and 21 special spending units, compliance rates across the public sector remain minimal: by the close of the year, only two of the 24 ministries — roughly eight percent — and eight of the 21 special spending units, about 38 percent, had completed their detailed annual procurement plans on the online portal.

The commission’s finances and operations drew further scrutiny from the Auditor General, whose audit found significant budget under utilisation.

The Auditor General raised particular concern over the ePMS rollout, noting the absence of clear documentation confirming Cabinet approval for accepting UNDP grant assistance to develop the system, as required under Section 37 of the Public Financial Management Act No. 44 of 2024. Of 42 public institutions brought onto the pilot — 24 ministries and 18 independent bodies, by the audit’s count — only eight had updated their data by December 31, 2025, and the system remained unable to generate annual performance reports, with operational manuals still unfinished.

The audit further found that 47 investigation cases remained incomplete at year-end because line ministries had withheld requested documents, that the commission had conducted only three of eight planned Training of Trainers programmes, and that it had been unable to meet requests from 108 state institutions seeking awareness sessions on the 2024 Procurement Guidelines.

Tamil translations of the Procurement Guidelines Manual, along with guidelines on land disposal, asset management, and public-private partnerships, also remained unpublished by year-end.

To address these systemic weaknesses and fulfil its mandate to “formulate fair, equitable, transparent, competitive, and cost-effective procedures and guidelines,” the commission has initiated sweeping regulatory changes.

A massive overhaul is currently underway following the issuance of the Procurement Guidelines for Goods, Works and Non-Consulting Services 2024, marking the first major update in nearly two decades. In addition to drafting a new Public Procurement Bill to meet structural commitments agreed upon with the International Monetary Fund, the commission is formulating comprehensive new guidelines for the disposal of state lands, contract management, and the continuous supply of pharmaceuticals and medical devices.
