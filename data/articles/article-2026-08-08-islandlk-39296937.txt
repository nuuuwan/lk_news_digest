Measured from Life

The cobbled streets of The Hague and Amsterdam are dotted with antiquarian bookshops and print dealers whose display windows entice the collector with artfully arranged books, maps and engravings. Inside, shelves, drawers and portfolios reveal layer upon layer of historical material. They are an antiquarian’s delight and an embarrassment of riches.

My father-in-law spent the last 40 years of his life in The Hague and devoted many happy hours to browsing these shops. Over time, the dealers came to know him as an informed collector of Sri Lankan books, maps and prints, and would often contact him when material of particular interest appeared. It was always a thrill when he returned with his latest find.

He had previously acquired several of Heydt’s Ceylon engravings as individual prints, the form in which they commonly appeared on the antiquarian market. That all 48 engravings had remained together in a single bound portfolio made this a rare and important acquisition. They had originally formed the Ceylon section of Johann Wolfgang Heydt’s Allerneuester Geographisch- und Topographischer Schau-Platz von Africa und Ost-Indien, published in 1744, roughly translated as The Latest Geographical and Topographical Survey of Africa and the East Indies. The book comprised 345 pages and 115 full-page engravings depicting places across Africa and Asia.

My father-in-law presented the portfolio to my husband and me, explaining that the set was an irreplaceable visual record of eighteenth-century Sri Lanka. At the time, Heydt’s name was only vaguely familiar to us. It was only on examining the engravings closely that we came fully to appreciate the insight behind his assessment.

Heydt’s work emerged from the Netherlands’ long maritime and overseas history and from a culture in which visual representation had acquired unusual sophistication. Drawing was not confined to professional artists. It was also an essential practical skill for military officers, engineers and surveyors, who used it to observe, measure and communicate what they encountered.

Within the Dutch East India Company, the impulse to record was inseparable from the need to administer and defend its possessions—and to understand territories beyond its control. In Sri Lanka, this acquisitive gaze was closely connected with the VOC’s cinnamon monopoly. Heydt observed that the Company sometimes destroyed a substantial part of the annual harvest in order to maintain its market price.

Yet utility alone cannot explain the richness of the Dutch visual record. Commercial and strategic interests existed alongside a genuine curiosity about unfamiliar places and cultures.

The palace grounds and part of the Dutch ambassador’s procession, detail from Heydt, 1744, Plate XC. Pathfinder Collection.

Johann Wolfgang Heydt belonged to this world, although he was German by birth. Trained as a surveyor and employed by the VOC as a military engineer, he spent approximately two and a half years in Ceylon between 1734 and 1737. After returning to Europe, he became Building Director and surveyor to the Hohenlohe court, a later appointment that accords with the pronounced architectural understanding evident in his plans and views.

The complete German title runs to nearly 90 words, in the expansive manner of many 18th-century works. It belongs to an encyclopaedic tradition that sought not merely to name a book but to summarize its contents, scope and authority on the title page itself. The title announces a work that records places visited, events witnessed and customs observed, while also incorporating information assembled from other sources. It foreshadows the habits of mind evident throughout the volume: exhaustive attention, exact description and an almost irresistible desire to add one further detail.

Something of Heydt’s character emerges not only from the extraordinarily long title of his book, but also from the observations of the man who translated him more than two centuries later.

Major R. Raven-Hart, the distinguished linguist and former military intelligence officer, published in 1952 what appears to have been the first English translation of Heydt’s complete Ceylon sections. He recognized that his challenge was not simply to translate 18th-century German but to convey something of the personality of its author. “Heydt is no stylist,” he observed, describing his “rather attractively wooden style” and his “labyrinthine sentences, with one relative clause crawling over the back of another.” His characterization—affectionate rather than dismissive—suggests a conscientious and methodical man whose overriding concern was exactitude rather than literary elegance.

Remarkably, the same qualities are reflected in his visual work. Almost every feature considered worthy of explanation is identified on the plates by circled letters or numbers linked to detailed legends.

Unlike many artists of his time, Heydt engraved many of his own copperplate’s. In the case of the 48 Ceylon plates, all bear his name as engraver, indicating the unusually close control he exercised over their development from observation and drawing to the finished print. His engravings combine a surveyor’s discipline with restless curiosity and a remarkable gift for visual expression, repeatedly carrying him beyond the immediate demands of his official duties.

Among the most comprehensive examples of his method is Plate XC, which combines a half-plan of Kandy, a Dutch ambassadorial procession and a detailed representation of the royal palace complex. Heydt explains that he presents only half the city because the reduced scale would not allow him to include the whole.

More revealing, however, is his admission that he was not permitted to sketch the city thoroughly enough to establish its scale or represent it fully. “My duty,” he insists, “is to set down here as much as I saw of the city.” Elsewhere he remarks that “the King fears, lest his land be spied out,” acknowledging that the Kandyan court carefully controlled what foreign visitors were allowed to observe. Insulated from the Dutch-controlled low country, the court was alert to the possibility that European embassies might use ceremonial access to gather intelligence. Yet, despite these restrictions, Heydt’s plan records the city’s streets, compounds and principal buildings in surprising detail.

He also comments on—and depicts—the long procession in which he himself took part on the way to the palace, rendering in astonishingly fine, minute strokes some 200 hundred Company men and attendants, together with horses and palanquins. At its head were the intimidating whip-crackers who announced the approach of important personages. Heydt records that they struck the ground with such ferocity that one lash caught him on the leg, leaving a large welt. He could not decide whether the blow had been accidental or deliberate. Text and image together contain layers of subtext that a more conventional architectural view could never convey.

The engraving may also show what the royal complex looked like before alterations made under Kirti Sri Rajasinha, Sri Rajadhi Rajasinha and Sri Vikrama Rajasinha.

Close examination reveals another intriguing detail. On the building at the far right—identified by R. K. de Silva as most probably the Temple of the Tooth—Heydt depicts the façade as covered with horizontal rows of animals. Some are recognizable as leopards, birds and other creatures, while others appear more fantastical, all rendered with stylized, elongated bodies and curling flourishes that recall Kandyan decorative traditions. Whether the decorative features painted on the external walls of the shrine in Heydt’s time closely resembled what he drew remains uncertain, but the detail is fascinating. Heydt may have employed these animal forms as a visual shorthand for a richly decorated façade. The present shrine containing the Tooth Relic stands as a separate building within the inner courtyard of the palace, its external walls bearing painted figures and stylized animals, lotuses and other floral motifs.

By contrast, Heydt’s interior and exterior views of the Dutch Reformed Church within Jaffna Fort (Plates LXXXVII and LXXXVIII) are more purely architectural studies. They dispense with the intrusive lettering, explanatory keys and densely layered information that characterize the Kandy engraving, allowing the artist freer rein to produce compositions of clarity, balance and quiet elegance.

One of the pleasures of studying Heydt’s engravings is that they refuse to yield all their secrets at a single viewing. The longer one looks, the more they reveal. Even figures scarcely a millimeter high prove to be individually observed, while details easily missed at first viewing alter the meaning of entire scenes. Architectural ornaments, ceremonial processions and even vegetation repay careful scrutiny.

One wilderness landscape depicts a leopard in the foreground. Nearby, two jackals are shown worrying the emaciated body of a dead man, one gripping it in its jaws. These figures occupy only a small fraction of the composition, yet they subtly transform the way one reads the landscape, reminding the viewer that travel through 18th-century Sri Lanka could be arduous and dangerous.

The scene recalls the dramatic illustration of Jacob Haafner’s encounter with a giant python in a later edition of his travels. Haafner’s lively account supplies a literary counterpart to the dangers that Heydt merely hints at.

Elsewhere, what appears to be a traditional estuarine fishing net beneath a bridge offers another example of how a seemingly incidental detail can preserve evidence of historical practice.

Heydt announced that his views were drawn “from life” and from his own experience. As a military surveyor, however, he did more than draw from life. He measured it, observed it and recorded it with a precision that still commands admiration today.
