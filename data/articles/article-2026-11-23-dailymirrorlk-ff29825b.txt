Foreign-linked loan apps continue to prey  on crisis-hit Sri Lankans from regulatory  black hole: CSF

More than five years after the Central Bank of Sri Lanka (CBSL) began cautioning the public against them, a web of foreign-linked loan apps continues to prey on crisis-hit households, rebranding and reshuffling to stay in business within a regulatory black hole, a study by the Centre for a Smart Future (CSF) found. The lenders were thrust into the spotlight after the 2022 economic crisis, as their harassment of defaulters spilled into public view and pushed some borrowers to the brink of suicide. Drawing on 39 borrower interviews, the study found that such harassment caused severe psychological distress and, in several cases, suicidal ideation. One borrower, whose employer, husband and children’s teachers had all been contacted by agents, told researchers she had decided to end her life before her family intervened.

Rather than retreating, several operators have resurfaced under new guises. A service raided by the Computer Crimes Investigation Division in 2024 now leads to a loan comparison site. The branding of an early Croatian-backed entrant that stopped lending now fronts a comparison site promoting other lenders. Others have switched developers or redirect applicants to sister brands, the study observed.

The study reviewed seven prominent services, all registered locally. One is offered by a Kyiv-based finance company and another is part of a Latvia-based fintech group. Three other brands share a single Latvian address, while a fourth lists an Estonian address that leads to a car park.

The Digital Alternative Lending Association (DALA) bills itself as the sector’s only self-regulatory body and counts the developers of the leading apps among its members, yet it has no Sri Lankan leadership beyond its local secretaries. As per DALA’s March 2026 change-of-directors filing cited by the study, all its directors are nationals of Portugal, Latvia and Russia.

Despite promises of zero interest, one borrower paid Rs.4,500 on a Rs.9,000 loan, half the principal, while another was charged a Rs.3,500 service fee on Rs.5,000, or 70 percent. In one case, a Rs.300 shortfall drew a demand for over Rs.100,000 a year later. Borrowers who fell behind described relentless calls, with one receiving as many as 60 a day, as agents contacted their families and workplaces to brand them fraudsters.

“Taking loans from these companies is like holding a tiger by its tail, you will not have any respite,” one borrower lamented.

However, the lenders remain outside the CBSL’s purview, as it regulates only deposit-taking institutions. The Microfinance and Credit Regulatory Authority Act, passed in March, provides for the regulation of digital money lending. Even so, the Authority is still to be established, and the Act is reportedly being revised before it has even been operationalised.

CSF urged the government to fast-track the Authority with explicit powers over app-based lenders. It warned that the network’s cross-border structure makes it difficult for any single regulator to rein in.
