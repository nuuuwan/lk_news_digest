Solar power industry warns new policy could threaten energy security

Industry experts and stakeholders sharing views during the panel discussion on “Affordable Clean Energy – A Right, Not a Privilege.”

Sri Lanka’s solar power industry is warning that a new policy could undermine energy security, increase costs for consumers and businesses, and weaken the country’s ability to reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels.

Sri Lanka is still recovering from an economic crisis, making energy security critical to economic stability. The Public Utilities Commission of Sri Lanka (PUCSL) and National System Operator (NSO) have warned of a potential electricity shortage of 450 MWh by the first half of 2027.

Against this backdrop, rooftop solar currently contributes more than 2,600 MW to the national grid and is estimated to save approximately Rs. 20.4 billion in foreign exchange every month by reducing the need for imported fossil fuels. This is equivalent to around Rs. 1.5 billion a day. Replacing the same volume of electricity with diesel could cost the Government approximately Rs. 2.5 billion per day, with diesel-generated electricity estimated at around Rs. 170–180 per unit.

New Policy Raises Concerns -Industry concerns centre on a circular issued by the Ministry of Energy on 11 September 2026, under which new connections and extensions of existing agreements are required to operate under the Net Plus scheme, replacing the previously available Net Metering and Net Accounting arrangements. The agreement period has also been reduced from up to 20 years to a maximum of 12 years.

The financial implications for consumers are significant. A typical rooftop solar investment of Rs. 1.5 million to Rs. 2 million was intended to enable households to substantially reduce their electricity bills. Under the previous system, a 5 kW system generating around 550 units could offset approximately 550 units consumed by the household.

Under Net Plus, however, the consumer would sell those 550 units to the Government for approximately Rs. 13,000, before purchasing the same amount of electricity for household consumption at the prevailing tariff, costing approximately Rs. 47,000–48,000. With electricity tariffs also increasing by approximately 5%–7.5% annually, the industry argues that this significantly weakens the economic incentive for consumers to invest in rooftop solar.

Businesses and Consumer Choice at Risk -The impact extends beyond households. Businesses that could use rooftop solar to reduce electricity costs and improve long-term energy security may have fewer viable options under the new arrangements, leaving them more dependent on grid electricity and potentially rising tariffs.

Industry representatives also argue that restricting consumers’ ability to choose how they generate and consume electricity raises concerns over consumer rights and the principle of least-cost electricity embodied in Sri Lanka’s Electricity Act.

The industry questions the justification that excessive solar capacity is placing pressure on the grid. If grid balancing is the concern, stakeholders argue that strengthening grid infrastructure and introducing storage solutions would be more effective than discouraging rooftop solar.

Dr. Lakmal Fernando, Vice President of the Sri Lanka National Chamber of Commerce, has called for greater investment in grid capacity, including the potential deployment of batteries at heavily utilised transformers to store excess daytime solar generation and release it when required.

Jobs, Investments and Energy Security- The policy could also have a significant economic impact on the solar industry. More than 800 companies currently operate in the sector, supporting over 21,000 jobs, while approximately 166,000 consumers have already obtained bank loans to invest in rooftop solar systems.

Industry representatives warn that a contraction of the sector could affect not only jobs and businesses but also the long-term maintenance of the country’s existing 2,600 MW solar capacity.

The Sri Lanka National Chamber of Commerce has called for the immediate revocation of the 11 September directive and the reinstatement of Net Metering and Net Accounting, while also proposing that future policy changes be independently evaluated by PUCSL. Solar industry representatives have separately written to all 225 Members of Parliament to highlight their concerns.

Industry Proposes Practical Solutions- Rather than making Net Plus compulsory, industry stakeholders are proposing that consumers be allowed to select the model most appropriate to their electricity requirements, including Net Metering and Net Accounting.

They are also calling for greater adoption of Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) to store excess solar power generated during the day and make it available during periods of peak demand. Upgrading transformers and distribution networks, together with the adoption of smart-grid technologies, has also been proposed to improve the management of distributed renewable energy.

A Critical Crossroads for Sri Lanka- Industry stakeholders warn that policy decisions made without adequate consultation and independent regulatory evaluation could undermine investor confidence, restrict consumer choice and weaken a sector that is already contributing significantly to Sri Lanka’s energy security.

At a time when the country faces potential electricity shortages and continues its economic recovery, they argue that protecting renewable energy capacity, enabling consumers and businesses to access least-cost electricity, and strengthening the national grid will be essential.

Without a balanced policy approach, Sri Lanka risks moving away from an energy source that reduces dependence on imported fossil fuels and helps manage electricity costs, while increasing exposure to expensive diesel-generated power.
