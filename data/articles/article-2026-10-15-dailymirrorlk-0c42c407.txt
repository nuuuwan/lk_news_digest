CA Sri Lanka calls for wider tax net, growth-focused budget

Urges government to use Budget 2027 to turn economic stability into stronger growth

Calls for a broader, fairer and more sustainable revenue system, supported by greater formalisation, stronger compliance and digitalisation

Sri Lanka must widen its tax net rather than place an ever-greater burden on the existing taxpayers, CA Sri Lanka said, urging the government to use Budget 2027 to turn the economic stability into stronger growth.

With the national budget due on November 12, CA Sri Lanka President Tishan Subasinghe called for a broader, fairer and more sustainable revenue system, supported by greater formalisation, stronger compliance and digitalisation.

“Our Budget 2027 proposals focused on a fundamental question: how do we build a revenue system that is broader, fairer and more sustainable, without placing an ever-greater burden on those already within the tax net?” he said addressing the 47th National Conference of CA Sri Lanka.

“We need a broader tax base, greater formalisation, better compliance, digitalisation and a tax system that people regard as fair.”

Subasinghe stressed that fiscal policy must also support the country’s transition from stabilisation to growth, with the forthcoming budget assuming particular importance.

“The government, business and professions must continue to listen to one another. Because ultimately, a country cannot tax its way to prosperity. We must grow our way to prosperity.”

Among CA Sri Lanka’s proposals was an increase in the monthly Advance Personal Income Tax threshold to Rs.200,000, providing relief to the middle-income earners while recognising the fiscal implications of the measure.

Subasinghe welcomed the government’s subsequent indication that it remained committed to moving the personal income-tax-free threshold towards Rs.200,000, although he said it remained a “wait and see situation”. He said the professional institutions had a responsibility to contribute constructively to policy formulation, rather than merely comment on decisions.

The tax proposals formed part of a broader argument that Sri Lanka’s recovery must now generate the investment, employment, productivity and foreign exchange needed to sustain its economic gains.

“Therefore, our challenge is not simply to avoid another default. It is to build an economy capable of generating sufficient growth, exports, investment and revenue to service our obligations while improving the quality of life of our people,” Subasinghe said.

He called for growth that creates jobs, allows businesses to invest and expand, raises productivity and brings more people into the formal economy.

Reflecting on the recovery, Subasinghe said Sri Lanka had recorded 11 consecutive quarters of economic growth, while the gross official reserves had risen to US $ 6.9 billion.

The reserves had stood at US $ 7.3 billion in February before absorbing the subsequent oil-price shocks, highlighting the continuing need to strengthen the country’s external buffers.

That position contrasted sharply with the crisis four years earlier, when inflation had approached 70 percent and the gross official reserves had fallen to around US $ 1.9 billion. According to Subasinghe, about US $ 1.5 billion of that amount was held under the People’s Bank of China swap arrangement, leaving less than US $ 400 million in immediately usable reserves.

“For a nation that needed to pay for fuel, food, medicine and essential imports, this was not simply an economic crisis. It was a fight for basic survival.”

However, he cautioned against treating stabilisation as the end of the recovery process.

“The next phase of our national journey must be about more than recovering from the crisis and stability. Now it must be about growth and resilience,” he said, questioning whether the country had the right policies to accelerate growth.

Subasinghe acknowledged the International Monetary Fund (IMF)-supported Extended Fund Facility’s role in restoring fiscal discipline, rebuilding reserves, advancing debt restructuring and strengthening confidence since 2023.

With the programme approaching its conclusion and its final review scheduled for 2027, he identified three possible paths for Sri Lanka: another IMF programme, a home-grown programme or a hybrid of the two.

Whether to enter another programme was a decision for the government, he said. However, the principles underpinning the recovery needed to become part of the country’s economic culture.

“Above all, we must ensure that the reforms we undertake are not reforms we implement because someone is watching us but reforms we uphold because we have learnt their value.”

Subasinghe also warned that the relief secured through debt restructuring did not remove the need to strengthen Sri Lanka’s capacity to meet its obligations.

He credited the current and previous governments for the restructuring effort, which he said had delivered substantial relief, including a targeted reduction of around US $ 17 billion in external debt service during the IMF programme period.

“Debt relief is not the same as debt elimination. The debt remains.”

He stressed that Sri Lanka needed additional foreign exchange and stronger reserves to meet its debt commitments while retaining the capacity to absorb the external shocks.

The debt sustainability framework also constrained the fiscal space available to the policymakers, he said, citing targets to bring public debt below 95 percent of GDP by 2032, keep average annual gross financing needs below 13 percent of GDP during 2027-2032 and annual foreign-currency debt service below 4.5 percent of GDP over the same period.

“These are not merely numbers in an IMF document. They are a reminder of the fiscal space we must protect.”

The geopolitical tensions added to those challenges, with Subasinghe warning that the conflict in the Middle East could affect the fuel prices, port-related transport costs, tourism and migrant earnings, ultimately influencing the foreign exchange available for debt service.

The shifting supply chains, risks to shipping routes and vulnerable energy markets meant Sri Lanka’s next phase of growth needed to build resilience alongside the competitiveness.

He urged the country to aspire to an economy that generates its own foreign exchange and attracts investment, technology, knowledge and talent, while offering young people stronger reasons to build their futures at home.
