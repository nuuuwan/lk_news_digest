Beyond the Bench and the Ward:  The Medical Aspects of Retiring at 67

Sri Lanka is once again locked in a fierce national debate over retirement ages. The government’s proposal to amend the constitution and extend the statutory retirement age of Supreme Court judges from 65 to 67 years, alongside tiered increases across lower courts, has drawn sharp criticism.

This controversy closely mirrors the nation’s healthcare crisis in late 2022, when a sudden decision to revert the public sector retirement threshold to 60 years threatened to cause an immediate collapse in specialised hospital care, forcing an emergency revision extending doctors’ service to 63 years.

While politicians, legal scholars and trade unions debate these policies from administrative and political angles, an essential perspective is often missing, the human organ at the centre of it all. What actually happens to the human brain when we push professional service late into our seventh decade? Is extending the retirement age to 67 a biologically sound decision, or a dangerous gamble with mental decline?

To understand cognitive aging, we must look at how the brain processes information over time. Cognitive architecture is broadly divided into two complementary systems: Fluid Intelligence and Crystallised Intelligence.

Fluid intelligence is our brain’s raw processing power. It governs mental speed, executive function, working memory and our ability to solve novel problems quickly without prior instruction. Science shows that fluid intelligence peaks surprisingly early—in our late 20s—and undergoes a steady biological decline. By age 60,  the average individual has lost a noticeable portion of their peak fluid processing speed.

If fluid intelligence were the sole measure of human capability, forcing professionals to retire earlier would make biological sense. But it is only half the equation.

Crystallised intelligence represents our accumulated lifelong knowledge, vocabulary, domain expertise and professional judgment. Unlike fluid processing speed, crystallized intelligence is remarkably resilient to normal biological aging. In fact, research demonstrates that crystallized capabilities remain stable or actively expand well through our 60s and 70s.

To simplify these two types of intelligence let’s consider an everyday scenario of a senior citizen driving a car. He feels dizzy from an unexpected blood pressure spike while driving down an unfamiliar, crowded highway. Since his fluid intelligence has declined,  he will struggle with reaction times and might not be able to continue driving. This is overridden by his mature crystallized intelligence, which guides him to anticipate the danger. The senior citizen will therefore safely slow down the car, steer off to park it in a shoulder with minimal effort.

Neuroscientists refer to this dynamic as the Complementary Capabilities Hypothesis. In high-level knowledge professions—such as judicial adjudication, senior clinical medicine, or strategic management—raw mental speed matters far less than pattern recognition, context, emotional regulation and deep experiential wisdom. A 67-year-old judge or consultant physician does not process new information as fast as a 28-year-old, but they leverage a vastly richer neural experience library in their minds to render far more balanced, accurate judgments.

Extending service late into life also offers a protective effect against mental aging. The human brain operates on a strict “use it or lose it” principle. The Cognitive Reserve Hypothesis shows that engaging in complex, intellectually demanding work builds a neural buffer that mitigates structural brain aging.

When high-functioning professionals retire abruptly, that mental stimulation vanishes overnight. In the landmark Whitehall II longitudinal study tracking thousands of public servants, researchers discovered that verbal memory declined 38% faster post-retirement compared to pre-retirement trajectories. Remaining professionally active in demanding roles keeps the brain’s neural networks firing, preserving verbal memory and cognitive vitality.

However cognitive longevity is not without biological vulnerabilities. Normal brain aging involves a gradual reduction in grey and white matter volume, accompanied by subtle declines in neurotransmitter levels.

While crystallized wisdom holds strong, the decline in fluid intelligence creates real vulnerabilities under specific working conditions:     Mental  Fatigue:  Working memory and attention span exhaust more rapidly in older brains  during prolonged high-stress periods.   Psychomotor  and Speed Constraints: Occupations requiring split-second physical reactions—such as  commercial aviation, where mandatory retirement remains strictly capped at  65—cannot afford fluid processing delays.   Inter-Individual  Variability:  Biological aging is not uniform. One 67-year-old may possess the sharp  mental clarity of a 50-year-old, while another of the exact same age may  experience subtle, unrecognized cognitive decline.

Crucially cognitive performance does not exist in a vacuum; it is always linked to systemic bodily health and overall physical fitness. Age-related changes in cardiovascular endurance, musculoskeletal strength, metabolic function and sensory acuity directly influence operational capacity and accelerate central fatigue. Evaluating an individual’s functional longevity and workplace safety therefore demands a holistic approach. One that assesses physical fitness and systemic somatic health alongside isolated neurocognitive metrics.

Across the world, public policy is adapting to these biological realities. The average statutory retirement age across OECD nations is rising toward 66.4 years, with nations like Denmark indexing retirement directly to life expectancy. In the United Kingdom, judicial legislation officially raised the mandatory retirement age for judges from 70 to 75 years to preserve judicial talent and tackle backlogs.

Sri Lanka’s proposal to set a 67-year threshold for Supreme Court judges aligns well with global neurocognitive standards. The judicial bench relies overwhelmingly on crystallized reasoning rather than rapid psychomotor execution.

However, medicine teaches us that blanket chronological cutoffs, whether setting retirement at 60 or 67 are blunt instruments. Rather than treating 67 as an absolute, unexamined right, professions should pair tenure extensions with objective, peer-driven health and competency evaluations.

Science shows that our late 60s can be among our most intellectually productive years. By understanding the neuroscience of aging, society can preserve the priceless wisdom of experienced professionals while safeguarding the institutional standards the public relies upon.
