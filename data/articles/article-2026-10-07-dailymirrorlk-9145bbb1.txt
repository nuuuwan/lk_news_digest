Statues of radicalisation and Statues of dynastism

How do terrorist rump and fallen dynastic rulers live on through metal and concrete?

After twenty-five years of war, Islamist terrorist attacks on Easter Sunday and a fallen regime, Sri Lankans should be wary of two kinds of statues. The first type is the statues of radicalisation, and the other is statues of dynastism.  Both would cloak themselves in innocuous jargon such as remembrance, reconciliation or patriotism. But they all unmistakably foster separatism, radicalisation, extremism and dynastism.

Let’s begin with the statues of radicalisation, not least due to the recent controversy over the police removal of a statue of Thileepan in Nallur, Jaffna, leading to protests, more so by the diaspora than locals. Rasaiah Parthipan, alias Lt. Col. Thileepan, an LTTE political leader who died in a hunger strike in 1987, was commemorated in a week-long ceremony last month, which also saw certain schools sending their students to pay homage.

Former President Mahinda Rajapakse named a record number of state institutions after himself

As a young war correspondent, I remember visiting Mullativu Maya Vidyalaya in the early 2000s, where a roll of honour of slain LTTE fighters, the old boys and girls, was engraved on a wooden board in the main hall. In the war that followed, all the adult students were conscripted, and many perished in the final phase of the war.

Thileepan was no Gandhi, but a façade of a nihilistic terrorist group. He became the inspiration of two generations of suicide terrorists, who turned the LTTE into the global champion of suicide terrorism in the 1990s, well before the Islamist extremists claimed the throne. However, the recent episode seemed to have moved certain local pundits and terrorist apologists alike, with the first group viewing the incident as denial of the right to remembrance promised by the JVP/NPP government, and the second associated with fringe elements of the Northern Tamil politics, feigning outrage for political advantage.

The starting point of analysis should be how on earth the government or the law enforcement agencies allowed a week-long commemoration of a senior LTTE cadre and a self-proclaimed Lt. Colonel of the LTTE?

There is a little difference here from the LTTE’s elaborate year-long ceremonies of slain LTTE cadres, with  Black Tigers, aka suicide cadres, Sea Tigers, female units, followed by a Mahaveer week in November, each assigned a week-long commemoration. Terrorists and revolutionaries celebrate the dead to conscript the living. Radicalisation is fomented through celebration of martyrdom, and martyrdom is promoted through the promise of elevation to a place on the altar of slain members.

While the North is a long way from another phase of suicidal terrorism, and the threat remains latent, a small group of hardened ethno-nationalists could foster the next wave within a decade (or much less), exploiting a confused state that fails to differentiate between remembrance and radicalisation.

Therefore, it is the right approach to investigate the whole episode under the Prevention of Terrorism Act, which, though much abused in the past, in clinical use would keep a tab on extremism until a replacement law is enacted.

The vast majority of the Sri Lankans would be rightfully offended if the JVP chose to erect in the city centre of Colombo a statue of Rohana Wijeweera, a megalomaniac no less than Velupillai Prabakaran. The government should expect the same restraint from the apologists of the terrorists in the North and legislate the laws that draw clear red lines.

Statues of radicalisation manifest in other forms. The Arabisation of Kattankudy in its architecture, Arabic name boards, Wahhabi mosques, and burqas during the previous wave of Islamism was overlooked by the same political apathy that contributed to the Easter Sunday attack. Yet they were symbols of parallel societies, self-alienation and breeding grounds of extremism, as the subsequent experience revealed. Though that wave lost momentum with the public pushback following the Easter Sunday attacks, the government should be mindful that with the rising tempers over Gaza, another wave of religious radicalisation could creep in.

Then there are statues of dynastism. Mahinda Rajapaksa during his two-term presidency named every new construction after himself. The list is long enough for this column to include a separate appendage. He envisaged a future where a visitor could travel to Sri Lanka on the now-defunct Mihin Lanka airline, disembark at Mahinda Rajapaksa International Airport in Mattala, watch an opera in Nelum Pokuna Mahinda Rajapaksa Theatre, and a cricket match at the Mahinda Rajapaksa International Cricket Stadium, and send his or her children to many schools named after Mahinda Rajapaksa. And there is also Magampura Mahinda Rajapaksa Port in Hambantota, which remained a white elephant during his term. If that is not enough, there is  Mahinda Rajapaksa National Telecinema Park, Mahinda Rajapaksa International Exhibition and Convention Centre, Mahinda Rajapaksa Vocational Training Centre and Mahinda Rajapaksa International Stadium in Diyagama, which has since dropped his illustrious name.

Mahinda Rajapaksa, like many of his kind, the tinpot third-world autocrats, failed to differentiate between the state and his role as the head of state, and sought to rebuild the nation on his own image. That ended in a costly failure culminating in the worst ever economic crisis in the nation’s history. While this renaming spree of the nation seemed ludicrous in the eyes of a sane observer and might invoke real-life ‘Autumn of the Patriarch,’ it also betrayed a deep-seated inferiority complex of the man. This was also part and parcel of a costly dynastic enterprise and a patronage network that contributed to high-profile corruption.

That dynastic enterprise was defeated, and the familial regime toppled, but many of the national landmarks named after a corrupt regime leader remains intact, though some only in their name boards. One should have that curious feeling when travelling to the Mahinda Rajapaksa International Airport, no different from what one might feel, if one such existed, heading to Sani Abacha Airport in Nigeria.

The government should rename these statues of dynastism, because a nation cannot escape its unsavoury past when half of its landmarks remain named after unsavoury leaders of the past. However, that would not guarantee another opportunistic politician renaming them after himself. Therefore, the government should make a law to ban any politician naming state projects after themselves unless, of course, they have been built with their personal wealth.

Even national landmarks built with private philanthropic contributions were not immune to Rajapaksa’s renaming spree.  The Sugathadasa Stadium, named after the country’s first sports minister, who donated his ancestral land for the construction of the stadium, came close to being renamed after Mahinda Rajapaksa, until the family raised objections. Undeterred, Mahinda Rajapaksa built another stadium in Diyagama and named it after himself.
