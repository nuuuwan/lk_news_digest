When economic warnings become political weapons

Sri Lanka has been through an economic crisis that brought the country to its knees. People stood in queues for fuel, medicines became difficult to find, businesses struggled to survive and families were forced to cut back on even their most basic needs. The wounds of that crisis are still fresh.

That is precisely why politicians must understand the enormous responsibility that comes with speaking about the country’s economy.

Former President Ranil Wickremesinghe last week raised concerns about Sri Lanka’s ability to meet its debt obligations from 2027 and 2028 onwards. Speaking at a book launch, he warned that the country would face a major challenge in finding the necessary funds and said Sri Lanka would need to build its reserves significantly and raise billions of dollars through international bonds.

The Government was quick to reject the warning. Minister Anil Jayantha argued that foreign reserves should be viewed as an external buffer rather than the sole measure of economic progress. Minister Vijitha Herath, meanwhile, assured the diplomatic community in Colombo that the Government was confident Sri Lanka would be able to meet its debt obligations from 2028 onwards.

Both sides are entitled to their views. But the public deserves something more than competing political statements. It deserves facts.

Sri Lanka cannot afford to turn the question of debt repayment into another political battlefield. Even rumours and exaggerated statements can affect investor sentiment, business decisions and public confidence. When politicians speak about another possible economic crisis, they must therefore understand that their words can have consequences far beyond the political stage.

There is nothing wrong with questioning the Government’s economic plans. Such scrutiny is essential in a democracy. The Government must be challenged on how it intends to meet future debt repayments, how much foreign currency it expects to generate, how it will increase exports, attract investment and strengthen government revenue.

But criticism must come with evidence. If there is a genuine risk that Sri Lanka could face a financing gap in 2027 or 2028, the public has a right to know exactly what that risk is. At the same time, if the Government has a credible debt repayment plan extending to 2030, it must publish enough information for independent economists, businesses and ordinary citizens to understand how that plan will work.

Saying everything is under control is not enough. Neither is saying another crisis is around the corner.

This is where political maturity becomes important. A former President who played a central role in managing the economic crisis has every right to raise concerns about the future. But such warnings should be accompanied by clear evidence and solutions rather than leaving the public with the impression that another economic catastrophe is inevitable.

Likewise, a Government that says the country is capable of meeting its obligations must be prepared to demonstrate how.

The Government has spoken about poverty reduction, agriculture, science and technology, digitalisation and sustainable development. These are all important ambitions. But Sri Lanka cannot borrow its way out of every problem. Nor can it depend indefinitely on foreign assistance.

People want stability. They want to know that their savings are safe, that their jobs will remain secure, that prices will not suddenly explode and that the country will not once again return to the desperate conditions experienced during the last crisis.

Those who warn of another crisis must explain how it can be avoided. Those who insist there will be no crisis must explain why the public should believe them.

Sri Lanka’s economic recovery is too important to become a contest in political point scoring. The country cannot afford another crisis created by economic mismanagement. The lesson from 2022 should be clear to everyone.

Economic stability is not something politicians should gamble with. Sri Lanka needs less political theatre and more economic truth. It needs politicians who will compete over solutions rather than predictions of disaster. And above all, it needs leaders who understand that when they speak about the economy, they are not merely addressing a political audience.

They are speaking to an entire nation still recovering from its worst economic trauma in generations.
