Reviving Sri Lanka’s chequered trilingual literary heritage

The burning of the Jaffna public library in 1981 destroyed over 97,000 volumes

Colombo’s literature scene came back to life with the inaugural Colombo Literary Festival which concluded on Wednesday (29). This trilingual celebration of literature, cinema, history and music brought together authors, artistes and literature lovers from across the country under one roof. At the core of this event is the commitment to provide an equal space to celebrate literary works done in Sinhala, Tamil and English.

Sri Lanka carries with it a rich literary heritage that spans over two millennia.  From ancient histories and poetry to contemporary novels and books of fiction, the literary past of the island is a reflection of its dense social fabric. The literature scene has evolved, from ancient chronicles and religious writings to modern day fiction and non-fiction books.

But,  over the course of history, Sri Lanka has witnessed some brutal events such as the armed conflict, the burning of the Jaffna Library to repression faced by Tamil writers from time to time in the post-war context. These events have challenged the coexistence of trilingual literary works.

The burning of the Jaffna public library in 1981 destroyed over 97,000 volumes, including irreplaceable palm-leaf manuscripts, dealing a devastating and permanent blow to Tamil literature, historical records, and cultural identity in Sri Lanka. According to scholars, Jaffna witnessed one of the most flourishing periods of Tamil literary celebration prior to the war. However, amidst the armed conflict, much of the post-war literary works revolved around injustices faced by their own people during the conflict. Many of these literary works were based on eyewitness accounts, reflecting the brutal past.

These atrocities have haunted the island for decades. The language barrier that still persists is a clear example of the impact. It is only when someone reads a novel or a poem in either Sinhala or Tamil that they really understand the depth of the respective language. But most Sri Lankans were distanced from learning Tamil as a result of the draconian Sinhala Only Act.

This is why Sri Lanka needs more trilingual literary festivals that celebrate literary works done in all three languages, at least now.

There’s much emphasis made on the concept of reconciliation, but how could different communities reconcile if they are forced to learn just one language while other languages have been excluded? Apart from Tamil, different communities such as the Malay community in Sri Lanka speak what is called Sri Lankan Malay. This is neither found in Malaysia nor Indonesia. But this unique language too,  is at risk of extinction since it is only being conversed by a particular community in the deep  South.

But that doesn’t mean that people should be aware of all languages in a country. India has 22 official languages, 121 major languages and over 19,000 mother tongues and dialects. In Sri Lanka, the Sinhalese dialect differs in different regions of the country and this is the same when it comes to Tamil as well. The Tamil dialect spoken by the Malaiyaha community is different to that spoken in Jaffna. The Tamil dialect in the Eastern province is even more different. This is the beauty of this culturally diverse nation.

A little bit of maturity is also required to facilitate the progression of the literature scene in Sri Lanka. Sri Lanka has witnessed literary festivals that catered to different audiences. Some have been attracting the elite, giving little space for the common man to come and witness what happens during these events. People from rural areas may have no idea about literary festivals happening in urban settings.

The Colombo Literary Festival has initiated the first step in introducing an event that’s affordable for people. Perhaps the next step should be its expansion to different locations of the country. It may be a challenge to empower people to read books in this era of social media and AI. But these initiatives would at least allow people to appreciate and take pride in the country’s long lost literary heritage.
