BRICS: While others lobbied,Sri Lanka lost ground

As leaders of some of the world’s most influential emerging economies gathered in New Delhi for the 18th BRICS Summit last weekend at Bharat Mandapam once again took centre stage in global diplomacy.

For Sri Lanka, however, the summit should trigger an uncomfortable question. How did a country that formally sought BRICS membership end up watching from the sidelines while Uganda, Nigeria, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam and others moved ahead as BRICS Partner Countries?

Sri Lanka wanted a seat at the table. Others secured one. We did not. This is not the time for excuses or bureaucratic silence. Policymakers must identify where the system failed, correct it and act.

In global diplomacy, opportunities lost today may not return tomorrow.

BRICS is not simply another diplomatic gathering. India’s 2026 Chairship has last weekend involved hundreds of meetings and engagements covering political cooperation, finance, trade, innovation, business and people-to-people exchanges. For Sri Lanka, closer engagement could mean greater access to investment, export markets, development finance, technology, energy cooperation and some of the world’s fastest-growing economies.

Sri Lanka’s interest was clear. In 2024, it is reported the then Government examined joining BRICS and the New Development Bank (NDB) and approved proceeding with applications. Following the change of government, it is reported that the current Foreign Minister Vijitha Herath communicated Sri Lanka’s interest to BRICS counterparts, while President Anura Kumara Dissanayake reportedly sought Russian support.

With parliamentary elections approaching, neither the President nor Foreign Minister was able to attend the 2024 BRICS summit, an understandable decision given domestic political circumstances. Sri Lanka was instead represented at the BRICS Plus Outreach Dialogue in Kazan by then Foreign Secretary Aruni Wijewardane, who formally reiterated the country’s interest in closer association with BRICS and the NDB and Sri Lanka’s desire for closer association with BRICS and the NDB, explaining both the strategic importance of BRICS to Sri Lanka and what Sri Lanka could contribute to the grouping.

Sri Lanka therefore cannot claim it was absent from the process. But it certainly cannot be satisfied with the result.

In June 2025, the Russian Ambassador in Colombo publicly urged Sri Lanka to take “practical steps” if it genuinely wanted to join BRICS, while indicating that Russia and China were supportive.

This statement should have set alarm bells ringing in Colombo.

BRICS subsequently expanded its Partner Country framework. Belarus, Bolivia, Cuba, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, Thailand, Uganda, Uzbekistan and Nigeria became partners, with Vietnam joining later.

Sri Lanka was not among them. There has been no public indication that Sri Lanka's membership application was formally rejected. But that makes the question even more important.

The President was interested. The Foreign Minister was interested. The Government wanted closer BRICS engagement.

Political interest without bureaucratic execution produces little. Diplomacy is not about sending letters and waiting for replies. It is about knocking on doors, building support country by country and following through relentlessly until the national objective is achieved.

Was BRICS treated as a genuine foreign-policy priority? Were India, China, Russia, Brazil, South Africa and the newer members systematically engaged? Were Sri Lankan missions given a coordinated lobbying strategy? Did we explain not merely why Sri Lanka wants BRICS, but why BRICS should want Sri Lanka?

Other countries of different sizes, regions and economic weight secured Partner Country status. They advanced. Sri Lanka did not.

This matters far beyond diplomatic prestige. At a time of rising protectionism, tariffs and great-power competition, BRICS represents an important multilateral push by emerging economies seeking greater influence over global economic and political decision-making.

For a smaller country like Sri Lanka, the answer should not be fewer relationships but more. We need more markets, more financing options, more partnerships and more seats at more tables.

BRICS should not be viewed as choosing one geopolitical camp over another. It should be another economic and diplomatic bridge consistent with Sri Lanka’s independent, balanced foreign policy.

Closer engagement could strengthen relations simultaneously with India, our closest neighbour and critical economic partner; China, a major investor and trading partner; and Russia, an important political, economic and tourism partner. Sri Lanka’s national interest is served by engaging all major powers through multi-alignment, economic pragmatism and national interest.

The economic stakes are equally important. BRICS is increasingly connecting economies through trade, investment, business partnerships, start-ups, technology and wider market access.

The wider shift is unmistakable. The Global South is rising.

Developing and emerging economies are becoming more economically powerful, politically influential and strategically important. The Global South is no longer merely watching the world order it is increasingly shaping it.

Sri Lanka also sought membership of the New Development Bank. For a country recovering from sovereign default and requiring substantial infrastructure investment, expanding access to long-term development finance should be a strategic priority. This is not a choice between the IMF, World Bank, Asian Development Bank or BRICS institutions.

The Government should now launch a coordinated, high-level BRICS diplomatic campaign. Engage every member government, identify reservations about Sri Lanka’s candidature, strengthen the economic case for admission and pursue NDB participation with equal urgency.

Colombo must ask one simple question. Why are we not in the room and what are we doing to make certain that when the door opens again, Sri Lanka walks through it?

Others saw the opportunity, lobbied and moved ahead. Sri Lanka cannot afford to remain a spectator. The Global South is building new economic bridges. We should be helping to build them not standing on the shore watching others cross.
