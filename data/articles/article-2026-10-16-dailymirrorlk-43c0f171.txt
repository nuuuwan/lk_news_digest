UK-styled insolvency law to dismantle exit barrier holding back private equity: StanChart CEO

New law rated better than India’s IBC on restructuring and recovery, but yet to be tested

Sri Lanka’s new UK-styled insolvency law, which goes live on 1 December, will remove a key exit barrier that has long kept private equity investors at bay, and open up a new ecosystem for investment, though the regime remains untested, Standard Chartered Bank Sri Lanka CEO Bingumal Thewarathanthri said.

Delivering a keynote at the THINC Sri Lanka & Maldives 2026 conference at Hilton Colombo, Thewarathanthri, who is also the Vice Chairman of Ceylon Chamber of Commerce, singled out private equity as a largely untapped source of capital for the country.

“There’s a huge private equity potential in the market,” he noted, but added that Sri Lankan companies “don’t want a lot of outsiders in the board,” a reluctance he observed had persisted for decades.

“There’s an entry barrier, there’s also an exit barrier. If things don’t go well, how can I get out?” he asked, describing Sri Lanka as a market that has so far been “here for good”, where investors who came in were effectively expected to stay.

“But now we are happy to say that we also have a bankruptcy law. If you have different ideas, you can exit,” Thewarathanthri told delegates.

Describing the legislation as very similar to UK law, he rated it ahead of India’s Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC). “From a restructuring and recovery standpoint, I would say it’s better than IBC code in India,” he remarked.

“Have we tested it? The answer is no. Will be live on 1st of December, we are waiting to see who will pull the trigger first,” he added. “But that will kind of create a new ecosystem for investors to come in.”

The Rescue, Rehabilitation and Insolvency (Corporate and Personal) Act, No. 12 of 2026 became law when Speaker Dr. Jagath Wickramaratne endorsed its certificate on 17 June, after Parliament passed the Bill following a debate on 6 May. The Act retires the Insolvency Ordinance of 1853 and moves the country away from a liquidation-first regime towards one built around rescue, replacing a statute that had stood for 173 years. The provisions establishing the Insolvency Regulatory Authority took effect immediately on certification.

Under the new framework, qualified administrators can run struggling companies while creditor enforcement is temporarily paused to create room for restructuring, and the Insolvency Regulatory Authority will oversee practitioners and set professional standards. The legislation also deals with receivership and cross-border insolvency, with the aim of improving predictability and confidence in the credit market.

Justice and National Integration Minister Harshana Nanayakkara, who presented the Bill, has positioned its alignment with global standards as a means of making the domestic credit market more attractive to international investors. During the Parliamentary debate, he held up India’s IBC of 2016 as a benchmark, saying it had improved recovery rates, shortened resolution timelines and lowered bank NPL ratios there. Sri Lanka’s law arrives a full decade after the Indian code, which Thewarathanthri believes the local law now surpasses.

However, Thewarathanthri cautioned that the country lacks a bench of seasoned insolvency practitioners, raising the likelihood that some expertise would have to be imported in the early stages.

“I don’t think Sri Lankan practitioners have had a full experience in terms of running a bankruptcy case,” he conceded. “We also don’t have the authority yet. We’re setting up the authority as we speak.”

Similar reservations surfaced during the passage of the law. Concerns were raised in Parliament that Sri Lanka’s existing parate execution framework could weaken the effectiveness of the new regime, while legal commentators have warned that its success will depend on the efficiency of the courts and the availability of trained insolvency professionals.

Even so, Thewarathanthri assured investors that many transactions would come their way.

Beyond the new law, he pointed to Sri Lankan family offices based overseas as another source of capital for those seeking partners, noting that such investors favoured private placements and other forms of co-investment.

For companies coming in from abroad, he recommended engaging a local consultancy and working through existing bankers with a presence in Sri Lanka, with facilities structured in centres such as Singapore or Mauritius where the parent’s funding sits.

‘‘If you can have a local partner, that will be good,’’ he advised.

“The investments will follow opportunities. That’s the way it’s going to work,” Thewarathanthri remarked, adding that the role of banks was to help create those opportunities and communicate Sri Lanka’s story better. (NF)
