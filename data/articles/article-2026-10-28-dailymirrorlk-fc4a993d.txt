Sri Lanka Open for Business-Turning Turbulence into Advantage

Tourism began the year on a strong footing, with arrivals recording robust

Every crisis changes who holds the advantage. It favours those who see early, act with purpose and keep their nerve. The Iran War 2026 is such a moment. It began on 28th February, when the United States and Israel struck Iran’s nuclear and military sites and killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei in the opening attack.

Iran answered with thousands of drones and missiles. By 10th May, the UAE’s Ministry of Defence said its air defence had intercepted nearly 2,850 of them. Iran also effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, and Gulf states that host U.S. forces were drawn in. Pakistan brokered a ceasefire in April. In mid-June, the Islamabad Memorandum opened a 60-day window for talks.

Fighting resumed in early July over control of the Strait, and the window lapsed in mid-August with no settlement. Mediated contacts continue, but no agreement has emerged.

Sri Lanka sits astride one of the world’s busiest maritime routes, close to the Gulf and long committed to neutrality. Any case for it must nonetheless rest on credibility and a realistic assessment of its present circumstances. Sri Lanka has made significant strides since its sovereign debt crisis in 2022, demonstrating resilience and a commitment to reform. Strong economic ties with the Gulf, including substantial remittance inflows, support foreignexchange earnings, and the country is pursuing energy diversification to strengthen long-term security.

Sri Lanka’s case rests on five strengths that few competitors can match in combination. Its strategic location at the crossroads of the Gulf and East Asia is supported by well-established ports and international airports. Its time zone bridges Gulf and Asian business hours, and English remains widely used in commerce, law and higher education. A highly skilled workforce is complemented by a global network of Sri Lankan professionals with extensive Gulf experience and connections. Cordial and constructive relations across the region reinforce its reputation as a trusted and neutral partner. Colombo also offers quality international schools, world-class private healthcare and modern residential facilities that compare favourably with long-established business hubs.

None of these attributes is unique in isolation. Their value lies in the combination. The opportunities are significant, but they will be realised only through continued preparation, policy stability and a story told withconfidence and clear evidence.

Ports are our oldest asset, and position is our greatest advantage. When conflictmakes routes costlier and riskier, shipping lines rethink their networks. Thegains for Sri Lanka will be modest ones, won one carrier and one cargo owner at a time. That takes an offer, not a map. We should present three ports with three clear roles. Colombo offers transhipment and connections. Hambantota, still underused for its cost, suits bunkering, bonded storage, ship servicing and bulk logistics.

Trincomalee’s deep natural harbour offers longer-term energy and industrialprospects, building on the existing arrangement with India and the UAE, if it is developed openly and with care for regional sensitivities. Fair tariffs, reliable berths, quick customs clearance and steady labour relations will keep diverted carriers once the crisis ends. Insurance help, ship supplies, crew changes and repairs are where margins are earned and loyalty is built.

Mattala Airport has been offered to Gulf airlines as a back-up hub. The idea deserves a hard commercial look, not enthusiasm. In March, Gulf airlines asked about waived landing fees, but by month’s end officials admitted that no carrier had committed. They also cautioned that the apron holds only about ten aircraft, fewer if wide-bodies dominate. Airlines will move only for a proven business case covering demand, ground handling, fuel, customs, security and competition with Colombo. The better course is to follow up the private expressions of interest planned for Mattala in cargo, aircraft maintenance and aviation training, while strengthening Colombo’s air-cargo and maintenance capacity.

Tourism began the year on a strong footing, with arrivals recording robust growth in the first two months of 2026. However, airspace closures linked to the Middle East conflict disrupted the Gulf transit hubs through which roughly a third of Sri Lanka’s international visitors typically travel, reducing their share to just over 21 per cent by June. In July, the Government revised its 2026 tourism target to 2.5 million arrivals and US$3.5 billion in earnings.

The Sri Lanka Tourism Promotion Bureau subsequently adopted a more ambitious industry goal of 2.7 million arrivals and US$4.2 billion in revenue, although arrivals in September remained marginally below last year’s level.

The response should be to broaden market reach and strengthen connectivity through expanded promotion in India, Europe and East Asia, increased direct air links, and a consistent message of safety, stability and hospitality. That message must be conveyed with sensitivity and restraint, never as though Sri Lanka benefits from the difficulties faced by others. Longer stays, wellness tourism, culture, nature and remote-work opportunities should all form part of the country’s appeal.

The Middle East has long been a centre of global wealth, and periods of uncertainty often encourage investors to diversify geographically. Whether Sri Lanka benefits from that shift will depend less on incentives than on confidence. Investors place a premium on policy consistency, the sanctity of contracts, efficient regulatory processes and macroeconomic stability. Sri Lanka must therefore demonstrate its seriousness through sustained reforms, visible resolution of legacy disputes and adherence to the IMF programme.

Several sectors are naturally well positioned to attract Gulf capital, including maritime services, wealth and fund management, hospitality, logistics, real estate, renewable energy, and knowledge-based industries such as IT and business process outsourcing. Gulf family offices, particularly those based in Dubai and Abu Dhabi, control substantial pools of capital and frequently operate at the intersection of private enterprise and sovereign investment.

Attracting established companies requires a more targeted approach. Promising prospects include regional headquarters, treasury and back-office operations seeking geographic diversification, shipping and logistics firms adapting to changing trade patterns, technology and shared-services companies requiring resilient business continuity locations, and investment firms looking for strategic regional platforms. There is also significant potential among South Asian professionals and entrepreneurs in the Gulf, including many Sri Lankans, who may wish to establish operations closer to home. Each segment has distinct priorities, requiring tailored engagement rather than a one-size-fits-all pitch. To seize these opportunities, the Board of Investment, the Foreign Ministry, the Colombo Port City Economic Commission and Sri Lanka’s missions in the Gulf should work in concert to identify and pursue priority prospects, drawing on the networks of Sri Lankan professionals overseas, chambers of commerce and industry bodies. Well-prepared delegations presenting practical, investor-ready solutions are likely to achieve more than broad promotional campaigns. Sri Lanka is also competing with other destinations pursuing the  same opportunities, which makes timely and decisive action essential.

Investors compare effort before they compare incentives. Every extra counter, form and unanswered query raises the cost of choosing Sri Lanka. Singapore is a useful benchmark, and many of its ideas can be adapted to our scale.

In Singapore, the Accounting and Corporate Regulatory Authority handles business registration, and simple incorporations are often completed very quickly through a fully digital system. Sri Lanka should establish the Board of Investment as the lead gateway for all relocating and incoming enterprises, working in full coordination with the Colombo Port City Economic Commission despite their differing statutory mandates. Investors should encounter a single, integrated system rather than multiple institutions. Representatives of the Registrar of Companies, Inland Revenue, Customs, Immigration, the Central Bank, the Central Environmental Authority and key utilities should operate through a unified one-stop platform, in person and online, with delegated authority to take decisions, issue approvals and resolve issues promptly. What investors seek is not another layer of administration, but certainty, speed and accountability.

Singapore’s GoBusiness platform gathers more than 300 government services into one gateway. We should build our own: one application, one digital identity, documents filed once and shared, and live tracking of every approval.

Technology alone does not guarantee speed, so each approval needs a published deadline. If a deadline is missed, approval should be deemed granted, or the case escalated to a named senior official.

Each serious investor should have a case manager from registration through tax, visas, banking, premises and utilities, and support for staff and families, including schooling and housing. Ready-made packages for each type of investor should state the approvals, timelines and incentives. Performance should be public, with monthly figures on applications, approvals and processing times, independently verifiable. Strong leadership at Cabinet level will be essential to coordinate stakeholders, maintain momentum and clear administrative hurdles. Singapore built its reputation over decades. We need not copy all of it. A smaller, well-run window, launched quickly and improved in the open, would send a clear signal: come to Sri Lanka, and your first decision will also be your simplest.

The fuel shock is the strongest argument for accelerating Sri Lanka’s energy transition, which should be viewed as a matter of national security rather than environmental policy. Every megawatt of domestically generated solar, wind or hydropower reduces exposure to external shocks and strengthens the balance of payments. Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) are equally important: they store surplus renewable energy when production is high and release it at peak demand, enhancing grid reliability. Investments in BESS, grid modernisation, transmission infrastructure and bankable powerpurchase agreements deserve as much attention as new generation capacity.

Conventional resilience remains essential. Sri Lanka needs a strategic fuel reserve, supported by expanded storage capacity and a clear, publicly communicated implementation timetable. Fuel procurement should be diversified across suppliers and transport routes, while improvements in energy efficiency and public transport reduce dependence on imported fuels.

The transition is also a significant investment opportunity. Gulf investors possess deep expertise in the energy sector, and with transparent procurement, predictable tariffs, bankable contracts and a supportive regulatory framework, Sri Lanka can become a regional hub for renewable energy and storage investment.

Remittances are our largest single source of foreign exchange, and the Gulf supplies roughly 45 per cent of them, according to the Central Bank. Our embassies and consulates need contingency plans, lines of communication with migrant communities, and evacuation capacity ready before it is needed. Returning workers bring skills, savings and experience in hospitality, construction, healthcare and driving. A structured programme of skills certification, job placement, start-up finance and recognition of foreign experience can turn a burden into an asset. Over time, certified skills and language training can open better-paid, safer destinations in East Asia and Europe.

A strong offer that nobody hears about wins no business. Sri Lanka should carry one national message, “open, neutral and ready”, through the Board of Investment, the Ports Authority, the tourism bodies, the airlines, the chambers of commerce and every mission abroad. Presentations in the Gulf should be led by people who can commit the government to terms on the spot.

The stories of early arrivals should be told, because a satisfied investor is a small country’s best advertisement. Promotion must follow delivery, never come before it, but delivery that goes unpromoted will go unnoticed.

None of this will happen by itself. Success rests on three foundations: economic stability, capable institutions and disciplined coordination. Stability means adequate foreign-exchange and fuel reserves, fiscal discipline and fidelity to the reform agenda. Capable institutions mean approvals, licensing and customs that are fast, predictable and free of discretionary delay. Coordination is vital because these opportunities span ministries, agencies and the private sector. Without a single accountable owner and measurable timelines, momentum will be lost, and a published, consistently implemented national plan will inspire more confidence than scattered announcements.

Sri Lanka does not seek to replace the Gulf’s leading commercial centres. Its advantage lies in being a strategically located complement to them. For regional headquarters, shared-services centres, technology firms, professional services and business-continuity operations, it offers skilled talent, lower operating costs and an attractive living environment, making it a compelling platform for growth and diversification.

Global upheaval has a way of redrawing the economic map well after the turbulence subsides. For Sri Lanka, the real question is not whether change will arrive, but whether we will be ready to turn it to our advantage. Guided by foresight, discipline and a common national purpose, we can modernise our ports, broaden our economic base, secure our energy future, and cement Sri Lanka’s reputation as a trusted centre for trade, investment and enterprise.

The turbulence in the Middle East is already influencing the flow of investment, trade, shipping, and energy across our region. Sri Lanka’s strategic location, longstanding neutrality, and enduring connections with both the Gulf and Asia place it in a uniquely advantageous position. Yet opportunity alone is never sufficient. Real success will depend on our ability to act with clarity, coordination, and determination. If we do so, this period of uncertainty will be remembered not as a crisis that Sri Lanka endured, but as a turning point that Sri Lanka seized, laying the foundations for greater prosperity, resilience, and national advancement for decades to come.
