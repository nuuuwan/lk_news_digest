More than half of Sri Lanka’s older persons have chronic diseases

Colombo, Oct. 1 (Daily Mirror) - More than half of Sri Lankans aged 60 and above are affected by at least one chronic disease, highlighting the growing healthcare needs of the country’s rapidly ageing population, according to the 2024 Census of Population and Housing.

The Department of Census and Statistics (DCS) said non-communicable diseases (NCDs) were more prevalent among older people and women.

According to the Census, 19.2% of Sri Lanka’s total population, or around 4.18 million people, were reported to be suffering from non-communicable diseases in 2024.

Among the NCDs reported, high blood pressure was the most prevalent at 10.1%, followed by diabetes at 8.5%. The DCS noted that these conditions were more widespread among females and older persons.

The Census specifically found that more than half of the population aged 60 years and above had at least one chronic disease.

The figures come as Sri Lanka undergoes a significant demographic shift, with people aged 60 and above accounting for 18% of the country's population, according to the 2024 Census.

Population Association of Sri Lanka President and University of Colombo Professor of Demography Prof. Manori Kaluthantiri Weeratunga said longer life expectancy did not necessarily mean that older people would live without illness.

She said some older persons had to manage several health conditions at the same time, alongside age-related changes in mobility, hearing, vision or memory.

Prof. Weeratunga said Sri Lanka therefore needed healthcare services that could support older people continuously and help them navigate the different services they required.

She identified strong primary healthcare, regular reviews of medicines, rehabilitation, mental health support, hearing and vision care, and assistance with daily activities as important areas requiring attention.

She also stressed that prevention, proper nutrition and physical activity should receive attention well before people reach old age.

“Healthcare should ask more than ‘What illness do you have?’ It should also ask, ‘What do you want to continue doing?’” she said.

With appropriate treatment, assistive devices and a supportive environment, many older people can continue to remain active while managing chronic conditions, Prof. Weeratunga said.

The Census findings also highlight the importance of adapting healthcare services to the needs of an ageing population rather than focusing only on treating individual illnesses.

Sri Lanka recorded a total population of 21,781,800 in the 2024 Census, which was conducted with the census moment set at midnight on December 18, 2024. The final Census report was released by the DCS in April 2026.

The findings come ahead of the International Day of Older Persons 2026, which focuses internationally on “The Age of Longevity: Rethinking Systems for Longer Lives.”

The data underlines the need for Sri Lanka to consider not only how long people live, but also how healthcare and support systems can help older people maintain independence and quality of life while living with chronic diseases.
