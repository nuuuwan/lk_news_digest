Robert Knox: Chronicler par excellence

The quip that “once the mud is washed off, the man working in a paddy field is fit to be king” which famously evolved into the colloquial paraphrase, did not originate from a Lankan familiar with local parlance. It came instead from Robert Knox, an Englishman, who spent nineteen years as a prisoner in the then Kandyan kingdom before escaping and writing his celebrated work “An Historical Relation of the island Ceylon.” It noted that “taking a ploughman from Udunuwara or Yatinuwara and washing the dirt off would make him fit for kingship.”

Although he limited the leadership potential of the farmer to two specific regions, Knox’s remark has endured for centuries. In fact, it re-echoed recently in Parliament when the mounting crisis of  paddy cultivators ignited a heated debate illustrating the continued usage of Knox’s quote.

Published in London in 1681, “An Historical Relation of the island Ceylon,” was the oldest major English language book which broadened the comprehension of the  Lankan way of life. It provided European readers with an unprecedented insight into Sri Lankan society, culture and the economy during the seventeenth century long before the British established control over the island.

What makes Knox’s episode particularly significant is that his captivity   which led him to write the book was linked with a major turning point in Lankan history. In fact, if not for the unforeseen turn of events  that occurred which made the reigning King, Rajasinghe 11 (1629-1687CE) ill-tempered, “increasingly moody, suspicious and tyrannical and caused him to develop a passion to imprison Europeans,” Knox would not have become a victim of royal wrath, and circumstances would not have led him to write the celebrated book.

Literally, it was one of the earliest works written based on firsthand experience. It introduced Sinhala words such as betel, veddah, rattan, wandura and rilawa to the Oxford English Dictionary, was translated into German in 1689, Dutch in 1692 and French in 1693 and having inspired Daniel Defoe to write his famous novel “Robinson Cruso,” the book merits deeper discourse and renewed attention during the current Literary Month.

As to his fate, Knox, in his autobiography recalled: “The Ann being hit by a storm, was forced ashore…..that fatal voyage in which I lost my father and myself and the prime of my time……”  Knox’s father and the crew of sixteen sailors were awaiting repairs in the Kottiyar Bay in the east coast not knowing that they had arrived during a period of political turmoil. An ambitious strategy employed by King Rajasinghe to expel the Portuguese from areas under their rule had misfired. The failure of his political gamble resulted in a major transition in  Lankan political history. The move in fact, led  Portugese rule in Sri Lanka’s coastal area to be replaced by the Dutch.

The unexpected development in which the Dutch kept the Portuguese territory they won instead of handing them over to the King as promised, transformed the King to a “disgruntled terror” as Knox described. The new development led people to coin the phrase “giving ginger and receiving chillies in return.”

Knox in his book described the situation vividly:  “Great vexation they gave the King by their irruptions into his Dominions. Great mischief they did him, the often times great loss on their sides. Great battles have been lost and won between them with great destruction of men on both parts. But being greatly distressed, at last he (Rajasinghe) sent and called in the Hollanders to his aid.”

Of the transition of power Knox wrote: The present King being wearied and  overmatched with the Portugese, sent for them (The Dutch) into his aid long ago from Batavia. And they did him good service,  but they feathered their own nests by the means and are now possessed of all sea coasts and the considerable Territories there unto adjoining.“

Historian K. M. de Silva in “A History of Sri Lanka” explained as to how the situation developed to a point where Rajasinghe’s sole objective became the expulsion of the Portuguese. To achieve this goal,  he was willing to pay a heavy price. K. M. de Silva wrote: The Portuguese, confined to the West Coast since they landed in 1505, had gradually strengthened their position. The conquest of the Jaffnapatan gave them to control over the lucrative pearl fisheries and the island’s elephant trade. Jaffna at the time, was the main market for elephants captured in the island.

With the interest to expand, the Portugese had been over the years attempting to make intrusions to the Kandyan Kingdom. Their attempt to install Dona Catherina as the reigning Monarch was frustrated by Konnappu Bandara who  married  her and secured his claim to the Kandyan Kingdom. Having installed himself as Vimaladharmasuriya I, (1591-1604CE), he strengthened the Kandyan Kingdom defeating Portugese hopes of conquering Kandy.

Senarath (1604-1635CE), his successor,  continued Vimaladharmasuriya’s policy of being anti-Portugese. Nevertheless, in order to prevent Portugese intrusions to the Kandyan Kingdom, Senarath signed a treaty with the Portugese in 1617 and acknowledged their authority over the maritime provinces. According to K.M. de Silva, Senarath even agreed to pay tribute to the Portugese with a promise to deny entry to their enemies.

According to “A History of Sri Lanka,” hopes of the Portuguese to subjugate Kandy renewed with the arrival of the Dutch in the Asian waters. Although this followed with regular forays, the Kandyan Kingdom however, continued to retain its independence.

It was with the ascension of Rajasinghe II that signs of change in the Kandyan policy towards the Portugese appeared. Having resorted to a more aggressive policy, he conducted intrusions deep into Portugese held areas with the aim of driving them out of the island.

In the first Treaty signed in 1633 with the Portuguese however, Rajasinghe recognised Portugese control over the eastern ports. His mood appeared to have changed as the King, adopting a more aggressive policy, sought foreign assistance to drive out the Portugese. This led him to commence negotiations with the Dutch which culminated in the Treaty of 1638.

The Dutch rapidly captured Trincomalee and Batticaloa in 1639 from the Portuguese and handed them over to King Rajasinghe. However, when the Dutch won over Galle and Negombo (in 1640) they refused to transfer control claiming that the Kandyan ruler had not paid the agreed military expenses for the previous expedition. As historian K. M. de Silva notes,   the eastern ports were not major cinnamon producing areas,  whereas Galle and Negombo commanded access to some of the richest cinnamon lands on the island.

In 1656, the Portugese Fort in Colombo surrendered,  but it took two more years to eliminate them completely,  ending over 150 years of Portugese presence in the island. The last to capitulate was the Jaffna Fort.

Well aware of the growing tension between the Dutch and the Kandyans, the English East India Company, doing trade in the Asian waters, sought opportunities to establish a trading presence in Sri Lanka. Their negotiations with Rajasinghe focused particularly on obtaining access to Trincomalee. Knox’s vessel “The Ann” which arrived on the eastern coast in 1659, was detained by the Kandyans followed by another in 1680, the English thus becoming victims of the increasingly complex political rivalry among the Dutch, the English and the Kandyan Kingdom.

While the English refused to concede to Dutch claims of the trade monopoly, their attempt to gain entry on the east coast of Sri Lanka made the Dutch rethink of the strategic importance of the eastern ports -- at least Trincomalee and, if permitted, Batticaloa.

Fortunately for the Dutch, according to K. M. De Silva, a rebellion against Rajasinghe in 1665 compelled him to seek Dutch assistance. Dutch troops in Kandy and naval support in the East coast enabled the Dutch to consolidate control over the entire coastline. From 1675 however, Rajasinghe recommenced attacks on the Dutch, demonstrating shrewdness in his choice of targets and eliminating much of the Dutch conquered areas. By 1677, however, the Dutch were following a policy of inactivity,  but continued to be vigilant. And Rajasinghe in his last years of reign, sought a more reconciliatory relationship with them.

Regarded as an exception among Kandyan rulers for his commitment to a policy of resistance toward the Westerners, Rajasinghe however appeared to have changed his attitude even towards those captured. The allowance as a result gave Knox to enjoy a degree of freedom unusual for a prisoner,  and enabled him to observe Sri Lankan society with remarkable detail and sympathy.

During the nineteen years as what was termed as a “free Prisoner,” Knox cultivated crops and knitted caps spending his life as a petty trader while exploring possible escape routes.

Following the publication of his book in London, some questioned  whether Knox had truly disliked living in Sri Lanka among strangers. If his overriding ambition had been escape, how had he produced such nuanced and empathetic observations of the people, their customs and   daily life?  His illustrations even included a portrait of Rajasinghe II whom he described had a “swelling belly, somewhat bald…..and a large comely beard with great whiskers, in conclusion a very comely man.”

Knox’s years in captivity evidently had not diminished his appetite for travel. Following his escape, he had undertaken at least five more voyages to the East. He died in 1720 and lived long enough to bask in the well deserved popularity of his book. His prediction “it being ye the only thing that will keep my name in memory in ye world,” proved correct as, nearly three and a half centuries later, Robert Knox remains remembered not simply as a prisoner who escaped from the Kandyan Kingdom but as one of the most perceptive chroniclers of seventeenth century Sri Lanka whose observations continue to illuminate the island’s history, the society and parlance.
