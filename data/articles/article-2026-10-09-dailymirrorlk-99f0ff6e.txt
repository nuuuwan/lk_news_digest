India’s rum row raises question of whether Sri Lankan consumers know what they drink

Local whisky, rum and brandy are built on flavoured neutral ethanol, says industry figure

1912 Excise Ordinance licenses flavouring and colouring of liquor but is silent on labelling

Consumers ought to be made aware that locally made whisky, rum and brandy are produced by adding colour and flavour to neutral ethanol, a practice similar to the one India’s food regulator is acting against on the grounds that it misleads the public, a prominent industry figure from one of the country’s top distillers, who wished to remain anonymous told Mirror Business.

The expert, who questioned whether it is ethical to market a flavoured spirit under the name of the spirit it imitates, cautioned that doing so risks misleading the consumer about what is in the bottle. A genuine spirit derives its character from the raw material it is distilled from—be it grapes for brandy, sugarcane for rum, grain for whisky or agave for tequila. That character is retained when the distillate is drawn off at a strength of around 64 to 70 percent before it is aged and allowed to settle. Once the spirit is rectified to a strength of 96 percent, however, that character is stripped away altogether, leaving behind neutral ethanol that “can be flavoured to anything,” the expert pointed out, citing the local practice of converting distillate from sugar distilleries such as Gal Oya into extra neutral alcohol before colour and flavour are added.

The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) has recently prohibited the manufacture and sale of fresh stocks of selected variants of Old Monk, McDowell’s No.1, Royal Challenge, Antiquity Blue and Bagpiper, after its tests detected flavouring added to recreate the spirit’s character. The regulator maintains there is “no internationally recognised manufacturing practice” of adding rum flavour to rum and wants such products sold under names such as rum-flavoured spirit, according to Indian media reports.

Sri Lanka’s law has recognised the practice for over a century. The Excise Ordinance of 1912, which continues to govern the industry, defines manufacture to include redistillation and every process of rectifying, flavouring, blending or colouring liquor, all of which require a licence.

However, the colonial-era law is silent on how a flavoured spirit ought to be described to the consumer, as it carries neither definitions of rum, whisky, brandy or gin nor any labelling provisions. Its term “foreign liquor” is a duty category covering all liquor other than country liquor and does not describe what is in the bottle.

A standard ratified by the Council of the Sri Lanka Standards Institution (SLSI) in January 2021, SLS 1687 Part 7, nevertheless treats such products as a category of their own. It defines emulated foreign liquor as a product that may carry flavouring, added sugar and other additives so that it resembles a distilled liquor of the same type, with distilled spirit of not less than 96 percent alcohol by volume as its base.

Under its labelling clause, the common name on each bottle is to carry the words “emulated foreign liquor” alongside the spirit it imitates, together with the statement “Made in Sri Lanka”—which is effectively the disclosure the FSSAI is seeking to enforce in India.

Most Sri Lanka Standards remain voluntary unless made compulsory through a gazette. No gazette giving the liquor standard legal force could be traced, leaving the use of the prescribed name to the discretion of producers. The Excise Department told the Committee on Public Accounts that liquor standards would be gazetted through an excise notification, according to a committee report tabled in Parliament in May 2022. It could not be immediately established how widely the name appears on bottles in the market.

The standard also lists only natural flavouring substances and flavouring preparations among its optional ingredients, making no provision for the artificial or nature-identical flavours that triggered the Indian action. It is also silent on age statements and does not extend to imported spirits.
