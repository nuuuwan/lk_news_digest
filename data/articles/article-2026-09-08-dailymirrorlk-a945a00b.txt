Beyond Who Wins: Judicial Reform, and the Future of Judiciary

People will always disagree with some court decisions. That’s normal in a democracy

Sri Lanka’s Constitution has already been changed 21 times, and now a Twenty-Second Amendment is being proposed. As usual, this has brought a lot of arguing between the government and the Opposition. But under all that noise is an idea that isn’t getting enough attention: a government is not the same thing as the State.

Governments come and go. Majorities in parliament change, parties rise and fall, and today’s ruling party can become tomorrow’s Opposition. But the State, as set out in the constitution, is supposed to last through all of that. This matters most for the judiciary, which must answer to the law, not to whichever government is currently in power.

That’s why the debate about judges’ retirement age, the main change in this amendment, can’t be treated as just a small administrative fix. The judiciary doesn’t belong to the government or the Opposition. It belongs to the State itself, and its credibility depends on staying clearly separate from both.

This doesn’t mean Sri Lanka’s courts have no real problems. They do: backlogs of cases, unfilled judge positions, poor infrastructure, slow appointments, and unequal access to justice. These are real issues, not made up. A judiciary that doesn’t work efficiently also weakens the rule of law — efficiency matters,  too. So the real question isn’t whether reform is needed. It clearly is. The harder question is whether this particular reform can happen without weakening the independence that makes the judiciary worth reforming in the first place.

Any constitutional change to the courts should pass three tests. Is it actually necessary to fix a real problem? Is the method being used the right size for the problem, not bigger than needed? And are there enough safeguards to stop unintended consequences? A government can have a good goal and still choose a tool that’s too big or too intrusive for that goal. Mixing these two things up is where much of today’s argument comes from.

Raising the retirement age for judges might help with the shortage of judges and let experienced ones stay on at a time when the courts are struggling. But convenience isn’t the only thing that matters when making constitutional decisions. The Constitution exists to stop short-term political pressure from reshaping institutions that are meant to outlast any one government. If the same goal -- fewer vacancies, keeping experienced judges, can be reached in a way that interferes less with how long judges serve, that option should be seriously considered first.

Appointments, how long judges serve, accountability, and how judges leave office are not separate issues to be bargained over one by one. They’re all part of one constitutional system. And underneath all of them is something harder to legislate: public trust. A reform that boosts efficiency in the short term but damages public trust in judicial independence could end up costing the country more than it gains.

The opposite is also true, a reform that improves both efficiency and independence at the same time would be a real success, not just a political compromise.

None of this means we should assume the government is acting in bad faith, or that every reform should automatically be treated with suspicion. Courts, unlike elected branches, have no independent political power of their own. Their authority comes entirely from the public accepting their rulings as fair.

People will always disagree with some court decisions. That’s normal in a democracy, not a sign of dysfunction. But an independent judiciary can handle that disagreement and keep people’s respect even when a ruling is unpopular. If a judiciary looks like it’s bending to political convenience, it loses that protection, and even its best decisions become open to suspicion.

So here’s the real test: not whether you trust the current government to use this new power responsibly, but whether you’d be just as comfortable with any future government, of any party, any ideology, having the same power. Today’s ruling party may be tomorrow’s Opposition, and people who support expanding government power now may one day be on the receiving end of it. That’s exactly why constitutional design shouldn’t depend on trusting a particular set of people in office.

If the amendment eventually goes to a referendum, voters deserve a clear, honest question, explained well enough to think it through: What problem is this meant to solve? Why this method, specifically? What risks have been raised? What safeguards exist? And were gentler options considered and rejected, for good reasons?

A referendum on constitutional matters needs a higher standard of public debate than a normal election, because voters aren’t just picking a side; they’re deciding the boundaries within which all future political and judicial power will work. They deserve to be treated as people making a serious constitutional choice, not just as voters to be won over.

Whether the retirement age ends up at 67 and 65 stays the same, or changes to something else,  is actually a smaller question than the one Sri Lanka should be asking: what does this whole argument show about how the country handles constitutional change under pressure? If the government’s case for modernising the justice system deserves a fair hearing, so does the Bar Association’s warning that some parts of this could hurt judicial independence. Assuming either side is acting in bad faith only weakens the debate. A constitutional system is healthiest when disagreements like this are settled through institutions and reasoned argument, not street pressure.

Whatever happens, the underlying problems, case delays, unfilled positions, limited resources, uneven public trust, will still be there, waiting for real attention long after this argument fades from the news. What Sri Lanka needs isn’t a one-time fix, but a lasting agreement that judicial reform and judicial independence aren’t competing goals, but two parts of the same unfinished job, achievable only through good laws, transparent appointments, enough resources, responsible political behaviour, and safeguards built to last no matter which government is in power.

The political noise around this amendment will eventually die down. Constitutional changes, once made, tend to last much longer. That’s exactly why this debate deserves more care than it’s getting, and why success won’t be measured by who wins the argument this year, but by whether, once it’s over, Sri Lanka is left with a judiciary that is more efficient, more accessible, more trusted, and still independent enough to hold every government, including the ones not yet elected, within the bounds of the Constitution.
