A SpaceX rocket is about to crash into the moon tonight — Can you watch it?

MEN'S JOURNAL - A SpaceX rocket stage is expected to slam into the moon early Wednesday morning in a rare event that astronomers have been tracking for months.

The Falcon 9 upper stage, which launched in January 2025 to send two private lunar landers toward the moon, is now on course to impact the lunar surface at approximately 2:35 a.m. ET on Aug. 5 after spending nearly 19 months drifting through space.

While there’s no danger to Earth, scientists say the collision offers a unique opportunity to learn more about the moon, and skywatchers may even have a chance to glimpse the aftermath.

The Falcon 9 rocket launched on Jan. 15, 2025, carrying Firefly Aerospace’s Blue Ghost lunar lander and Japanese company ispace’s Resilience lander.

After sending both of the spacecrafts on their way, the rocket’s upper stage was expected to eventually leave Earth’s vicinity. Instead, a combination of gravity and solar influences gradually altered its path until it was pulled toward the moon.

Independent astronomers first predicted the impact earlier this year, and NASA later confirmed the rocket stage was indeed on a collision course. The booster is expected to strike the lunar surface near Einstein Crater, close to the moon’s western edge.

The impact itself will occur on the moon’s sunlit side, making the brief flash difficult to spot from Earth. Experts say the actual collision will likely last no more than a second.

However, astronomers are hopeful that the collision will send a plume of debris high enough above the lunar surface that it could be visible through large telescopes, and possibly even binoculars under ideal viewing conditions.

Because the impact site sits near the moon’s western limb, any bright ejecta could stand out against the darkness of space. Researchers around the world are expected to monitor the event, though there is no official public livestream of the impact itself.

The Falcon 9 upper stage weighs roughly 4,000 kilograms (about 8,800 pounds) and is expected to hit the moon at more than 5,400 mph.

Scientists estimate the impact will create a crater roughly 65 to 90 feet wide, while ejecting lunar material hundreds of feet — and possibly much farther — into the air.

NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter won’t witness the impact in real time, but it is expected to photograph the area afterward to compare before-and-after images and measure the newly formed crater.

Researchers hope the collision will provide valuable data about how impacts behave on the moon, where the lack of an atmosphere creates very different conditions than on Earth.

The observations could also help scientists better understand potential hazards for future lunar missions, including NASA’s Artemis program, as more spacecraft begin traveling to (and operating around) the moon.
