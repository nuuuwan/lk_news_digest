When politicians leave, who answers for the past?

Colombo, Nov. 18 (Daily Mirror) - Former Power and Energy Minister Kanchana Wijesekera’s very recent statement that he has no intention of returning to politics has prompted discussion about his departure from public life. In a post on X, he reflected on his years in politics, his move to the corporate sector and the different priorities he now enjoys. He is entitled to make that choice.

But the reaction to such announcements raises a question about Sri Lanka’s political culture. Why does a politician’s decision to leave public office sometimes become an occasion for praise and celebration? Should stepping away from politics be treated as an achievement in itself? And should the public conversation not also examine what a politician did while holding power?

Wijesekera entered Parliament in 2015 and served until 2024. He held ministerial positions, including the Power and Energy portfolio from 2022 until September 2024. Those were significant years. His time in office coincided with the economic crisis, fuel shortages, long queues, power cuts and the difficult task of stabilising the energy sector.

It would be unfair to suggest that one minister, or one administration, alone caused Sri Lanka’s economic collapse. The Central Bank and the International Monetary Fund have identified a combination of long-standing factors. These included chronic fiscal and external deficits, excessive borrowing, tax reductions, depleted reserves and delayed policy responses. The crisis resulted from decisions made over many years by successive governments and institutions.

That is precisely why political accountability cannot be limited to one individual or party. Many people who occupied positions of power had been in politics for a long time. They were a part of governments that made decisions, supported budgets, defended policies or remained silent when warning signs were visible. The public has a right to ask what responsibility each office-holder accepts for the conditions the country was left in.

A minister may point to difficult decisions taken during the crisis, and some decisions could not be avoided if essential services were to continue. In the energy sector, tariff revisions and reforms were defended as necessary to address financial losses and improve State institutions. Such decisions should be assessed on their merits, results and impact on ordinary people. Neither praise nor criticism should replace that examination.

There is a difference between acknowledging a person’s contribution and glorifying a politician. Public service deserves recognition when it produces measurable benefits. But political office is not a personal favour granted to  people. It is a responsibility entrusted by voters, funded by taxpayers and subject to scrutiny. A departure announcement alone cannot establish whether that service deserves praise; the record, decisions and results must also be considered.

Sri Lanka has often treated politicians as celebrities. Their statements, family events and personal decisions can receive more attention than the policies they introduced or promises they failed to fulfil. This culture places personalities above institutions. It also turns public debate into a contest over popularity.

The issue is not that former politicians speak about their experiences or express gratitude to supporters. Nor should anyone be criticised merely for moving into the private sector. The corporate world is a legitimate career path, provided there are no conflicts of interest, misuse of confidential information or improper benefits arising from public office. Transparency is especially important when former ministers enter industries they once regulated or oversaw.

What is missing from many political exits is a serious account of public responsibility. Before closing a chapter, a politician could explain what was achieved, what went wrong, which decisions they would defend and what lessons they have learned. The public, too, must ask better questions. Did policies improve lives? Were public funds used responsibly? Were warnings taken seriously? Were institutions strengthened, or were problems passed on to the next government? These questions should apply equally to politicians from every party, including those who remain in office and those who have left.

A change of career does not erase a public record, just as a period in office should not define a person’s entire life. Wijesekera is free to pursue the corporate path he has chosen. His record, like that of every former minister, remains open to public assessment.
