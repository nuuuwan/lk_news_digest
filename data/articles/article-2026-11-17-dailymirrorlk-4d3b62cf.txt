Rubber traders push for early extension of plantation leases

CRTA says leases due to expire in 2045 are already a consideration for long-term estate investment

Ageing trees, insufficient replanting and rising costs weigh on domestic rubber production

Traders seek help tracing rubber supplies to meet EU deforestation rules

Sri Lanka’s rubber traders have called for an early extension of the land leases held by the Regional Plantation Companies, saying the uncertainty over what happens when the agreements expire in 2045 could hold back investment in replanting and estate modernisation.

The Colombo Rubber Traders’ Association (CRTA) made the call in a statement marking 150 years of natural rubber cultivation in Sri Lanka.

It said clearer terms on long-term land tenure would encourage the companies to replace the ageing trees, improve yields and upgrade infrastructure.

The association gave no proposed lease duration or estimate of the investment at stake.

“However, this anniversary must be more than a celebration of our past. It must serve as a renewed commitment to revitalising the industry, addressing the challenges confronting our stakeholders and securing a commercially sustainable, competitive and prosperous future for Sri Lankan natural rubber,” CRTA Chairman Harin de Silva said.

The association said productivity had stagnated or declined in many traditional growing areas, where the producers face the rising costs, labour shortages and inadequate use of rain guards, which allow tapping to continue in wet weather. It called for better access to quality planting material and practical support for the smallholders to keep cultivation commercially viable.

The supply challenge extends beyond the estates. Natural rubber feeds the domestic manufacturing industries that produce solid tyres, surgical gloves and latex bedding.

The association called for investment in technology and product development, alongside efforts to preserve the quality of premium raw rubber grades such as latex crepe.

“A competitive industry will depend not only on producing more rubber but also on producing it efficiently, maintaining consistent quality, meeting international standards and delivering products that create sustainable value for all participants in the supply chain,” de Silva said.

The CRTA also urged Sri Lanka to assess whether rubber could be grown in suitable drier areas outside the traditional wet-zone growing regions. It said expansion would depend on climate and land-use assessments and the development of planting material and cultivation practices suited to those areas.

For the exporters, the association flagged a separate pressure on market access. It called for coordinated data collection and technical assistance to help the growers, processors and traders document the origin of rubber supplies and meet the traceability and geolocation requirements of the European Union Deforestation Regulation.
