Historic Shenzhen summit as world teeters between  war and peace

Putin, Xi and Trump ahead of their historic meeting in China’s tech capital, Shenzhen

International relations are so interconnected today that it is increasingly difficult to analyse developments in one country without referring to developments in other parts of the world, regardless of distance or significance.

The world appears to be a political island when the dots are connected. Yesterday, history was made as the world’s three most powerful leaders, from China, Russia and the United States, held the first-ever trilateral talks on the sidelines of the APEC (Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation) summit in Shenzhen, China’s technology capital. Needless to say, numerous global crises were on the agenda. According to media reports, topics such as nuclear disarmament, the wars in Iran and Ukraine, China’s dominance in the rare-earth trade, and the United States’ lead in artificial intelligence chip manufacturing were discussed.

Sadly, issues such as global warming, development assistance and international cooperation aimed at restoring respect for international law were reportedly absent from the agenda.

If the trilateral talks were to become a regular fixture, they would have the potential to usher in a new balance of power. But this depends on the sense and sensibility of the three leaders. A leader with sense makes sound decisions based on facts. A leader with sensibility understands the human consequences of those decisions. Someone who possesses both demonstrates wisdom tempered by compassion.

Yet international relations are fundamentally a struggle for power. There is little room for compassion and empathy when countries make decisions solely to advance their respective national interests, which are more often than not in conflict. Prudence is often advocated in such situations, but whether nations pursuing power act with such wisdom remains uncertain. While sense and sensibility can be discerned in the actions of Chinese leader Xi Jinping and, to a lesser extent, Russian leader Vladimir Putin, the actions of the United States under Donald Trump are widely perceived as lacking both. He launched the war against Iran based on the self-serving advice of Israel’s hawkish Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, disregarding ground realities and the global repercussions. Trump appears to show little remorse for the suffering the world is undergoing, particularly in poor developing countries, some of which he once derided as “shithole countries” in a display of characteristic billionaire arrogance.

However, he could display no such arrogance at yesterday’s meeting with Xi and Putin in the Chinese city of Shenzhen. If the three leaders were to meet annually under a G3 framework, as G7 leaders do, it could become a valuable platform for addressing mutual concerns and fears, provided it does not evolve into a forum for deciding the fate of humanity, as the elusive or illusory Illuminati are sometimes said to do. Such concerns have already been voiced by the beleaguered Ukrainian leader, Volodymyr Zelensky. Adding to his concerns, Trump greeted Putin, Zelensky’s bête noire, with a handshake, saying, “He is my friend.” The chemistry among Putin, Trump and Xi appeared to be at its strongest during the Shenzhen meeting.

As the trilateral talks commenced yesterday, Xi described them as being of “groundbreaking significance”, while Trump said the leaders were “really making history”. Meanwhile, Putin stated that Russia, China and the United States share a “joint responsibility” for “maintaining international peace and security”.

Comparisons are being drawn between the Shenzhen meeting of the leaders of the world’s three leading powers and the 1945 Yalta Conference, where the three principal World War II allies, the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union, sought to shape the emerging post-war world order. Unlike Yalta, which brought together wartime allies united by a common enemy, the Shenzhen discussions involved ostensibly rival nations with competing visions of the world and no shared adversary, according to CNN analyst Lex Harvey. Harvey quoted David Shullman, vice president at the Asia Society Policy Institute, as saying that Beijing will seek to avoid parallels with Yalta.

“From a Chinese perspective, they do not actually want the perception or the optics of a Yalta 2.0,” Shullman told CNN.

It is interesting to note that while the United States, a long-time advocate of economic liberalism, is moving towards protectionism through the imposition of steep tariffs on both rich and poor countries, its socialist counterpart, China, is calling for greater openness in global trade. Of course, China stands to benefit from such a policy.

According to the Chinese newspaper Global Times, China has put forward four proposals at the APEC summit for building an Asia-Pacific community and fostering shared prosperity in the region: upholding peace and stability, building bridges through openness and cooperation, promoting innovation-driven growth, and expanding opportunities for mutual benefit and win-win outcomes.

Although Sri Lanka is not a member of the APEC forum, the decisions taken by the grouping have a bearing on the country, as it falls within the Asia-Pacific region both geographically and geopolitically. The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad) meeting that Trump attended in Manila during the US-ASEAN Summit, together with the leaders of Australia, India and Japan, may signal that Washington, after a period of apparent indifference to the Quad, is once again seeking to counter China’s growing political influence in the Asia-Pacific region.

While the three major powers, which together possess 11,082 nuclear warheads, almost 91 per cent of the global nuclear stockpile, held trilateral talks that lasted for 90 minutes following bilateral meetings yesterday, elsewhere all is not well on the planet we call home. This week has been particularly troubling for those who long for peace. Their hopes are being shattered by, on the one hand, the escalation of the war with Iran, marked by US attacks on Iran’s Qeshm Island, Iran’s retaliatory strikes on Kuwait and Bahrain, several oil tankers being damaged by mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and the intensification of the conflict in Yemen, where the Ansarallah movement has reportedly made further gains against Saudi-backed forces.

On the other hand, serious cybersecurity concerns have arisen in the United States over technology outages affecting the aviation sector, banks, email systems, software services and internal communications at the National Security Agency, coming on the heels of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s sex scandal allegedly linked to Chinese espionage.

Adding to these concerns were claims in the US media that Iran was preparing to attack the US capital with a crude nuclear bomb. Iran has denied the allegation, while Iranian analysts have dismissed the report as a subterfuge or part of a false-flag operation devised by US and Israeli intelligence agencies to rally support for the war with Iran after the US electorate delivered a strong anti-war message in the midterm elections. Trump now claims that Iran is planning to use biological weapons against Americans.

Peace initiatives by Qatar and Pakistan are making more headlines than headway, with Trump toeing Israel’s hawkish line instead of respecting the anti-war verdict of the American people. Paradoxically, the Iran war has become a “forever war”, a term Trump once used to denounce the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan while presenting himself as an anti-war president. His hypocrisy has been laid bare, yet he appears to believe that serving Israel is a greater cause than serving Americans, who are now paying more for fuel and groceries while the war has already swallowed US$47 billion in taxpayers’ money.

The Ukraine war continues, with neither Moscow nor Kyiv willing to compromise. Elsewhere, conflicts in Sudan, Ethiopia and other regions continue as though they are of little concern to the international community.

One cannot be overly optimistic that the Shenzhen summit communiqué will resonate with the hopes of a peace-starved world and pave the way for peace in West Asia, Ukraine, Africa and elsewhere, thereby ushering in a new world order that brings prosperity to all nations.
