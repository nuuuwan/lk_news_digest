Mounting global curbs on citizenship

The receiving countries, which are mostly of the First World, ignore the fact that they themselves could be the cause of the distress migration

The increasing movement of people across countries and continents due to a variety of push and pull factors is now being curbed the world over. The reasons are -- mounting concerns over security, and fears of demographic and economic changes threatening existing power structures.

Affected countries and people do protest loudly, mostly on grounds of human rights sanctified by international conventions. But these protests have had little or no effect on governments that are  increasingly pledged to safeguarding their national or domestic political interests.

People migrate legally or illegally in search of opportunities, to escape from wars or instabilities created by political unrest, the effects of climate change and scarcities created by an unbridled increase in populations. People take advantage of improvements and increase in the modes of transport. But all these are negated by firewalls put up by receiving countries.

Old liberal immigration and citizenship laws have been tightened and made more restrictive. Efforts to plug loopholes are on. Countries also dictate what kind of immigrants they will welcome and what kind they will bar. Receiving countries work out deals with the sending countries to tackle the problem at its source.

The receiving countries, which are mostly of the First World, ignore the fact that they themselves could be the cause of the distress migration. It is they which had ruined their colonies by exploitation over centuries thus indirectly causing migration. Migration from West Asia,  on the rise now, can be attributed to the wars unleashed by imperialist powers moved by self-aggrandisement.

The latest issue being tackled in the West is “birthright citizenship” through “birth tourism.” Western countries are increasingly shifting away from granting automatic citizenship based solely on birth within their territories, to stricter conditions that tie citizenship to factors like parental residency, legal status, and long-term integration. As a result, millions of immigrants and travellers are facing new barriers that could reshape the global landscape of immigration and international mobility.

In the United States, birthright citizenship has been a contentious issue for years. Under the 14th Amendment of the US Constitution, anyone born on US soil is automatically granted U.S. citizenship, regardless of their parents’ immigration status. However, the practice of “jus soli” has faced increasing scrutiny in recent years, especially with the rise of anti-immigration sentiment and concerns over “birth  tourism.”

In 2025, the US President issued an Executive Order seeking to limit birthright citizenship by requiring that at least one parent be a US citizen or legal permanent resident for a child born in the US to receive citizenship. This executive order, which has been met with legal challenges, could fundamentally alter the future of US immigration law.

Switzerland is now further tightening its birthright citizenship laws. Historically, Switzerland followed the principle of “jus sanguinis” (right of blood), which granted Swiss nationality to children born abroad to Swiss parents. However, Switzerland did not have an unrestricted “jus soli” (right of soil) system—meaning birth within Swiss territory did not automatically grant citizenship.

In 2026, Switzerland made headlines by joining several other European nations in tightening its stance on birthright citizenship. Children born in Switzerland to foreign nationals can only claim citizenship through naturalisation if they meet long-term residency requirements, further reinforcing the country’s commitment to residence-based citizenship.

Until 2004, Ireland granted automatic citizenship to anyone born within its borders, but the introduction of the Nationality and Citizenship Act in that year limited this entitlement. The reform stipulates that children born in Ireland can only acquire citizenship if at least one parent is an Irish citizen or has been legally residing in the country for a specific period.

The changes in Ireland’s birthright citizenship policy reflect broader concerns about immigration and the potential for “birth tourism”—the practice of foreign nationals traveling to Ireland to give birth, thereby granting their children Irish citizenship. This policy change has had a significant impact on the number of children born in Ireland who automatically receive citizenship, leading to debates about fairness and equality in immigration laws.

Historically, Germany had a mix of “jus soli” and “jus sanguinis”, where children born in Germany could acquire citizenship through birth if at least one parent had been living in Germany for a specified period. However, in 2000, the government passed legislation that tightened this provision and emphasised the importance of parental legal status in the country.

In 2026, Germany introduced further reforms that make it harder for children born to foreign nationals to acquire German citizenship. Children born in Germany to foreign parents must now prove a significant connection to the country through factors such as residency and education.

This regulation is part of Germany’s broader effort to control immigration and prevent the creation of multiple generations of citizens without direct ties to the country.

Before 2006, Italy granted citizenship to any child born on Italian soil, but reforms introduced in 2006 placed limits on this automatic citizenship. Now, children born in Italy to foreign nationals are required to meet certain residency and educational criteria before they can apply for citizenship. These reforms have been aimed at reducing the number of immigrants obtaining citizenship through birth, and ensuring that the child has a strong connection to the country before being granted citizenship.

As with other countries, the Italian government is focused on ensuring that citizenship is tied to integration and residency, rather than simply place of birth.

Australia’s shift toward stricter birthright citizenship laws began in 1986 when the country eliminated unconditional birthright citizenship. Now, a child born in Australia can only acquire citizenship at birth if at least one parent is an Australian citizen or holds permanent residency. This change is part of Australia’s broader immigration policy, which prioritises skilled workers and long-term residents over casual visitors or migrants.

In 2026, Australia’s government reinforced these rules, tightening the criteria for birthright citizenship. Children born to temporary visa holders or tourists in Australia are no longer automatically granted citizenship. This is consistent with Australia’s long-standing policy of restricting citizenship to those with strong ties to the country, and it reflects the nation’s ongoing commitment to managing immigration.

France, one of Europe’s most popular destinations for immigration, has a mixed approach to birthright citizenship. While France has long been a proponent of “jus soli”, where children born in the country could claim citizenship, recent reforms have introduced more conditions. Children born in France to foreign parents must now meet specific residency criteria and, in some cases, prove their long-term integration into French society before they can apply for citizenship.

These reforms, which began in the early 2000s and were further tightened in 2026, have been part of France’s effort to control immigration and prevent the creation of a large population of citizens with little connection to the country.

As these nations increasingly impose stricter rules on birthright citizenship, the implications for immigrants, particularly those from low-income and undocumented backgrounds, are profound. These changes are likely to affect millions of people seeking to provide their children with better opportunities.

India does not allow immigration or give citizenship easily. There is a residence qualification for citizenship. There are barriers to citizenship for Muslims from Pakistan, Bangladesh and Afghanistan while non-Muslims from these countries are admitted more easily.  Sri Lankans have problems getting citizenship even though many Tamil refugees have been demanding it citing decades of residence in India.

Marriage to an Indian citizen does not automatically make a foreign national an Indian citizen. Under Indian law, marriage only creates eligibility, not citizenship itself, meaning that even after a valid and legally registered marriage, a foreign spouse does not automatically acquire Indian nationality, does not receive an Indian passport, and does not gain any immediate citizenship rights. Instead, the foreign spouse must strictly follow the Indian citizenship framework, which requires lawful and continuous residence in India for a prescribed period, compliance with visa and immigration rules, police and security verification, and formal approval by the Government of India. The final outcome depends on government discretion, not on marriage alone. India does not have birthright citizenship.
