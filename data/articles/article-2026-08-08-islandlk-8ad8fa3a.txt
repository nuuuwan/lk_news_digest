The Tragedy of Vision: Sri Lanka’s Hidden Eye Health Crisis

“The greatest tragedy is not that blindness exists. The greatest tragedy is that so much of it could have been prevented.”

Every morning, millions of Sri Lankans wake up and open their eyes without giving a second thought to the extraordinary gift of sight. We see the faces of those we love, read the morning news, travel to work, care for our families, and move through our daily lives with confidence. Vision is so deeply woven into everyday existence that we rarely appreciate its value—until it begins to disappear.

For more than two and half decades, I have had the privilege of working in eye care across Sri Lanka. During that time, I have met thousands of people whose stories have shaped my understanding of blindness—not merely as a medical condition, but as a profound human tragedy.

I have watched elderly men and women gradually withdraw from society because cataracts deprived them of their independence. I have met schoolchildren whose poor academic performance was simply the result of an undiagnosed refractive error. I have seen individuals with diabetes lose their vision because retinal disease was detected too late. I have also witnessed families fall deeper into poverty when a breadwinner became visually impaired from a condition that modern medicine could have treated.

Most of these observations were not made during my official work at the hospital or office, almost always during volunteer visits to the community. I had the opportunity and privilege of travelling across the country to every corner, every district, hundreds of villages, seeing thousands of people at their locations. I have clearly witnessed their agony of not being able to see, I have shared their sorrow and frustration.

It begins with blurred vision while reading. Difficulty recognizing faces across the street. Trouble driving at night. A child who cannot see the classroom board. A grandmother who stops sewing because the thread has become impossible to see. A person living with diabetes who notices a dark shadow in one eye but assumes it will improve on its own.

By the time many seek help, precious vision has already been lost.

Blindness is often imagined as complete darkness. In reality, it is far more complex. It is the gradual loss of independence. It is a father who can no longer earn a living because he cannot see clearly. It is an elderly woman who becomes afraid to leave her home after repeated falls. It is a student whose educational potential is diminished because a simple pair of spectacles was never prescribed. It is a family that slips deeper into poverty because one member has become visually impaired from a condition that could have been treated.

The true cost of blindness cannot be measured only in medical terms. It must also be measured in lost opportunities, lost productivity, reduced quality of life, emotional suffering, and the burden carried by families and caregivers.

Sri Lanka has long been recognised for its remarkable achievements in public health. Universal healthcare, high literacy, and strong maternal and child health programmes are rightly celebrated. Yet eye health rarely receives the same level of national attention. It remains one of the country’s least recognised public health challenges, despite affecting people of every age and every community.

The encouraging truth is that much of this suffering is avoidable.

Modern medicine has transformed eye care. Cataracts can often be treated successfully with a relatively straightforward operation. Most refractive errors can be corrected with appropriate spectacles. Diabetic eye disease can frequently be slowed or prevented through regular screening and timely treatment. Glaucoma, although irreversible, can often be controlled if detected early enough. Childhood eye conditions can frequently be managed before they permanently affect development.

The challenge is not simply whether treatments exist. The challenge is ensuring that people receive them at the right time.

Many citizens still arrive at eye clinics only after significant vision has already been lost. Some postpone care because they believe deteriorating eyesight is a normal part of ageing. Others struggle with travel, financial pressures, long waiting periods, or limited awareness of early symptoms. Too often, preventable visual impairment becomes permanent simply because intervention came too late.

Sri Lanka is also entering a new demographic era. Our population is ageing rapidly. At the same time, diabetes and other chronic diseases are increasing. These trends mean that the number of people living with cataract, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, and low vision is likely to rise in the years ahead. Without stronger preventive services and better access to timely care, the burden on individuals, families, and the health system will continue to grow.

Eye health should not be viewed as the responsibility of ophthalmologists and optometrists alone. It is a national development issue. Good vision supports education, employment, road safety, healthy ageing, economic productivity, and social participation. Protecting sight contributes directly to national well-being.

Another uncomfortable reality deserves honest reflection. Sri Lanka is fortunate to have many highly skilled and deeply committed ophthalmologists, optometrists, and allied eye care professionals who dedicate their lives to restoring sight. However, like many health systems around the world, ours is not immune to the pressures of commercialization and professional incentives.

Increasingly, success is measured by busy private clinics, sophisticated technology, and procedural volumes, while the silent burden of avoidable blindness in remote villages, underserved urban communities, and among the poorest citizens remains largely unseen. The greatest need often lies far from consulting rooms—in communities where people have never had an eye examination, where cataracts remain untreated for years, and where children continue to struggle because no one has tested their vision. Eye care must never become solely a profession of treating those who can reach us or afford us; it must also be a commitment to seeking those who are left behind. The true measure of our profession is not only the excellence of the care we provide within our clinics, but also our willingness to reach those who would otherwise remain invisible.

We must therefore move beyond treating blindness after it occurs. Our focus should shift toward preventing avoidable vision loss through stronger primary eye care, regular vision screening, public education, improved referral systems, school eye health programmes, diabetic retinal screening, and equitable access to quality services across the country.

Every Sri Lankan deserves the opportunity to enjoy a lifetime of good vision, regardless of age, income, or where they live.

The measure of a compassionate society is not only how it treats illness, but how effectively it prevents unnecessary suffering. Preventable blindness is one of the clearest examples of suffering that we have the knowledge and the means to reduce.

If we value healthy ageing, we must protect the eyesight of older adults.

If we value productivity, we must preserve the vision of our workforce.

And if we value human dignity, then protecting sight must become a national priority.

Because when a person loses vision unnecessarily, it is not only an individual tragedy. It is a loss shared by families, communities, and the nation itself.

As someone who has devoted more than two and a half decades to community eye health, I make this heartfelt appeal to His Excellency the President, the Government of Sri Lanka, and the Ministry of Health: let us not wait until avoidable blindness becomes an even greater national burden. The time to act is now. Sri Lanka has the knowledge, the expertise, and the dedicated professionals needed to transform eye care—we need only the national commitment to bring them together under a strong, well-resourced National Eye Care Programme. By revitalizing eye health as a national priority, strengthening leadership within the Ministry of Health, investing in primary eye care, expanding community screening, ensuring timely referral and treatment, and implementing evidence-based strategic interventions, we can dramatically reduce avoidable blindness and visual impairment in the years ahead. The vision of a nation is measured not only by what it sees today, but by how wisely it protects the sight of future generations. Let history remember that this was the moment when Sri Lanka chose to see, chose to act, and chose to ensure that no citizen lost sight simply because help came too late.

This article is adapted from the forthcoming book The Tragedy of Vision: The Hidden Eye Health Crisis in Sri Lanka, which explores the human stories, scientific evidence, and policy solutions needed to reduce avoidable blindness and visual impairment across the country.
