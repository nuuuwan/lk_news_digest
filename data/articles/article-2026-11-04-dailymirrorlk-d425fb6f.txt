Sri Lanka needs to save its garment industries

Sri Lanka has spent decades building a garment industry that is now one of its biggest economic strengths. It has earned the confidence of leading clothing brands, created hundreds of thousands of jobs and brought in billions in foreign exchange. Yet manufacturers are finding it increasingly difficult to operate here.

High operating costs, taxes, electricity bills and labour shortages are making Sri Lanka a less attractive manufacturing destination. Neighbouring countries are competing for investment. Sri Lanka risks losing industries it spent years building. Why should an investor stay when another country offers lower costs, a larger workforce and a more predictable business environment?

Apparel remains Sri Lanka’s largest export industry, generating around US$5 billion annually and providing employment to hundreds of thousands. Behind these figures are families whose livelihoods depend on factories remaining open. Losing this industry would mean losing jobs, foreign exchange, buyers, expertise and decades of investment.

Recent developments should concern the Government. NEXT Manufacturing closed its Katunayake factory in 2025, affecting around 1,400 employees, citing high operating costs. In 2026, MAS Holdings announced production at its Methliya facility in Thulhiriya would cease, affecting approximately 2,200 employees. Production would move elsewhere and the site be repurposed for fabric manufacturing. This was a restructuring, not a complete withdrawal. Nevertheless, it raises questions about Sri Lanka’s ability to retain production.

Sri Lanka cannot assume investors will remain loyal because they have operated here for decades. Business decisions are driven by costs, efficiency and profitability. Bangladesh, Vietnam, India and Cambodia compete for orders with competitive costs, skilled workers and incentives. If Sri Lanka becomes too expensive, manufacturers and buyers have alternatives.

Labour shortages are among the industry’s biggest problems. Factories cannot operate at full capacity without enough workers. But migration, overseas employment, rising living costs and changing career expectations have made recruitment more difficult.

The answer is not simply to ask more people to join production lines. Workers need reasonable wages, safe workplaces, transport and opportunities to advance. The industry must challenge the perception that factory work offers few prospects. Training and promotion can make these jobs more attractive.

Employers cannot absorb every increase in costs without losing competitiveness. Protecting workers and keeping factories viable must go hand in hand. Taxation is another concern. Sri Lanka’s standard corporate income tax rate is 30 per cent, while businesses face other levies and operating expenses. Although direct exports are zero-rated for VAT, the wider cost of doing business and imported inputs put pressure on manufacturers. Electricity and fuel costs matter too. Garment factories run machinery for long hours, and higher energy bills quickly erode margins. Manufacturers need predictable pricing and access to renewable energy. Sudden changes make it harder to plan orders and compete.

The Government should consider targeted incentives for genuine export manufacturers. These could include tax relief linked to retaining and creating jobs, investing in machinery, training workers and increasing exports. Support must have clear conditions and measurable results.

Sri Lanka must improve productivity. Technical training centres should work with manufacturers to teach modern production methods, quality control and technology. Automation should improve efficiency and create higher-skilled roles, not simply replace people. Global demand is changing. Buyers expect competitive prices, quality, reliable delivery and compliance with labour and environmental standards. Sri Lanka should build on its reputation for ethical manufacturing rather than compete only on cheap labour. Access to markets, including through the European Union’s GSP+ facility, is valuable.

Transport operators, food suppliers, maintenance services, small businesses and entire communities depend on the industry. Sri Lanka cannot take this industry for granted when foreign exchange and employment remain essential to economic recovery. Its success was built through years of hard work and trust. That trust must be protected.

The Government must stop treating apparel as a success that will look after itself. It must listen to manufacturers, workers and exporters, identify why production is moving and act. The objective is not to enrich companies at workers’ expense. It is to keep businesses profitable, workers earning a decent living and export earnings in the country.

Sri Lanka cannot keep talking about creating jobs while allowing the factories that provide them to look elsewhere.
