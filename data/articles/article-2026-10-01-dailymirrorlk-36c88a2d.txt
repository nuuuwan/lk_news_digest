Children’s rights: My day, my rights, my future

Children’s rights are not rewards for being well-behaved or successful. They are fundamental human rights

Listening does not mean allowing children to make every decision. It means respecting their views according to their age and maturity,  and ensuring those views are considered in decisions affecting their lives

Every child has the right to grow up healthy, safe, loved and respected. Children have the right to learn, play, dream and express their views. Yet,  millions continue to face poverty, conflict, violence, displacement, malnutrition and unequal access to education and healthcare.

This year, Universal Children’s Day observed in Sri Lanka on October 1 carries the message “My Day. My Rights. My Future.” The theme calls on children to speak about their lives, rights and aspirations, while urging adults and leaders to listen and act.

Universal Children’s Day commemorates important milestones in the history of children’s rights. On November 20, 1959, the United Nations adopted the Declaration of the Rights of the Child. Thirty years later, on November 20, 1989, it adopted the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC).

The CRC recognises childhood as a special period of life and establishes rights including survival and development, health, education, protection from violence and exploitation, and participation in decisions affecting children.

Sri Lanka signed the Convention in 1990 and ratified it in 1991, committing to uphold and protect children’s rights. While Sri Lanka celebrates Children’s Day on October 1, November 20 is observed internationally as Universal Children’s Day.

Children’s rights are not rewards for being well-behaved or successful. They are fundamental human rights.

Every child has the right to adequate nutrition and healthcare, regardless of family income. Every child has the right to education and protection from physical, emotional and sexual abuse. Children also have the right to be heard.

The CRC is guided by four key principles: non-discrimination; the best interests of the child; the right to life, survival and development; and respect for the views of the child.

These principles should guide parents, teachers, healthcare professionals, communities and governments when making decisions that affect children.

Challenges facing childrenThe world has made significant progress in child survival, vaccination, education and access to basic services. However, poverty, conflict, climate-related disasters and inequality continue to threaten these gains.

UNICEF identifies child poverty as a major threat to children’s rights. Poverty can mean inadequate nutrition, poor housing, limited healthcare, unsafe water and sanitation, and fewer educational opportunities. UNICEF estimates that 412 million children worldwide live in extreme monetary poverty.

Climate-related disasters also place children at particular risk. Floods, droughts and heat waves can disrupt education, damage health services, cause displacement and increase the risks of infectious diseases and malnutrition. Armed conflicts similarly expose children to devastating consequences despite their having no responsibility for the conflicts themselves.

Sri Lanka has made considerable progress in child health and education. Childhood immunisation is widely available, under-five mortality has declined and primary school attendance is high.

However, challenges remain. Some children continue to experience poverty and unequal access to essential services, while vulnerable communities face difficulties related to nutrition, healthcare, sanitation and education. Child abuse, neglect and exploitation also require continued vigilance.

Nutrition is another major concern. Sri Lanka faces a triple burden of malnutrition, with undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies existing alongside increasing overweight and obesity. A 2022 assessment found that around one in four children aged 5–17 were underweight, while overweight and obesity also affected a significant proportion of schoolchildren and adolescents.

Perhaps the most important word in this year’s theme is “My.” Children should not simply be spoken about; they should be listened to. They may have concerns about bullying, safety, examinations, friendships, social media, climate change or their future.

Listening does not mean allowing children to make every decision. It means respecting their views according to their age and maturity,  and ensuring those views are considered in decisions affecting their lives.

Universal Children’s Day should go beyond celebrations and competitions. It should be an opportunity for meaningful action.

Parents can create homes where children feel safe to speak without fear. Teachers can make schools safe and inclusive. Healthcare professionals can advocate for children’s health, development and protection. Communities can support vulnerable children and families. The media can give children a voice while protecting their privacy and dignity.

Decision-makers, meanwhile, have a responsibility to ensure that policies, programmes and budgets place children’s best interests at the centre.

Children are not merely the adults of tomorrow. They are citizens and rights-holders today.

On Universal Children’s Day 2026, let us move beyond speaking about children and start listening to them.

Let every child have the opportunity to learn, play and feel safe. Let every child know their rights and have those rights respected. And let every child imagine a future—and receive the support needed to achieve it.

My Day. My Rights. My Future. The message is simple. The responsibility belongs to all of us.

(Dr. Maheshaka Wijayawardena is  Consultant Paediatrician at the Base Hospital,  Marawila)
