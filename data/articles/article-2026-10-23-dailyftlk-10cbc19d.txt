Monkish Black Magic

For a monk, who has taken vows to take refuge exclusively in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Sangha, to lead public supplications and occult offerings to a demon is a total renunciation of the primary tenets of Buddhism. It is an explicit admission that this brand of political Buddhism has completely emptied itself of the Dhamma, replacing the quest for Nirvana with the invocation of dark forces to curse political adversaries

destruction of any such state. By not so willing anything in the world, he grasps after nothing; by not grasping, he is not anxious; he is therefore fully calmed within

The ‘comic opera’ in  Colombo’s green heart, “Vihara Mahadevi Park” is a stark pointer to the bizarre and dangerous cultural crossroads at which we have arrived.

Ven. Kotapola Rahula Thero, a senior prelate of the Kotte Sangha Sabha, was an early participant. He initially described this saffron shindig as a righteous Satyagraha for the “protection of the robe.”

As I write this missive, it has degenerated into a sacrilegious theatre—a ‘Saffron Circus’ orchestrated by a cabal of politicised priests and defeated opposition factions.

Choreographed by political manipulators, the often-rowdy lay participants are visibly emboldened by the unconcealed patronage of elements within established ecclesiastical bodies in Colombo. Meanwhile, the Kotte Sri Kalyani Samagri Dharma Maha Sangha Sabha is virtually walking on eggshells.

This exercise represents something far more sinister than a mere political protest. It is the systematic weaponisation of blind piety, as attested to by the shocking performance of dark occult rituals like the Mahason Pideniya—an offering to the demon of death and disease. This ritual was led by infamous figures like Samantha Bhadra (formerly known as Pitiduwe Siridhamma), an absolute apostate wrapped in monastic robes.

How has Buddhism—specifically Ashokan Buddhism, a philosophy anchored in radical non-violence, mindfulness, and the eradication of the ego—been corrupted into a vehicle for political survival and public cursing? To find answers to this riddle, one must revisit the foundational texts of Sri Lankan political anthropology. The crisis we witness today is not an overnight aberration; it is the logical conclusion of historical processes that have systematically exchanged the ethical core of the Dhamma for the preservation of temporal power.

The distinguished anthropologist H.L. Seneviratne, in his seminal work The Work of Kings, meticulously demonstrates that the modern ‘theory of the Buddhist State’ in Sri Lanka is a relatively recent ideological construction. Seneviratne argues that this theory was formally articulated and institutionalised by nationalist scholars and monks only after the pivotal General Election of 1956.

Prior to 1956, the Buddhist revivalist movement focused largely on cultural reclamation and education in the face of colonial marginalisation. However, the post-1956 political landscape fundamentally altered the monastic vocation. Ideological entrepreneurs engineered a modern narrative that fused the state, the ethnic identity of the majority, and the preservation of the Sasana into a single, indivisible unit.

Monks were no longer merely seekers of internal purification or community educators; they were transformed into the structural guardians of a majoritarian, unitary state polity. By establishing this framework, post-colonial political actors on both sides of the aisle created a monster. They invited the monkhood into the arena of partisan politics, setting a dangerous precedent where the robe could be traded for policy leverage, state patronage, and eventually, immunity from the law.

We seem to practice two kinds of Buddhism: the sublime compassion of Prince Siddhartha, and the capricious priestly vocation evident in the ongoing protest at the public park. This structural division brings us directly to the profound observations of another towering scholar, S.J. Tambiah.

In his classic and controversial text Buddhism Betrayed?, Tambiah wrote extensively about what can be conceptualised as ‘Two Buddhisms’ coexisting within the Sri Lankan consciousness.

The first is the Buddhism of the texts—a path of sublime compassion. This is the ethically elevated philosophy that preaches universal loving-kindness (Metta), psychological liberation, inner peace, and the transcendence of worldly attachments. This is the Buddhism that inspired the moral imagination of millions and produced centuries of artistic and philosophical genius.

The second, however, is a Buddhism driven by a capricious priestly vocation. This version represents the dark underside of the institution: a caste-entrenched, politically hyperactive monastic hierarchy that manipulates religious sentiment for temporal security.

When Buddhism is reduced from a path of spiritual awakening to a protective guild for the clerical class, it sheds its ethical imperatives. Tambiah warned that when this capricious, defensive majoritarian complex takes over, the Sasana becomes an instrument of exclusion, division, and aggression. The ‘Saffron Circus’ at Viharamahadevi Park is the ultimate manifestation of this dark underside, where spiritual duties are abandoned in favour of political grandstanding.

The strange alignment of varied actors at Viharamahadevi Park makes complete sense when viewed through the lens of recent electoral shifts, particularly the National People’s Power (NPP) gaining a two-thirds majority under President Anura Kumara Dissanayake. Following these seismic democratic shifts and subsequent anti-corruption crackdowns, the old political guard has found itself utterly displaced. The political architecture of patronage, nepotism, and systemic corruption that governed Sri Lanka for decades has been soundly rejected by an electorate demanding fundamental structural change.

If Sri Lanka is to emerge from this chronic crisis of faith and governance, we—the Buddhist laity—must learn to decouple the sublime ethical framework of the historical Buddha from the self-serving acrobatics of the ‘Saffron Circus.’ . It is time to reject the apostates who desecrate the robe for political theatre and demand a return to a Buddhism defined not by majoritarian supremacy and occult curses, but by wisdom, intellectual rigour, and absolute compassion

Faced with this stark structural irrelevance and impending legal accountability for past follies, defeated opposition groups have resorted to the ultimate strategy for political survival: engineering a crisis of faith. Deprived of a credible economic program or a visionary governance alternative, the fractured opposition has hitched its wagon to the oldest, most reliable populist anchor available. They are exploiting the inherent anxieties of traditionalists by manufacturing a false narrative that the current state apparatus is bent on destroying the Sasana.

This voodoo stratagem has the hallmarks of the marketing wizards behind the Sarva Jana Balaya, and it seems to have seduced elements of the Samagi Jana Balawegaya (SJB) as well. The strategy is transparent: hide behind the sacred robe to secure personal protection, evade judicial scrutiny, and derail progressive legislative or anti-corruption efforts. It is transactional politics at its absolute worst. Politicians provide funds, logistical support, and media amplification; in return, rogue monastics provide a religious human shield, transforming what should be a criminal or political accountability process into a false defence of the faith.

What makes this mobilisation particularly insidious is the collusion between discredited political forces and robed charlatans who weaponise the blind piety of ordinary lay devotees. Laymen, confusing the defence of an ordained hierarchy with the defence of the Buddha›s actual teachings, are cynically manipulated into joining these pseudo-religious agitations.

This institutional complicity should not be ignored. The silent or unconcealed support offered by influential chapters, such as the Kotte Samagri Sangha Sabha, provides these fringe elements with a veneer of institutional legitimacy. When established Nikayas fail to discipline monastics who openly engage in political subversion and heresy, they degrade their own spiritual authority, reducing the Sangha to a mere factional interest group.

Nowhere is this decay more vividly captured than in the figure of Samantha Bhadra (formerly Pitiduwe Siridhamma). Emerging originally as a charismatic preacher who claimed advanced spiritual realisations, his trajectory has devolved into a grotesque caricature of monasticism. His organisation of the Mahason Pideniya in a public park under the guise of political protest is an act of absolute spiritual apostasy.

Mahasona is a demon of the graveyard—a symbol of fear, destruction, and delusion. For a monk, who has taken vows to take refuge exclusively in the Buddha, Dhamma, and Sangha, to lead public supplications and occult offerings to a demon is a total renunciation of the primary tenets of Buddhism. It is an explicit admission that this brand of political Buddhism has completely emptied itself of the Dhamma, replacing the quest for Nirvana with the invocation of dark forces to curse political adversaries.

The tragedy of the Viharamahadevi Park spectacle is that it exploits a deeply held cultural identity to serve the narrow interests of an elite political class and its monastic allies.

When Buddhism is reduced to public black magic, political sit-ins, and the defence of individuals accused of secular crimes, it loses its universal moral authority.

If Sri Lanka is to emerge from this chronic crisis of faith and governance, we—the Buddhist laity—must learn to decouple the sublime ethical framework of the historical Buddha from the self-serving acrobatics of the ‘Saffron Circus.’

We must look back to the warnings of Seneviratne and Tambiah to recognise that the politicisation of religion has brought nothing but moral and social bankruptcy. It is time to reject the apostates who desecrate the robe for political theatre and demand a return to a Buddhism defined not by majoritarian supremacy and occult curses, but by wisdom, intellectual rigour, and absolute compassion.
