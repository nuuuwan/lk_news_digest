Sri Lanka has little room for tax hikes or cuts: IPS

Urges shift towards direct taxes; warns fiscal consolidation has squeezed productive public investment

Sri Lanka should resist both further tax rate increases and premature tax cuts in Budget 2027, and instead widen the tax net and strengthen compliance as the country enters a critical phase of fiscal consolidation, the Institute of Policy Studies of Sri Lanka (IPS) has recommended.

Setting out policy priorities for the upcoming Budget, the think tank said the next phase of revenue mobilisation should focus less on changing headline tax rates and more on addressing weaknesses in the tax base, exemptions, informality and compliance, particularly among high-income earners and the self-employed.

“Further tax rate increases are not recommended, but tax cuts are premature because of challenges such as uneven compliance, informality, and exemptions,” IPS said.

The recommendation places the tax burden at the centre of the Budget 2027 debate, as policymakers face the difficult task of preserving the fiscal gains achieved since the economic crisis while creating sufficient space for investment and growth.

Government revenue has risen sharply from 8.2 percent of GDP in 2022 to 16.6 percent in 2025, according to IPS. However, the tax structure remains heavily tilted towards consumption, with direct and indirect taxes accounting for a 40:60 share.

IPS argued that revenue policy should increasingly shift towards direct taxation by broadening the tax base, reducing exemptions, improving compliance among self-employed and high-income earners and accelerating the digitalisation of tax administration.

It also backed wealth taxation as part of a more equitable revenue framework, arguing that those with a greater ability to pay should contribute a larger share.

The recommendations come as Sri Lanka faces a tighter fiscal framework in 2027 following temporary easing this year.

Under the IMF-supported programme, the Government has committed to return to a primary surplus of 2.3 percent of GDP from 2027, following temporary fiscal easing in 2026 to accommodate post-cyclone reconstruction and the impact of external shocks. The primary expenditure ceiling is also expected to return to 13 percent of GDP.

The IMF has similarly emphasised broadening the tax base and improving compliance. Following a visit in June, IMF staff said Sri Lanka should continue efforts to improve tax compliance, broaden the tax base and strengthen public financial management.

The Government has committed under the programme to approve revenue measures supporting fiscal consolidation in 2027 and to develop a Medium-Term Revenue Strategy informed by a tax diagnostic. The IMF programme documentation says the strategy is intended to make taxation more efficient and equitable through broader tax bases, rationalised tax expenditures and improved administration and compliance.

IPS’ proposals, however, underline the need to balance that revenue requirement against the risk of further squeezing households and businesses through higher tax rates.

The institute pointed to the transformation in Sri Lanka’s revenue position since the crisis, while arguing that the composition and efficiency of taxation now require greater attention.

The fiscal adjustment has also come at a cost to productive government spending. IPS noted that public investment fell from around 5 percent to 3 percent of GDP during the adjustment period, even as high public debt and limited fiscal space continued to constrain policy choices.

This creates a difficult policy trade-off for Budget 2027: maintaining sufficient revenue to protect debt sustainability while ensuring fiscal consolidation does not continue to crowd out expenditure capable of lifting the economy’s productive capacity.

IPS said expenditure rationalisation should therefore move beyond simply controlling spending and focus on improving the Government’s capacity to select and execute investments.

It called for stronger technical capabilities across the entire public investment cycle, including project appraisal, readiness, implementation, monitoring and evaluation. Public procurement should also place greater emphasis on transparency and competence rather than established networks, it said.

The concern over investment execution is also reflected in the IMF’s assessment. The Fund has said Sri Lanka needs to resolve bottlenecks in spending execution and improve public investment management while maintaining fiscal discipline.

Sri Lanka substantially outperformed its fiscal targets last year, recording a primary surplus of 5.4 percent of GDP in 2025, against the programme target of 2.3 percent. However, the IMF has cautioned that part of the performance reflected elevated motor vehicle-related revenue and under-execution of capital expenditure, highlighting the importance of developing a broader and more durable revenue base.

For 2027, IMF projections envisage government revenue and grants at around 15.1 percent of GDP, a primary balance of 2.3 percent and central government debt at 93.5 percent of GDP. Real GDP growth is projected at 3.2 percent.

Against that backdrop, IPS’ prescription is effectively for Budget 2027 to shift the emphasis of fiscal consolidation from higher rates towards better collection and a broader, more progressive tax base, while protecting expenditure capable of supporting productivity and longer-term growth.

The institute said the broader fiscal strategy must continue to protect macroeconomic stability while confronting high public debt, constrained fiscal space and weak investment in productivity-enhancing sectors.
