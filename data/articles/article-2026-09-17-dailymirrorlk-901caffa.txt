Lethal Autonomous Weapons, an increasing threat to humans

Advocates on AI driven warfare point to human limitations to justify its expansion

On Tuesday (September 15), Saudi Arabia issued emergency alerts warning of a ‘potential danger’ in Jeddah, Mecca and Taif, urging people to seek shelter and follow official safety instructions. Even though the nature of the potential danger was initially not revealed, it was later reported that a Saudi-led coalition intercepted a drone launched by the Houthis headed towards the holy city of Mecca.

On the same day, an Italian fighter jet flying for NATO had brought down a suspected Russian drone in Lithuanian airspace. It is in such a backdrop that there are renewed calls by member states gathering at the United Nations Human Rights Council (UNHRC) sessions on Geneva to implement stricter rules and regulations on Lethal Autonomous Weapons.

Lethal Autonomous Weapons Systems (LAWS) often referred to as killer robots are military systems that use artificial intelligence to identify, select and engage targets without further human intervention. According to Human Rights Watch (HRW), the use of autonomous weapons will be the latest, most serious example of encroaching ‘digital de-humanisation’.

Advocates on AI driven warfare point to human limitations to justify its expansion. According to them, soldiers make errors in judgement, act on emotion, require rest and demand wages. But in the case of machines, they ‘improve’ every day at identifying threats based on behaviour and movement patterns. Some proponents suggest that the next step is to allow autonomous weapons to decide when to pull the trigger!

Human rights groups argue that people with disabilities are at particular risk as wheelchairs for instance could be mistaken for weapons. AI itself is flawed and there could be mistakes made when identifying facial features and biometric measurements. Rights groups have also raised concerns regarding the fact that machines cannot face trial or take legal responsibility for violating international humanitarian law. They argue that it is legally ambiguous to assign the blame to a commander, programme or manufacturer when a flawed algorithm is responsible for killing civilians.

The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine states that short-range drones have caused more casualties than any other weapon in Ukraine. While official figures on the number of casualties vary in different conflict areas, the UN recorded at least 395 civilian deaths and 2635 injuries caused by short-range drone attacks on Ukraine following the full scale invasion in February 2022 and April 2025. Drones operated by Haitian security forces and private contractors have killed at least 1243 people and 738 others according to HRW.

In early September, 128 states that are party to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) – a framework that regulates and bans weapons considered as indiscriminate or causing too many injuries, agreed on a document that could pave the way to international rules on autonomous weapons. However, there have been mixed reactions towards the definition provided for an autonomous weapon and measures to reduce their possible harmful effects on civilians.

Sri Lanka too has called for a clear negotiating mandate to establish a legally binding international instrument on Lethal Autonomous Weapon Systems (LAWS) at the forthcoming seventh review conference of the CCW. Sri Lanka’s Permanent Representative to the UN Sumith Dassanayake stressed that human judgement and control must remain essential throughout the entire life cycle of autonomous weapon systems. He said that life and death decisions in armed conflicts must never be delegated to machines without human interventions.

In the Asian context, countries such as Myanmar used integrated armed drones extensively in ongoing conflicts. Indonesia, the Philippines and Thailand deploy drones and UAVs for border security, counter-insurgency and maritime monitoring in contested zones such as the South China Sea.  Malaysia and Singapore have enhanced regional security by training recruits in drone operations, developing local interceptor drones and expanding defense partnerships. In Sri Lanka, drones are being used for coastal monitoring, tracking illegal activities at sea, search-and-rescue operations during natural disasters,  and it is currently being tested for agricultural purposes. However,  there is strict enforcement and nationwide monitoring on civilian drones to prevent security risks.

Boundaries and limitations need to be set between the right use of technology and protecting human rights,  but who would ultimately determine these boundaries remains a question.
