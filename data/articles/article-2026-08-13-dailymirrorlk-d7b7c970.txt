Can the NPP implement  the death  penalty  again?

Sri Lanka has not carried out any death row execution since 1976

The NPP’s move to re-introduce the death penalty -- going against the first policy declaration of the JVP drafted by its founder leader Rohana Wijeweera and some others in 1973 - would never materilise, given the threat it poses to the country’s economy

It took almost two weeks for the Catholic Church to express its stance on the death penalty imposed on former Defence Secretary Hemasiri Fernando and former Inspector General of Police (IGP) Pujith Jayasundara over the 2019 Easter Sunday terrorist attacks.

The Muslim terrorist group that carried out the series of suicide bombings on that fateful day targeted mainly Catholics who were praying in three churches and holidaying in three luxury hotels.

Was it the Daily Mirror article published on August 6, titled ‘Are the Easter Sunday victims happy with the death sentences?’, that prompted Cardinal Malcolm Ranjith to break the silence on the death penalty, 11 days after it was imposed on two senior officials?

While expressing full confidence in the independence of Sri Lanka’s judiciary to deliver justice, the Cardinal stated that the Catholic Church cannot accept or agree with the imposition of the death penalty, even for those responsible for the 2019 Easter Sunday bombings. Citing paragraph 2267 of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, he emphasised that capital punishment violates human dignity.

On the same day (Monday, August 10) the Cardinal released the statement informing the Catholic Church’s stance on capital punishment, interestingly,  the National People’s Power (NPP) government announced that it considers implementing the death penalty for drug-related offences as part of a tougher response to the growing threat posed by narcotics.

This was revealed at a meeting of the “Ratama Ekata” National Operations Council chaired by President Anura Kumara Dissanayake and attended by religious leaders, among others, at the Presidential Secretariat. Sri Lankan courts still impose the death sentence for murders and drug-related offenses, although it has been under a moratorium since 1976.

The question of reimplementing the death penalty in Sri Lanka comes to the fore occasionally when a brutal murder takes place, such as the rape and killing of five-year-old Seya Sadevmi of Kotadeniyawa,  and 18-year-old schoolgirl Sivaloganathan Vithiya of Pungudutivu, both in 2015. However, every time the discussion has been suppressed by the opposition,  by those who claim to be concerned about human rights,  and those who  really see the practical problems in reimplementing it.

The last judicial execution in Sri Lanka was carried out on June 23, 1976, at Welikada Prison, when convicted criminal Embilipitiye Chandradasa, also known as “Honda Papuwa”, was hanged. Consequently, on 22 May 1977, the fifth anniversary of the Republic, President William Gopallawa commuted the sentences of everyone on death row: 144 men and 3 women. Thereafter, all executive Presidents too,  commuted every sentence of death.

Veteran legal expert Dr. Nihal Jayawickrama who served then as the Permanent Secretary to the Ministry of Justice argues that there are no prisoners on death row now according to a procedure prescribed in a justice ministry standing order introduced when Mr. Felix Dias Bandaranaike was Minister of Justice. “If either the trial Judge or the Attorney General had recommended that the sentence should not be carried out, the Minister advised that the sentence be commuted to one of life imprisonment,” he points out,  and contends that a prisoner serving a life sentence cannot now be hanged. This is what has hitherto happened.

However, President Maithripala Sirisena in 2018 wanted to implement the death sentence handed to drug related offenders, citing the anti-drug campaign launched by Philippine’s President Rodrigo Duterte. Following a state visit to the Philippines in January, 2019, at the invitation of Philippine President Duterte, President Sirisena in June 2019, signed the death warrants for four convicted drug offenders, inviting a huge backlash from the legal community,  as well as  local and international human rights organisations. Finally, he backed down.

Nevertheless, people’s opposition to death penalty is in a way strange, as they in most cases either support elimination not only their sworn enemies but sometimes those who hold differing opinions,  or keep silent when such eliminations occur. And especially in politics, almost all political parties in Sri Lanka have been involved in extrajudicial killings or ironically defended such crimes while opposing official death sentences.

Over 10,000 young men and women, mostly innocent people,  were killed by the armed forces and the police during the first insurrection of the JVP in 1971, while the JVP on its part also took away the right of life of  thousands of those seen by them as government informants. The number of extra judicial killings in the south by the armed forces and  vigilante groups - PRRA and Green Tigers - during the second insurrection of the JVP was placed at more than 60.000. One wonders if the  JVP could  have had such a large number of armed fighters.

The mass graves found in various places in the island are undisputable evidence of extrajudicial killings during the two southern rebellions and the three-decade-long separatist war, irrespective of who the culprits were. Skeletal remains of over 500 persons including infants have been found during the ongoing excavations, so far, at the site of Chemmani mass graves. It is doubtful if we can find a single person who rejects all these killings, irrespective of the ethnicity or the political affiliation of the victims.

The best case in point for the hypocrisy of the international community that rejects any kind of deprivation of right to life is the ongoing genocide in Gaza.

On the other hand, even if we accept  capital punishment as a deterrent to certain crimes, the inevitable question that challenges the implementation of it is whether we have  an untainted law enforcement and justice system. The media occasionally carries out allegations of introducing drugs into vehicles, homes, or personal properties of innocent people by corrupt police officers. It must be remembered that the police had extracted a “confession” of rape and murder of Seya Sadewmi from a person whom they nicknamed “Kondaya,” before they arrested the real murderer.

Hence, the danger in carrying out the death sentence, even in the event of its acceptability being established, is the possibility of wrong people being sent to the gallows on planted or manufactured evidence. In a casual search in free Wikipedia, several pages were found that have catalogued a large number of such miscarriages of justice around the world in which some unfortunate men had been exonerated decades after they were executed or incarcerated.

The NPP’s move to re-introduce the death penalty -- going against the first policy declaration of the JVP drafted by its founder leader Rohana Wijeweera and some others in 1973 - would never materilise, given the threat it poses to the country’s economy. It might threaten the GSP plus concessions and hinder the very efforts to eradicate drug menace, as many countries would reconsider repatriation of drug lords to Sri Lanka to face the gallows.
