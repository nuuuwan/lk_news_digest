Sri Lanka back in ‘B’ league

Four years after Sri Lanka’s economic collapse pushed its sovereign rating into default territory, the country has climbed back into the single-‘B’ category, earning international recognition for a recovery built on higher taxes, tighter spending and painful economic reforms.

Fitch Ratings yesterday upgraded Sri Lanka’s Long-Term Issuer Default Ratings to ‘B-’ from ‘CCC+’ and assigned a Stable Outlook, signalling that the immediate danger of another sovereign default has receded, even as the country remains a high-risk borrower burdened by heavy debt and limited foreign-exchange protection.

The upgrade is more than a symbolic vote of confidence as it strengthens Sri Lanka’s credibility among international investors, could gradually improve financing conditions for the government and domestic companies, and supports Colombo’s ambition to return to global bond markets in 2027.

It does not, however, amount to an economic clean bill of health.

Sri Lanka remains several steps below investment grade, while its debt and interest costs are substantially higher than those of similarly rated countries. Its foreign reserves cover less than three months of external payments, and debt repayments are set to increase sharply after 2028.

The upgrade therefore marks a transition from crisis management to credibility management. Sri Lanka has demonstrated that it can stabilise an economy after default. Its next challenge is to preserve that stability when external financing becomes more difficult, debt repayments rise and domestic political pressure to loosen fiscal policy intensifies.

“The upgrade reflects the implementation of macro-stabilisation policies, underpinned by structural reforms, which have eased external financing risks and provide a degree of resilience to shocks,” Fitch said.

The rating agency pointed to sharp improvements in the fiscal and external accounts, a modest rebuilding of foreign-exchange reserves and stronger government revenue.

In April 2022, Fitch downgraded Sri Lanka to ‘Restricted Default’ as the country’s foreign-exchange crisis deepened. It upgraded the sovereign rating to ‘CCC+’ in December 2024 and affirmed it in October 2025.

The latest upgrade to ‘B-’ shows that Fitch now considers Sri Lanka’s credit profile to be more consistent with a sovereign facing a material, though no longer imminent, default risk.

For investors, the change provides an external validation of the country’s economic adjustment and could help reduce the risk premium attached to Sri Lankan assets over time. It may also improve sentiment towards government securities, banks and corporates whose credit profiles are influenced by the sovereign rating.

However, the immediate impact on borrowing costs may be limited as Sri Lanka remains in speculative-grade territory, and investors are likely to demand high returns until the country builds larger reserve buffers, lowers its debt burden and demonstrates several more years of policy consistency.

The ‘B-’ Country Ceiling also means the rating upgrade could create some room for improved assessments of Sri Lankan companies, although individual upgrades would depend on their own creditworthiness and exposure to sovereign risks.

A sovereign rating upgrade does not automatically generate foreign direct investment either. Fitch identified Sri Lanka’s continued difficulty in attracting FDI, expanding exports and reforming state-owned enterprises as constraints on medium-term growth.

The rating action is therefore likely to be most valuable as a confidence signal. It tells investors and lenders that Sri Lanka’s economic direction has improved, while placing greater pressure on the government to avoid reversing the policies that supported the recovery.

At the centre of the upgrade is Sri Lanka’s stronger fiscal performance.

Fitch forecast a primary Budget surplus of 2.6 percent of gross domestic product in 2026, down from a record 5.4 percent in 2025. The primary balance excludes interest payments and is a key measure of whether government revenue can cover non-interest expenditure.

Maintaining a primary surplus is critical for Sri Lanka because it allows the government to gradually reduce debt rather than borrowing further to fund its regular operations.

The overall Budget deficit is nevertheless expected to widen to 4.1 percent of GDP in 2026 from 2.3 percent in 2025, partly reflecting reconstruction expenditure following Cyclone Ditwah and targeted energy-support measures.

Government revenue has been strengthened by tax reforms and a temporary surge in import duties following the release of pent-up demand for imported vehicles.

Although vehicle-related revenue is expected to moderate, Fitch forecast government collections to remain just below 16 percent of GDP over the coming years. It expects primary surpluses to remain above 2 percent, supported by revenue mobilisation and expenditure restraint.

The Public Financial Management Act limits non-interest expenditure to 13 percent of GDP through 2031, providing a legal anchor for fiscal discipline. The limit has, however, been exceeded in 2026 due to cyclone reconstruction spending.

Fitch also warned that the risk of fiscal slippage could increase as the 2029 elections approach.

That warning is particularly significant for Sri Lanka. The country’s present stability rests heavily on the government’s willingness to sustain unpopular revenue and expenditure reforms. Any return to election-driven tax reductions, subsidies or unfunded spending could quickly weaken the fiscal gains underpinning the upgrade.

Government debt is forecast to decline to 92.9 percent of GDP in 2026 from 96.7 percent in 2025 and fall to the low-80 percent range over the next five years.

The direction is positive, though Sri Lanka’s debt burden remains far above the 54.7 percent median for sovereigns in the ‘B’ rating category.

Fitch expects the interest-to-revenue ratio to decline to 41 percent in 2026 from 45.6 percent in 2025 and a peak of 76.3 percent in 2023. Despite the improvement, the projected ratio remains more than three times the 12.7 percent median for ‘B’-rated countries.

This means more than Rs. 4 out of every Rs. 10 collected by the government is effectively absorbed by interest payments, leaving limited space for infrastructure, healthcare, education and social protection.

Sri Lanka has therefore improved its ability to service debt, though debt servicing continues to restrict its capacity to fund development.

Fitch expects higher energy prices to push the current account into a deficit of 1.2 percent of GDP in 2026, following three consecutive years in which Sri Lanka recorded an average surplus of 1.5 percent.

The US-Iran conflict has raised the energy import bill and temporarily weakened tourism inflows. Rising worker remittances have helped cushion the impact.

Sri Lanka’s dependence on imported fuel and fertiliser remains a structural weakness, leaving its external accounts exposed to geopolitical conflict and global commodity-price shocks.

Fitch expects the current account to return to near balance in 2027 as the energy shock eases.

Continued funding from the International Monetary Fund and other multilateral agencies is expected to support the balance of payments and allow Sri Lanka to build gross official reserves to US$ 7.7 billion by the end of 2026.

That would cover about 2.9 months of current external payments, still a relatively thin buffer for an economy with a history of foreign-exchange shortages.

The modest reserve position means another prolonged oil-price shock, a fall in tourism receipts or a weakening of remittance inflows could quickly place pressure on the rupee and external liquidity.

Sri Lanka’s most difficult test may arrive after the current period of multilateral support.

External debt repayments are expected to rise over the next five years, particularly after 2028. Fitch also expects the highest threshold attached to the country’s macro-linked bonds to be triggered, resulting in higher interest and principal payments.

The government is considering a return to global bond markets in 2027, while the present IMF Extended Fund Facility is due to end in March next year.

A ‘B-’ rating makes a future market return more credible than it was under ‘CCC+’. However, borrowing at sustainable interest rates will depend on global financial conditions, investor confidence and Sri Lanka’s ability to demonstrate that its debt trajectory remains manageable.

Entering international markets too early or accepting excessively high yields could recreate the refinancing risks that contributed to the previous crisis.

Fitch said a follow-on IMF programme remains possible. Such an arrangement could offer a financing backstop and preserve reform discipline as debt repayments increase.

Fitch forecast economic growth to slow to 4.1 percent in 2026 from an average of 5 percent over the previous two years, before settling at slightly above 4 percent over the medium term.

The agency said the economy had remained resilient to Cyclone Ditwah and the US-Iran conflict, although risks remain tilted to the downside because of Sri Lanka’s energy vulnerabilities.

Inflation is projected to average 6.3 percent in 2026, compared with negative 0.5 percent in 2025, driven by the global energy shock, El Niño conditions and domestic price pressures.

Fitch does not expect another monetary policy tightening after the Central Bank raised its key policy rate by 100 basis points to 8.75 percent in May. Inflation is forecast to fall in order to just below the Central Bank’s 5 percent target in 2027.

A further sovereign upgrade would require a substantial decline in government debt and interest costs, stronger growth and a large, sustainable increase in foreign-exchange reserves. Reduced fiscal discipline, weaker revenue collection, slower debt reduction or renewed external liquidity pressures could reverse the progress.
