Friends with all, subservient to none: Why Sri Lanka must guard its national interest

Whether it is a warship in distress, a tariff set in Washington, an investment from Beijing or a café in Arugam Bay, the question before us is the same: Are we protecting the interests of Sri Lanka and its people?

There was a time when Sri Lanka stood taller in the world than its size or its wealth would suggest. Under the late Sirimavo Bandaranaike, this country practiced what I believe was the finest non-aligned foreign policy in our history. The idea of the Indian Ocean as a zone of peace was first raised by Sri Lanka at the Non-Aligned summit in Cairo in 1964. In 1971, it was Sri Lanka, later joined by Tanzania, that took the proposal to the United Nations, and on 16 December that year the General Assembly adopted Resolution 2832, declaring the Indian Ocean a Zone of Peace. In August 1976, Colombo hosted the Fifth Summit of the Non-Aligned Movement, welcoming leaders from across Asia, Africa and Latin America, and Mrs. Bandaranaike assumed the chair of the Movement. A small island helped to set the agenda for the great powers.

That was not luck. It was the product of three qualities: clarity, consistency and credibility. We knew who we were, we said the same thing to every capital, and we were believed. As our country faces one of the most complicated external environments in its modern history – with a war in the Middle East spilling into our waters, volatile trade policy in our largest market and intense competition for influence in our ports – those three qualities are worth recalling.

Foreign policy is unlike any other area of government. In domestic policy, a government can experiment, make a mistake and correct it at the next Budget. In foreign policy, a careless word, a hasty decision or a single port call can follow a nation for a generation. Foreign policy is not something to be played with for political applause at home.

Every country, large or small, has economic, political and security interests. Those who deal with us pursue theirs, and they do so without apology. Our task is to pursue ours with the same seriousness, but with a clear understanding of who we are, what our real strength is and where we stand. That means building a foreign policy that survives changes of government: one that is debated in Parliament, understood by the public and applied consistently by every ministry, so that our partners know what to expect from Sri Lanka regardless of who sits in office.

During the Cold War, the rivalry between the superpowers was largely conducted directly between them. Since then, geopolitics has changed. The contest is no longer fought mainly between the great powers themselves; it is fought in other countries: in their ports, their sea lanes, their infrastructure, their debts and their politics. Sri Lanka, sitting beside some of the busiest shipping lanes in the Indian Ocean, has become geopolitical turf. We must be mindful of that every single day.

We have learnt this the hard way. Many will remember 4 June 1987. Shortly after our Navy had turned back a flotilla of boats carrying supplies, parippu and other provisions were dropped from the air over Jaffna, and we were powerless to prevent it. Within weeks, the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord had been signed. We will remember, too, that in the years before that, those who took up arms against this State received training on foreign soil. I do not raise these matters to reopen old wounds with a neighbour with whom our relations today are close and valued. I raise them as lessons. When the interests of larger nations are engaged, a small nation must know its actual strength, and it must ask itself honestly whether it can take other countries on in the global arena.

This brings me to the events of this year. On 4 March, a United States submarine torpedoed the Iranian frigate IRIS Dena in international waters off Galle, as it was returning home from a naval exercise hosted by India. The ship had around 180 people on board. Responding to its distress call, the Sri Lanka Navy, supported by the Sri Lanka Air Force, rescued 32 survivors and recovered 87 bodies. The next day, a second Iranian naval vessel, IRIS Bushehr, reported engine trouble and sought assistance; more than 200 of its crew were brought ashore and the ship was placed under Sri Lankan control. Last month, around 40 Iranian sailors from tankers stranded off our southern coast by the naval blockade in the Gulf, many of them in urgent need of medical care, were brought ashore through Galle for treatment and then sent home.

We cannot leave our export earnings so heavily dependent on a handful of markets, however friendly. Trade agreements with emerging markets in Asia, the Middle East and Africa must be pursued with urgency, and our exporters, particularly small and medium-sized enterprises, must be given the market intelligence, finance and diplomatic support to reach them. Our missions abroad should be judged, in part, by the new markets they open. That, too, is foreign policy

Let me be clear: Sri Lanka must never compromise on its humanitarian and human rights obligations. A sailor in distress is a human being first; his nationality comes second. International maritime law requires us to render assistance, and our own values, rooted in every one of our faiths, demand it. The President was right to say that every life is as precious as our own. I commend the Sri Lanka Navy, the Air Force, the doctors and nurses in Galle and Colombo, and everyone who responded so swiftly and professionally.

But humanitarian duty must go hand in hand with careful diplomacy. We must act transparently and through established international procedures, so that no one can say that Sri Lanka has taken sides in a war that is not ours. Parliament and the public deserve a clear account of the rules we are applying - who may enter our waters, on what terms, for how long, and what becomes of a vessel once it is in our custody. Neutrality is not silence, and it is not indifference. It is a principled position, and it must be explained clearly and consistently to all our partners, East and West alike. A small country that is seen to be neutral in word but partisan in deed loses the trust of everyone.

Nowhere is this balance more important than in our economy. Consider the facts. Our exports go primarily to the West. The United States alone is our largest single export market, taking more than a fifth of our merchandise exports, and together with the European Union it accounts for nearly half of all we sell abroad; add the United Kingdom, and it is more than half. Apparel dominates our shipments to America, and the industry employs more than 300,000 workers directly, many of them women from our rural villages.

When tariffs are pushed up in Washington, it is our apparel workers in Katunayake, in Biyagama and in our villages who feel it first. Over the past two years, we have watched the duty on our goods swing wildly. In April 2025, 44% reciprocal tariff was announced on Sri Lankan exports; it was later cut to 30% and then to 20%. In February this year, the United States Supreme Court struck down the legal basis for those tariffs, and a temporary 10 per cent duty was imposed in their place. In July, a new regime set our rate at 10%, sparing us a higher bracket for now. Our exporters have lived with every twist of that uncertainty, and each adverse shift has pushed our economy downhill. Planning a factory, a production line or a season’s orders in such conditions is extraordinarily difficult, and it is the worker on the factory floor who carries the risk.

At the same time, China is a key source of foreign direct investment for this country. The Colombo Port City and the proposed $ 3.7 billion Sinopec oil refinery at Hambantota – described, when it was signed in January 2025, as the largest foreign direct investment in our history – are only the most visible examples. Much of that investment is meant to produce goods and services that we will sell abroad, including to the West. So we invest with one side and earn from the other. It is a complex scenario, and it cannot be managed with slogans. It requires skill, patience and a steady hand, and it requires us to ensure that every major investment is transparent, commercially sound and consistent with our sovereignty and our neutrality.

When the present Government assumed office, there was considerable concern that it would abandon the IMF program, and that it would engage only with the East and ignore the West. In fairness, that has not happened. The Government has continued with the program agreed in March 2023; the IMF Executive Board completed the combined fifth and sixth reviews in May this year, and staff reached agreement on the seventh review in October. The Government has engaged Washington on tariffs and Beijing on investment, and has, so far, managed to strike a balance. Credit must be given where it is due.

A word of caution, however. Remaining within the program is one thing; being ruled by the dictates of the IMF is quite another. The program is a means to recovery, not a substitute for our own economic policy. As Parliament debates the 2027 Budget, on which the next IMF tranche depends, the pensioner, the small trader and the daily-wage earner must see that the decisions taken in their name are made here, by our own Parliament, for their benefit, and that the burden of reform is shared fairly.

We must also diversify. We cannot leave our export earnings so heavily dependent on a handful of markets, however friendly. Trade agreements with emerging markets in Asia, the Middle East and Africa must be pursued with urgency, and our exporters, particularly small and medium-sized enterprises, must be given the market intelligence, finance and diplomatic support to reach them. Our missions abroad should be judged, in part, by the new markets they open. That, too, is foreign policy.

Yet all of this balancing rests on something that, at present, we lack, our own ambassadors in the two capitals that matter most to us. Sri Lanka’s ambassadorial posts in Washington DC and in Beijing both stand vacant. Our Ambassador to China took formal leave of the Chinese Foreign Ministry in April, and our Ambassador to the United States ended his term and returned home in July. In August, by contrast, both the United States and China sent new ambassadors to Colombo, and each presented credentials to the President on the same day, 25 August. They are here, in our capital, advancing the interests of their countries, as they are entitled to do. We have no one of equal rank in theirs.

Some foreign nationals have arrived in Sri Lanka and taken jobs that are meant for our own citizens. Some have set up businesses, including guesthouses, surf schools, cafés and restaurants, and some of these establishments do not even welcome locals. In our own country, Sri Lankans are being made to feel unwelcome in places run by visitors. How can this happen? Who issued the approvals? Who is checking the visas? Who is collecting the taxes?

This is not a question of protocol. An ambassador is not a ceremonial figure. He or she is the voice of the Head of State in a foreign capital, with access to ministers, legislators and decision-makers that no chargé d’affaires, however able, can command. Consider what is at stake. In Washington, the tariff on our goods has been set and reset four times in little more than a year, and every change has been decided in rooms where Sri Lanka needed to be heard. In Beijing, the largest investment in our history is still to be built, and the terms on which it proceeds will shape our economy for decades. And in both capitals, our handling of the Iranian vessels is being watched closely and judged. These are precisely the moments when a small country must speak through its most senior representative, not wait for messages to be relayed.

Our business community has already sounded the alarm. In August, the BRICS Chamber of Commerce (Sri Lanka) wrote to the President and the Minister of Foreign Affairs about the vacancies in both the United States and China, urging the appointment of envoys with proven diplomatic skill and commercial standing. Our apparel exporters, our investors and the hundreds of thousands of workers who depend on them cannot afford to have Sri Lanka’s case presented by default.

I therefore urge the Government to nominate ambassadors to Washington and Beijing without further delay, and to place those names before the Committee on High Posts at the earliest opportunity. Those chosen must be selected on merit – for their experience, their judgement and their understanding of trade and investment – not as a reward for political loyalty. And they should be appointed together. Filling both posts at the same time would itself send a message, East and West alike, that Sri Lanka gives equal weight to both relationships. An empty embassy in one capital is a misfortune; empty embassies in both, for months on end, risk being read as indifference, and that is a message no non-aligned nation can afford to send.

Let me also be clear that we welcome every tourist. Visitors who respect our laws, our culture and our communities are our honoured guests. Genuine foreign investors who come through the proper channels, create jobs for Sri Lankans and pay their taxes are welcome too. This is not about any nationality, and it must never become so. It is about the rule of law

As a former Deputy Minister of Tourism, I have watched with pride as our tourism industry has recovered from the Easter Sunday attacks, the pandemic and the economic crisis. Last year, Sri Lanka welcomed 2,362,521 tourists, the highest number in our history, surpassing the previous record set in 2018. Tourism feeds families in every corner of this island: the three-wheeler driver, the cook, the guide, the fisherman who supplies the beach restaurant. But I am deeply concerned about what is happening in some of our best-known resorts.

Some foreign nationals have arrived in Sri Lanka and taken jobs that are meant for our own citizens. Some have set up businesses, including guesthouses, surf schools, cafés and restaurants, and some of these establishments do not even welcome locals. In our own country, Sri Lankans are being made to feel unwelcome in places run by visitors. How can this happen? Who issued the approvals? Who is checking the visas? Who is collecting the taxes?

The law is clear - those who come on tourist visas are not permitted to do business here. Yet in Arugam Bay, in Weligama, in Hikkaduwa and elsewhere, such businesses continue, often through proxy local partners. Our registered hoteliers and tour operators pay their taxes, follow the regulations and train local staff. These unregulated operators do none of that, and they undercut our own people. They also deprive the Treasury of revenue at a time when every rupee counts, and they damage the reputation of destinations that our own entrepreneurs have spent decades building.

I acknowledge that the authorities have acted. According to the Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority, 75 foreign nationals were arrested and 15 deported last year for doing business while on tourist visas. But treating these cases merely as individual visa violations is not enough. The problem is structural, and it requires a structural response. Other destinations in our region have faced the same challenge and have learnt that occasional raids, without proper registration, licensing and tax enforcement, simply move the problem from one beach to the next.

Let me also be clear that we welcome every tourist. Visitors who respect our laws, our culture and our communities are our honoured guests. Genuine foreign investors who come through the proper channels, create jobs for Sri Lankans and pay their taxes are welcome too. This is not about any nationality, and it must never become so. It is about the rule of law. The unregulated way in which some of these businesses operate is a direct threat to a vibrant and inclusive tourism industry – an industry that must, above all, benefit the people of Sri Lanka.

I have therefore called upon the Government to take four steps:

1. Carry out a comprehensive, district-by-district audit of foreign-run tourism businesses, so that we know the true scale of the problem.

2. Close the proxy-ownership loopholes and strengthen joint enforcement by the Sri Lanka Tourism Development Authority, the Department of Immigration and Emigration and the Police.

3. Introduce clear and transparent rules for foreigners who genuinely wish to invest or work in tourism, so that the honest are welcomed and the dishonest are removed.

4. Make it unlawful for any tourism establishment in Sri Lanka to refuse service to Sri Lankans on the ground of their nationality.

The threads of this argument run together. A warship in distress, a tariff in Washington, an investment from Beijing and a café in Arugam Bay may seem to be unrelated matters. They are not. Each tests whether we are willing to put the interests of Sri Lanka and its people first – calmly, consistently and without fear. Each demands that we know our strength, respect our obligations and speak with one voice. And that voice must be heard where it matters most: we cannot speak with one voice in Washington and Beijing if there is no one there to speak for us.

Mrs. Bandaranaike showed us that a small country can be respected by all and dominated by none, if it knows its strength, acts with consistency and speaks with one voice. Friends with all, subservient to none. That must be our foreign policy, our economic policy and our tourism policy.
