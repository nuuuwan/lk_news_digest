The iconic MiG-21:  Interceptor which gave third world air forces a fighting chance

On July 7 1966, a sleek, fast fighter plane hitherto un-encountered by Western air forces came like a bolt from the blue to down an American F-105 Thunderchief fighter bomber over North Vietnam, marking the first-ever MiG-21 aerial victory and forcing US to re-think air warfare tactics.

But, while the Republic of Vietnam retired its MiG-21 fleet a decade ago in 2015, India retained its faith in this iconic Cold-War era interceptor, finally  retiring its MiG-21 fighters only  in September 2025, after 62 years of active service.

The Mikoyan-Guryevich MiG-21, produced in greater numbers than any other supersonic aircraft in aviation history, still continues to serve with several air forces including Cuba, Angola and North Korea, testimony to its adaptability and durability.

The MiG-21 Bison (its NATO codename) entered service with the Indian Air Force (IAF) in 1963,  when   North Vietnam’s fledgling air force was still in the making, and pilots were sent to China and the ex-USSR for training. They would have to combat formidable American  opponents such as the McDonnell Douglas F-4 Phantom II and the  F-105 Thunderchief, the backbone of the U.S. Air Force’s tactical bombing operations during the early and middle years of the Vietnam War.

The Americans believed the Phantom to be invincible in the air. It was a twin-engined, two seater multi-role intercepter, and its designers were so confident of the potency of its  twin Sidewinder air-to-air missiles  that they did away with the traditional Canon armament altogether. It was also believed that the small, newly formed North Vietnamese air force, equipped initially with subsonic MiG 17 interceptors, would be no serious threat  for the Phantom.

North Vietnam did not have any jet fighters  prior to 1964. In early 1964, Hanoi received 36 subsonic MiG-15 trainers  and MiG-17s.

Against this, the US had 300-400 fighter jets flying missions into North Vietnam by end of 1965. The first American aircraft shot down by MiGs in air-to-air combat over North Vietnam were two  F-105 Thunderchiefs,  downed on April 4, 1965 by  MiG-17s.

The first MiG-21s arrived in Vietnam in 1965. It was much lighter than the Phantom and had a shorter range. The Mikoyan-Guryevich Design Bureau had designed the  MiG-21 for very short ground-controlled interception (GCI) missions. It became renowned for this type of mission over the skies over North Vietnam.

Together with the already proven MiG 17s, they performed “guerrilla warfare in the air” with the MiG-17s flying below radar and appearing like ghosts from below American formations,  while the MiG-21s attacked from the rear. These tactics were so effective that the F-105 Thunderchiefs which flew over 20,000 combat missions over Vietnam was finally withdrawn from service.

Although 13 of North Vietnam’s flying aces flew the MiG-21,  many North Vietnamese People’s Air Force (NVPA) pilots preferred the MiG-17 because the high wing loading of the MiG-21 made it relatively less maneuverable,  and the  canopy of the MiG-17 gave better visibility.

But  British author Roger Boniface who  interviewed NVPA aces Pham Ngoc Lan and  Nguyn Nht Chiêu (who scored victories in both MiG 17s and 21s) was told by Pham Ngoc Lan  that “The MiG-21 was much faster, and it had two ATOLL missiles which were very accurate and reliable when fired between 1,000 and 1,200 yards.”

Chiêu asserted that “... personally I preferred the MiG-21 because it was superior in all specifications in climb, speed and armament. The ATOLL missile was very accurate and I scored four kills with the ATOLL. ... In general combat conditions I was always confident of a kill over a F-4 Phantom when flying a MiG-21.”

Once its Sidewinder missiles were fired, the Phantom was defenseless against the MiG-21’s 30mm canon. The Americans quickly equipped the Phantom with a fast-firing 20mm canon, and established the Top Gun fighter training school so that American pilots could match the dog-fighting skills of opponents flying the highly maneouverable MiG-21s and MiG-17s.

Although the MiG-21 lacked the long-range radar and heavy bomb loads of the Phantom,  with its RP-21 Sapfir radar it proved formidable in the hands of experienced pilots, especially when used in high-speed hit-and-run attacks under GCI control.

The North Vietnamese flew their interceptors with guidance from ground controllers, who positioned the MiGs in ambush battle stations to make their “one pass, then haul ass” attacks.

During the 1965 Indo-Pakistan War, the MiG-21 did not play a major role as the IAF had only 12-16 of these at the time, and they were not fully operational. But it was the  was the IAF’s mainstay during the 1971 war with Pakistan. The IAF seems to have fallen in love with the aircraft, putting it into uses it wasn’t originally designed for. It  showed its abilities as a fighter bomber in the 1999 Kargil conflict. By then, several women pilots too, were flying the MiG-21, and this versatile interceptor made its final combat appearance in the subcontinent when  a MiG-21 engaged a Pakistani F-16 on February 27, 2019  over Jammu and Kashmir,  and was shot down.

The F-16 is a multi-role, incredibly versatile fourth generation  fighter introduced two decades after the MiG-21.  The IAF said it had only the MiG-21 available at that location to meet the threat. It was a mismatch. But the very fact that the IAF decided to send up a by then obsolete MiG-21 against a F-16 shows the degree of faith this iconic aircraft inspired in India.

In the 2019 Kargil conflict, the IAF used its MiG-21s as night bombers carrying laser bombs for precision bombing, and the then IAF commander said these operations were crucial in defeating the enemy.

MiG-21s were used in large numbers by Egyptian and Syrian air forces in their wars with Israel. Several Arab aces flying the MiG-21 are credited with downing Israeli Mirage and Phantom fighter jets, but information on these combats is hard to obtain. Large Syrian air losses in the latter stages of the 1973 Yom Kippur war were due to data supplied by American AWACs aircraft, which gave the Israelis a tremendous advantage.

More than  11,000 MiG-21 variants were built worldwide. Additionally, India and Czechoslovakia built 851 more under license. China made thousands of license-built reverse-engineered variants such as Chengdu J-7 and these were sold to roughly 60 countries. It was ideal for third world air forces, and cheaper to buy and maintain than any Western interceptor. It could fly from rough, short air fields while its American counterparts required the finest concrete. Elegantly simple, it was designed to get off the ground fast, approach the target quickly and destroy it. The skies over North Vietnam amply proved its abilities.

There is one sour note to the MiG-21 legend. Due to a number of accidents in India, it earned the unsavoury nickname ‘Flying Coffin’ in its later years with the IAF. Various theories have been forwarded for this, including one that it tends to stall during certain maneouvers at high speeds.

But nothing of the kind has been reported from any of the approximately 60 air forces worldwide which flew the MiG-21. If the aircraft was at fault, what happened in India should have happened elsewhere, too. Therefore, the IAF crashes may be attributed to maintenance or assembly issues, though this too, is only conjecture. The fact remains that the IAF chose to fly its MiG-21s year after year despite the crashes. The final, moving farewell ceremony in 2025 made it look as if the IAF was almost reluctant to let the MiG-21 retire.
