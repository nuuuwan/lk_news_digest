Grenades become weapon of fear: Available for as little as Rs. 10,000 – 20,000

Colombo, Sep. 12 (Daily Mirror) - Hand grenades are increasingly being used by organised criminal groups to threaten rivals and settle disputes, raising concerns among security authorities over the availability of old military explosives and the danger they pose to the public.

The Daily Mirror learns that the explosives can reportedly be obtained on Sri Lanka’s black market for as little as Rs. 10,000 to Rs. 20,000, according to security sources.

“They are available through various sources, including areas associated with former military personnel, where hand grenades may remain buried. If someone seeks to purchase one, it can be obtained for around Rs. 10,000 to Rs. 20,000, even without the knowledge of the original owner,” the sources revealed.

The availability of such explosives has come to attention following yesterday's incident in Kawdana, Dehiwala, which claimed the lives of two children and seriously injured their father.

Police investigations found that the family was not believed to have been the intended target. The explosive was allegedly meant for a neighbouring residence linked to an underworld dispute but was thrown at the wrong house.

A senior police officer said criminal groups often use such devices primarily as a means of intimidation rather than as a direct attempt to kill.

“They throw a bomb to frighten someone and leave. It is mainly used to threaten members of rival groups or people involved in disputes over drugs and other criminal activities,” the officer said.

Security sources said firearms have become harder to obtain amid ongoing police operations against organised crime, leading some groups to turn to locally made weapons and explosives.

Of particular concern are old military hand grenades believed to have remained in illegal stockpiles. Security officials said some can remain dangerous for years if properly preserved.

“Most of the time, they use intimidation against members of their own groups—like when others sell drug stock belonging to them, or take up contracts from others. In those situations, to scare them, they throw a bomb and go. That’s what they do.

“There are no weapons; nowadays, even for shootings, they mostly use improvised firearms or local single-barrel guns. Those are locally made and brought out. Weapons are very rare for them,” the officer said.

The recent incidents have raised concerns that explosives are becoming an increasingly common means of intimidation in disputes involving organised crime.

On September 9, two men travelling on a motorcycle allegedly threw a hand grenade into the premises of a house in Kalubowila, Dehiwala, before fleeing. The blast damaged windows and glass panes at the front of the residence, but no injuries were reported. Police said the property belonged to an individual believed to be a close associate of a major drug trafficker. Only a 56-year-old woman was inside at the time.

Earlier, on August 23, a house on Lesley Ranagala Mawatha in Borella was targeted. The grenade failed to explode, and no injuries were reported. Police said the residence was allegedly linked to an associate of organised-crime figure “Wanathe Chathu,” who was recently arrested in Dubai.

Another incident was reported on August 5 on Sri Gnanendra Mawatha in Ratmalana, where two grenades were allegedly thrown at a property. One exploded, but no injuries were reported. Police said the premises were located close to the residence of the mother of alleged drug trafficker “Russell Smith.”

Police suspect the incidents are linked to an ongoing conflict between rival organised-crime groups.

The growing use of explosives in organised crime raises serious public safety concerns, as such attacks can have consequences far beyond their intended targets and cost innocent lives.
