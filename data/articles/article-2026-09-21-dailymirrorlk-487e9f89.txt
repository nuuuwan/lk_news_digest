Sri Lanka navigates path to implement  out-of-country voting

Even though OCV was discussed during the 2024 Presidential Election, Sri Lanka is still in its preliminary stages

Two-day regional conference held in Colombo to discuss persisting challenges regarding voting rights for overseas Sri Lankans

Instrument that addresses political participation of immigrants is the Intl. Convention for the Rights of Migrant Workers

Despite having over 1.5 million citizens working overseas, Sri Lanka is one among many South Asian countries that is yet to implement Out-of-Country Voting (OCV). Studies indicate that in South Asia, millions of people relocate annually due to factors driven by employment, education, family circumstances, safety concerns or environmental pressures. But, apart from a few exceptions, electoral frameworks across the region require people to be physically present at the polling station on the day of the election. Even though OCV was discussed during the 2024 Presidential Election, Sri Lanka is still in its preliminary stages. In this backdrop, a two-day regional conference was recently held in Colombo to discuss international best practices and persisting challenges regarding voting rights for overseas Sri Lankans.

At this conference, jointly organised by the Elections Commission of Sri Lanka and the International Foundation for Electoral Systems (IFES), representatives from Australia, Bangladesh, Maldives, Nepal and the Philippines shared their practical experiences and successful mechanisms for securing voting rights for citizens living abroad.

Modern democracies are evolving and human mobility is reshaping societies, economies and political systems across the globe. There was a time when elections were conducted within the boundaries of countries. People’s democratic rights too were exercised within these boundaries.

According to IFES’ Regional Director Vasu Mohan, around 304 million people were living outside the borders of their countries as per 2024 statistics. “Mobility is becoming an increasingly important feature in modern society. This is an issue significant for countries with large populations abroad. So what are their rights and how are their rights going to be expressed? There is no single international answer, but many countries have developed their own approaches,” he said in his remarks.

Mohan said that the only international instrument that talks about political participation and voting rights of citizens living abroad is the International Convention for the Rights of Migrant Workers. “But even this convention specifies that it must be done ‘in accordance with national legislation.’ So national constitutions and domestic legislations are going to be the overarching framework for this process. The question is not how we move ballots across borders, but how we move democratic rights, because people are already moving. IFES doesn’t recommend any country to either implement or not implement OCV. These are sovereign decisions completely rooted in each country’s own experience. What we do instead is facilitate sharing of international best practices, particularly in south-south collaboration and regional collaboration among Asia-Pacific nations,” he added.

Speaking at the inaugural event, Sri Lanka Elections Commission member Ameer Faiz said that Sri Lanka is ready to implement OCV. “But what lies in between implementation and intention is the legal authority to proceed. The difficulty has never been the principle; difficulty is in the details,” he said.

Faiz raised some pertinent questions in his remarks. “How do we reach citizens spread across the globe? Some are abroad for a season and others for a lifetime. Which model suits us-postal, in-person at our missions, electronic or a combination of the three? Or are there any other models in sight which we could jointly explore? How do we ensure that every citizen is given an equal opportunity to vote? How much does it cost? What do we do when something goes wrong at 2.00 am in a time zone with a 6 hour difference? These are not questions that a textbook could answer. These should be answered by people who have actually done it and more valuable still, people who have done mistakes doing it and are candid enough to say so aloud,” he added.

He further said that there is a substantial population of citizens living and working overseas. “Most of them remain deeply connected to their families, communities and country. They contribute to their country through remittances, skills, knowledge, investments and through connections they build between Sri Lanka and the wider world. Many care deeply about strengthening democracy and safeguarding rights. While we count their contributions in many ways we are yet to count them in our ballot boxes,” Faiz underscored.

In her remarks, Australia’s Deputy High Commissioner to Sri Lanka Ruth Baird said that the diplomatic partnership between Sri Lanka and Australia is reaching its 80-year milestone.

“Elections are fundamental to democratic societies. Supporting citizens to participate in elections while living abroad is an issue many election management bodies continue to consider and navigate. Overseas voting raises a range of legal, policy, administrative, technological and operational considerations. Different countries have approached these questions in ways that reflect their own circumstances, institutions and priorities,” she added.

She underscored the need to explore how election management bodies could maintain the integrity and security of electoral processes while making voting accessible. “How can systems respond to citizens living in very different locations and circumstances? Key to all, how can public confidence be maintained throughout? Each country has developed approaches shaped by their own constitutional frameworks, their own electoral laws, institutions, geographies, diaspora patterns and administrative realities. Australia, home to vibrant diaspora communities and a proud democratic tradition, recognises both the challenge and the necessity of getting overseas voting right,” she added.

The NPP government has appointed a Parliamentary Select Committee to initiate the process of establishing an OCV mechanism for Sri Lankans living overseas, said Minister of Public Administration, Provincial Councils and Local Government Chandana Abeyratne.

He said that the government has demonstrated its commitment to democracy by conducting free and fair elections while maintaining public confidence in the country’s democratic process. “NPP’s electoral framework aims to build a more democratic, transparent and inclusive system of representation in Sri Lanka. Over 1.5 million citizens live and work overseas. Their relationship with Sri Lanka extends beyond geographical distances. As we consider expanding facilities for our citizens residing overseas to participate in elections and exercise their democratic rights, we must ensure that such measures safeguard electoral integrity, security, transparency and public confidence in the electoral process,” he added.

OCV models implemented in several regional countries are as follows ;

Bangladesh – The push for OCV followed the August 2024 student-led mass uprising. The Bangladesh Election Commission was left with a window of only 9 months to design, test, and implement an OCV system from scratch. After analyzing three primacy OCV models; postal, proxy and online voting the commission built consensus around a hybrid postal model.

Australia - In 2025, the Australian Elections Commission (AEC) delivered services to a relatively small overseas electorate of roughly 17,000 voters across 83 countries with 101 polling places. The AEC relies on in-person voting at diplomatic missions and postal voting supported by a hand-to-hand courier service.

Maldives - Maldivian OCV is automatically open to all citizens over 18 based on permanent residency. However, two-thirds of the population does not reside at their permanent address, living instead in the capital (Malé), industrial zones, tourist resorts, or overseas. To resolve this, the election management body introduced a dynamic registration layer, allowing voters to choose their polling destination. To manage costs, the Maldives established strict minimum thresholds: for example, a polling station was set up in domestic resorts/industrial zones if a minimum of 30 voters registered.

Philippines - Guided by the 1987 Constitution, the Philippines enacted Republic Act 9189 (Overseas Absentee Voting Act of 2003), which was later amended by Republic Act 10590 in 2013 which granted the Commission of Elections broad powers to explore electronic and internet-based OCV. Marking a historic milestone, the 2025 midterm pilot enrolled 234,475 pre-voting enrollees (19.2% of registered overseas voters) and implemented internet voting across 77 of 93 foreign posts, covering more than 1.2 million registered voters.

Nepal – Similarly to Sri Lanka, Nepal is reviving efforts to implement OCV. Over the last 13 years, Nepal has successfully conducted over 251 institutional and party elections using electronic voting machines. The introduction of the Digital Verifiable Paper Audit Trail —which displays the voter’s selection on a screen for 10 seconds and can audibly announce it in their native mother tongue before printing and dropping the receipt into a physical ballot box—has drastically increased voter confidence and simplified post-election recounts.
