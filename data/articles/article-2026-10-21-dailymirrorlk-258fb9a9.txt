Sri Lanka must move beyond calls for more FDI: Balendra

Calls for targeted policies to identify competitive exports, benchmark costs and address supply chain gaps

Stresses consistent taxes and business policies to build long-term investor confidence

Sees India-led growth opportunities in ports and tourism as JKH focuses on returns from major investments

Sri Lanka must back its ambitions for higher exports and foreign direct investment with targeted policies that identify where the country can compete and address the practical needs of investors, premier diversified blue-chip John Keells Holdings PLC chief said.

JKH Chairman Krishan Balendra last week cautioned that repeated calls to boost exports and investment had not been matched by sufficient work to identify competitive products, benchmark costs against rival destinations and establish the supply chains needed to support production.

“We keep talking about exports and FDI, but we need to do a lot more work to identify in what areas that investment should be targeted,” Balendra told the THINC Sri Lanka & Maldives 2026 conference at Hilton Colombo recently.

The head of one of Sri Lanka’s most diversified listed conglomerates, with interests spanning tourism, transportation, retail, consumer foods, financial services and property, stressed that economic stability must now be supported by measures that deliver growth.

Balendra questioned whether Sri Lanka had adequately studied the products in which it could enjoy a comparative advantage and compared the cost and ease of producing them with competing locations such as India, Malaysia and Vietnam.

Investment promotion also required a closer examination of supply chains, including whether manufacturers could obtain the inputs needed to operate competitively.

“One product will need many inputs. Are the inputs available? How do we enable those inputs?” he asked.

While there was considerable discussion about increasing exports, Balendra said there had been insufficient policy work to determine which products could drive that expansion.

He identified policy consistency as another priority, pointing to longstanding concerns among foreign and local investors over frequent changes to taxes and other business conditions.

Tax rates had moved up and down over the past 15 to 20 years, while elections brought changes in policymakers and ministers. Consistent policies and tax rates over an extended period were critical to giving investors the confidence to commit capital, he said.

Balendra nevertheless maintained that Sri Lanka offered a more stable investment environment than it had for a long time, arguing that international perceptions had yet to fully reflect the recovery from the 2022 economic crisis.

The severity of the crisis had enabled policymakers to take difficult decisions and implement reforms that would otherwise have been politically unpopular, he said.

He pointed to improvements in fiscal revenue, primary and current account balances, and the performance of state-owned enterprises as evidence of the stronger macroeconomic position.

“The macro stability I think is there. We need to maintain it and grow from here.”

Balendra also identified India’s expansion as a major opportunity for Sri Lanka, particularly in ports and tourism, likening the country’s position to being next door to China 25 years ago.

Colombo’s proximity to the main east-west shipping route and its role as a transshipment hub for Indian cargo provided a strong platform for growth. He said Colombo Port could handle twice its current volume, or more, over the next decade.

India’s growing outbound travel market presented a similar opportunity, although Sri Lanka needed a more coordinated effort to position and promote itself.

Balendra said the increase in tourist arrivals since the end of the civil war had largely occurred without a sustained government and private sector strategy to develop Sri Lanka as a high-value destination.

He pointed to aggressive promotion by competing destinations such as Thailand, Indonesia and Malaysia in the Indian market, contrasting this with Sri Lanka’s limited visibility.

Closer coordination between the government and private sector was needed to develop tourism policy, establish the country’s positioning and market the destination, he said.

For JKH, the immediate priority following a major capital expenditure cycle was to secure returns from investments including City of Dreams and the West Container Terminal.

While the group continued to assess overseas opportunities, Balendra indicated that its near-term focus would be on getting the best out of those investments.

He also affirmed the group’s intention to deepen its presence across its existing businesses, citing substantial scope for growth in supermarkets, life insurance, consumer foods, tourism and ports.

Despite the expansion in supermarket networks, only about 16 to 17 percent of grocery consumption took place through modern retail, he estimated. Low life insurance penetration and relatively low per capita consumption of soft drinks and ice cream also offered room for expansion.

“So we will double down on all these sectors because we think the potential is there,” Balendra said.
