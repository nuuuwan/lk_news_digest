The Rising Tide of Aging uncovers the Silver Economy in Sri Lanka

Originally introduced in 1973 as Silver Seats for elderly passengers on Tokyo and Osaka lines, they were renamed and re-branded in the 1990s as priority seats

Sri Lanka is currently experiencing one of the most rapid demographic transitions in South Asia. In the early 2020s, approximately 12.3% of the population was aged 60 years or older, representing the highest proportion of older adults in the region, according to data compiled by the United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs. Projections by the World Bank indicate that this share is expected to rise to nearly 25% within the next 15 years.

This shift signals a transformation from a predominantly youthful demographic structure to one increasingly characterised by older age groups, a phenomenon often described as a “silver tsunami.” While this demographic change presents significant socio-economic challenges, it simultaneously opens many avenues for innovation, entrepreneurship, and market diversification etc.

The identification of demographic changes will allow probable transitions that may rigour business development opportunities for new products and services in the market.  Identifying and tailoring such products and services to fulfill the needs of older adults will enable strengthening Sri Lanka’s economic landscape and fostering an inclusive economic growth.

The rising demand for eldercare and preventive health services has become a need than a wanting.  Expanding geriatric care, strengthen community-based support systems, and integrate technology-driven assistive devices to address the needs of Sri Lanka’s aging population requires much attention in service perspective. These interventions not only enhance the quality of life for older adults but also stimulate new economic opportunities within the healthcare and technology sectors. Furthermore, the introduction of lifelong savings schemes, mobile banking solutions tailored to older users and micro-insurance products can promote financial inclusion and security to this segment of community. Collectively, these initiatives demonstrate that Sri Lanka’s aging population is not merely a social challenge but a catalyst for a new business market era.

The term “silver economy” was born to the world in the year 1970 when Japanese National Railways suggested “silver seats” to elderly citizens. The world over, the aging population is catalysing the future proofing,  Silver Market and has become a powerful force to build new markets with products and services while creating and redefining aging as economic and social value. The silver economy refers to the economic activities created by the needs and the spending power of the population of older adults

Sri Lanka has been traditionally heavily relying on labour migration and their remittances; now it prompts for new pathways for diverse arrays for new business projects and to offer different services for older adults for their unique consumption patterns and services. This is where the country should review the tailored economic policies that are essential to the shifts in the economic platform to maximise the benefits to all stakeholders to harness the full potential of the silver economy.

The inclusive policies would improve the citizens of all walks of life that entrust and broaden the strategic approaches in managing human capital for better economic creation,  thus ensuring that older adults  too, are actively contributing to the economy without becoming an economic burden. In addition, it provides flexible retirement systems and provides a focus business market for eldercare products and infrastructure to expand the business platforms leveraging the silver economy.

Silver economy will open many opportunities for leadership roles in emerging industries and startups,  and their experience and expertise position them to drive innovation, guide for strategic growth and mentor younger entrepreneurs.

It is evident that the silver transition is driven by the demographic shifts that are taking place towards ageing societies and in many western countries the older adult population engages actively in the workforce surpassing 50-60 years. Usually the retirement policies specify a “minimum age” rather than fixing a retirement age with an upper ceiling.  Very surprisingly in Sri Lanka, the Minimum Retirement Age policy clauses are misunderstood by the majority of employers; the “minimum retirement age” is being treated as the “mandatory retirement age” misinterpreting the Employment Act of Minimum Age of Retirement and thereby limiting the opportunities for older adults contributing to the economy at a higher age limit of 60 years.

Sri Lanka also could draw lessons from Singapore, Japan and Belgium by re-introducing targeted tax incentives to active older employees aligning same to corporate tax deductions, corporate tax credit on qualifying older worker payroll cost, statutory contribution concessions for employers who recruit and retain older adult workers beyond a standard retirement age, designing a tax incentive model for corporate taxes creating an economic opportunity.

Due to the high dynamics and unpredictability of changes taking place in the modern economy and the constant increase in competition in the business market, the ability to attract and retain  experienced talented employees may offer re-employment and obtaining their expertise for sustainable competitive advantage,  also strengthening the sustainable tools of diversity, inclusivity and equity in human capital management approaches in Sri Lanka.

According to some available statistics,  the status-quo of the older adults is unclear.  It is required to enable the older adults with employment opportunities and they have the great potential to contribute to Sri Lanka’s economy.  The records revealed that 49% of 55-64 years older adults are economically inactive, and the labour force involvement rate in males and females recorded 36% and 11% respectively may be due to limited employment options.

For example, companies that retain or hire employees over the age of 60 beyond the statutory retirement age could qualify for a percentage reduction in their total corporate income tax liability, proportionate to the remuneration made to the older workers in their workforce when calculating taxable profits.

Sri Lanka stands at a pivotal juncture where the challenges of an aging population can be transformed into opportunities for sustainable growth. By strategically investing in social support systems such as healthcare, workforce participation, and digital inclusion,  the nation can foster a robust silver economy. Such an economy would not only enhance the quality of life for older citizens,  but also strengthen overall economic resilience by recognising older adults as vital contributors to Sri Lanka’s human capital.

The ageing population in Sri Lanka should be viewed as a growing economic opportunity and transform longevity into an economic advantage. It includes phased retirement plans with employment opportunities, part-time work, and also work-from-home arrangements to the targeted older worker with flexible work arrangements to engage them with economic activities to increase productivity. It is viewed that the older adults can significantly contribute through continued employment, mentoring, entrepreneurship, investment, consumption, volunteering to strengthen intergenerational knowledge transfer.

The Silver Economy focuses on economic activities, markets, and also on policy frameworks regarding older adults, above age 50 for their evolving needs and could generate many significant opportunities for social development and sustainable economic growth in Sri Lanka. A challenging country policy framework may transform the perceived social challenge of an ageing population into a great opportunity to use the knowledge, entrepreneurship, productivity, investments consumption etc.

Sri Lanka can unlock the demographic opportunity of a Silver Economy with the Silver Dividend to turn longer lives, to stronger economic lives. The question is whether Sri Lanka is ready to turn longevity into an economic opportunity as the population ages; economic dependency is not necessary when older adults are empowered to remain contributing to society as long as they are ready and able,  and they are seen  as an economic asset.
