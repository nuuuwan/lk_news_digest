A decade after Paris Agreement: Financing farms and natural capital

Sri Lanka should use climate finance to protect farms and the soils, water, forests and biodiversity that sustain them. A decade after the Paris Agreement entered into force, success should be judged by whether investment reduces crop losses, secures water and protects livelihoods. Farmers need timely advice as rainfall becomes less predictable; families need reliable food supplies at affordable prices.

Adopted in December 2015, opened for signature in April 2016 and brought into force in November that year, the Agreement created a shared framework to limit warming, strengthen adaptation and support developing countries. It seeks to keep warming well below 2°C while pursuing efforts towards 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. Its anniversary invites scrutiny of both achievements and unfinished work.

The Paris Agreement established a continuing process through which countries submit climate commitments, strengthen them over time and report progress. These Nationally Determined Contributions, or NDCs, have brought climate concerns into national planning. Common reporting arrangements and periodic global reviews allow countries’ collective progress to be assessed.

Yet these institutional achievements have not secured a safe climate pathway. The Emissions Gap Report (2026) of the United Nations Environment Program (UNEP) estimates that delivering national commitments and continuing similar efforts would limit warming this century to about 2.6°C. The Paris Agreement has organised the global response, but commitments and implementation remain inadequate. The Adaptation Gap Report (2025) of UNEP has estimated the annual adaptation needs for developing countries in 2035 would cost around

$ 310–365 billion, compared with international public adaptation finance of $ 26 billion in 2023. Although the years differ, the figures illustrate the scale of the financing challenge.

According to the OECD, developed countries first exceeded their collective annual $ 100 billion climate finance goal in 2022, two years late. The 29th UNFCCC climate conference (COP29) agreed on a new goal of at least $ 300 billion annually by 2035 for developing countries. Developed countries are to lead in mobilising this finance from public, private and other sources. This is a future funding goal, not money already disbursed to vulnerable communities.

Spending priorities matter too. Reducing emissions, or mitigation, must proceed alongside adaptation that helps people cope with changing conditions. Farmers need support before harvests fail. Governments and funding agencies should assess projects by timely disbursement, lasting benefits and the share reaching those facing the greatest risks.

Difficulties in the agricultural sector have emanated due to several causes, for example: Input costs, policy decisions, markets and land degradation interact with weather. Hence, attributing every production decline to climate change would weaken the argument. Finance should address demonstrated climate risks while improving farmers’ ability to respond.

The NDC 3.0 of Sri Lanka covers the period 2026–2035 and includes agriculture in both mitigation and adaptation segments. Priorities include climate advice, resilient varieties and breeds, better soil and water management, efficient input use and reduced postharvest losses. These provide a direction for investment.

There is practical experience to build on. The Green Climate Fund (GCF)-supported Climate Resilient Integrated Water Management Project connects village irrigation improvements with water security in the Malwathu Oya, Mi Oya and Yan Oya basins. This approach links farming with the landscapes supplying its water. Its lasting value requires independently verified outcomes and continued maintenance.

National commitments alone do not demonstrate greater resilience. Government agencies and project implementers should establish whether households experience fewer losses, more dependable water and more stable incomes. Interventions must remain affordable and effective after external funding ends, with clear responsibilities and national budgets for continued operation.

The Government should make natural capital assessment part of agricultural and climate investment planning. Natural capital comprises soils, water, forests, wetlands and biodiversity. These assets support farming through water regulation, erosion control and pollination. Assessing their condition helps identify where degradation threatens production and where protection offers lasting benefits.

Such assessments should identify the assets, their condition, the people who depend on them and their exposure to climate risks. Existing surveys, satellite information and local knowledge need field verification. Priority assessment areas could include upper watersheds supplying reservoirs, degraded agricultural slopes and vulnerable tank catchments.

Natural capital assessment should begin with physical measures such as soil loss, water quality, habitat condition and seasonal water availability. Economic valuation can then estimate selected benefits, including avoided sediment removal or crop losses, while explaining uncertainty. Ecological and cultural importance cannot always be expressed in rupees; valuation should not imply that every ecosystem is replaceable.

These findings should guide restoration budgets and project selection. Where erosion threatens a reservoir and nearby farms, for example, agencies could finance slope protection, streamside vegetation and improved farming practices together. Monitoring water quality and sediment would help establish whether the investment protects both agricultural production and downstream water supplies.

Conservation costs and benefits must also be shared fairly. Upstream farmers may benefit from healthier soils while bearing costs for land management changes that also help downstream water users. Technical support or payments for verified stewardship could make participation affordable. Assessments can inform contributions from public agencies, utilities and businesses, with clear land rights and benefit-sharing arrangements.

Over time, natural capital accounts could track changes in these assets alongside economic indicators. The findings should inform budgets, land-use decisions and lending. Assessments deliver value when they lead to action and subsequent monitoring.

The Government should prioritise reliable water management from upper catchments to farms in its climate investment program. Rehabilitating a tank without protecting its catchment leaves it exposed to sedimentation. Programs should connect watershed restoration, tank cascades, irrigation scheduling, drainage and maintenance, with farmers involved in water allocation.

Soil protection belongs in the same package. Suitable contour measures, ground cover, organic matter management and appropriate nutrient use can protect productive land. Evidence on erosion, water stress and livelihoods should guide site selection. Success should reflect soil condition, water reliability and production stability, alongside hectares treated.

Solar-powered irrigation pumps need similar care. Cleaner energy can reduce operating costs, but cheaper pumping may encourage excessive groundwater extraction. Financing should include water budgets, extraction controls and equipment maintenance.

Forecasts must explain what expected rainfall and temperatures mean for planting, irrigation, fertiliser application and harvesting in each locality. Advice should arrive in time, in accessible languages, through trusted extension services and digital channels.

Funding should connect weather observations, soil information, field trials and farmer feedback to produce practical advice. Research on heat- and drought-tolerant varieties must connect with seed multiplication and distribution. Diversified cropping, homegardens and integrated crop–livestock systems deserve support where resources and markets are suitable. Public investment must cover extension staff and operating costs as well as equipment.

Insurance can complement these measures if farmers understand premiums, exclusions and payment rules. Weather-based products must be tested against actual losses, reducing the risk that a damaged crop receives no compensation because a distant gauge recorded different conditions.

Finance should support food production with fewer avoidable losses and lower environmental costs. Better fertiliser timing and placement, soil testing and integrated pest management need practical training and evidence of farm benefits. Suitable paddy water management can reduce methane emissions, provided irrigation is controllable and yields are safeguarded.

Investment should extend along the value chain. Appropriate drying, storage, packhouses, transport and efficient cooling can protect food quality and earnings. Projects need a clear climate benefit, sufficient produce and affordable operation. Otherwise, even a modern cold room may become an expensive idle asset.

Financing must match the investment. Grants and public budgets suit shared services such as climate information and catchment protection. Affordable loans, guarantees and private investment may suit viable storage, processing and renewable energy businesses. Smallholders should not face unaffordable debt for investments whose benefits extend across society.

The Government should develop an investment program, coordinated by the Ministry of Finance, linking agricultural resilience with natural capital protection. Each proposal needs a climate rationale, baseline, responsible institution, maintenance budget and measurable outcomes. Farmers, including women and tenants, should help design access arrangements. Implementing agencies should disclose expenditure, beneficiaries and results, provide a complaints process and commission independent evaluation. Universities can help establish baselines and assess outcomes.

Priority should be investments that farmers can sustain and public agencies can maintain. Connecting resilient farming with healthy watersheds and soils would give climate commitments practical meaning: more dependable harvests, secure livelihoods and reliable food supplies.

(The author is Senior Professor in Crop Science, Faculty of Agriculture, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka)
