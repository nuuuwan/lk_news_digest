Speaking at the 81st Session of the United Nations General Assembly, India’s External Affairs Minister Subrahmanyam Jaishankar warned that unresolved conflicts are undermining peace, obstructing progress, and penalising innocent nations, urging the UN to renew its purpose as an instrument of justice and lasting peace.  Jaishankar further observed that the inability to maintain peace is not just undermining security, but also threatening progress and retarding development. In a globalised world, the effects of war are felt worldwide; countries far from the theatre of conflict are penalised for no fault of their own. Those most impacted often have the least say. This Assembly, he stressed, must face up to the true state of affairs.”

Within the span of a single week, the 81st UN General Assembly session, three developments occurred that offer a revealing portrait of US power in 2026, A war against Iran that has visibly drawn down US military stockpiles, a negotiated security agreement over Greenland that expands access without annexation, and a reciprocal state visit by Xi Jinping conducted from a position that analysts describe as one of greater Chinese confidence than his last such visit a decade ago.  One reading of the through line is not US decline in any simple sense, but US overstretch a power still capable of shaping outcomes across multiple theatres simultaneously, but doing so at a cost that some of its own former officials now discuss openly.  For a small, trade-dependent, non-aligned state like Sri Lanka, this matters less as spectacle and more as a signal of how much weight to place on any single great power’s guarantees.

The conflict now known as the 2026 Iran War began on 28 February, when the United States and Israel jointly initiated strikes against Iran, following a year of Iranian economic contraction, a currency crisis, and a government crackdown on protesters reported to have cost tens of thousands of lives. Tehran has characterised the initial strikes as an unprovoked violation of its sovereignty and has asserted a right to respond in self-Defence. Seven months on, the war has expanded well past its original combatants.  Iran has responded through a network of allied armed groups and direct-action missile and drone strikes on Israel and on US bases in the region, a contested claim of authority over shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and, most recently, a Houthi attempt to strike Riyadh with a ballistic missile, the first such attack on the Saudi capital since the war began.  The Houthis have simultaneously opened a new front in Yemen’s civil war, displacing more than 100,000 people. In Lebanon, a fragile cessation of hostilities remains in place along the UN-recognised Blue Line, while Israeli forces continue to maintain positions and a self-declared buffer zone north of it, in an area where UNIFIL operates under its peacekeeping mandate under the peacekeeping mandate expanded in 2006, but that mandate is now in its final extension: the Security Council voted in August 2025, at Washington’s request, to renew it once more through 31 December 2026, after which a phased withdrawal begins, concluding with full handover of southern Lebanon’s security to the Lebanese Armed Forces by the end of 2027. The result is a peacekeeping presence scheduled to shrink as regional tensions rise, even as Israeli forces continue to maintain a buffer zone beyond the line a timing mismatch worth watching.

Former US Defence Secretary Leon Panetta has described the current moment as “dangerous,” stating his view that the United States may no longer have the capacity to deter a future conflict in the South China Sea while remaining committed in the Middle East; one former official’s assessment, not a settled fact. The figures cited alongside that assessment are notable: within under five months, reporting indicates the US had drawn down the majority of its Patriot interceptor stock, roughly 80 percent of its THAAD inventory, most of its PrSM (Precision Strike Missile) and ATACMS (Army Tactical Missile System) munitions, and close to half its global Tomahawk supply. The estimated military cost of the war has grown past $43.6 billion, and disruption to Red Sea and Hormuz shipping is a measurable factor in global trade and insurance costs, with knockon effects for import-dependent economies such as Sri Lanka’s.

Neither side has achieved its stated war aims. What the record does show is that sustaining high-intensity commitments across more than one theatre at once has practical limits, and the current conflict is testing them a consideration relevant to any US ally or partner whose planning assumes reliable, on-demand American backing, including in the Indo-Pacific and the Indian Ocean littoral.

On 19 September, Saudi air Defences intercepted a Houthi ballistic missile aimed at Riyadh the first such attack on the capital since hostilities escalated while reportedly intercepting additional strikes aimed at Bisha, a city in southwestern Saudi Arabia, in Asir Province, close to the Yemeni border, Taif a major city in Mecca Province, in the western highlands near Mecca. Farasan, and Yanbu a major port city on the Red Sea coast in Al Madinah Province, further north.  The missile’s approach disrupted flights at King Khalid International Airport and produced a visible plume of smoke near a fuel storage area; Saudi authorities reported no casualties.  The Houthis stated the strikes targeted “sensitive sites” in Riyadh and Saudi oil infrastructure a claim that remains independently unverified. Saudi Arabia has responded with intensified airstrikes on Houthiheld territory, the Houthi advance has continued.

This follows a pattern through 2026, a March strike hit Prince Sultan Air Base in Riyadh Province, a projectile killed two people in Al-Kharj the same month, and Saudi Arabia cut oil production by 20 percent after strikes on offshore facilities.  By July, the Houthis had declared a blockade of Saudi ports and Babel-Mandeb shipping and claimed strikes on Saudi tankers in the Red Sea, adding a second contested maritime corridor alongside Hormuz.

Riyadh’s response has included the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement with Turkey and Pakistan, publicly framed as a mutual-Defence arrangement; one analysis (IANS, 21 September) assessed it as largely symbolic, on the view that Pakistan is unlikely to commit forces to Saudi Arabia’s Defence. Separately, Washington signed a civilian nuclear cooperation agreement with Riyadh in July, and the Trump administration has pressed Saudi Arabia to join the Abraham Accords as a condition of that agreement’s full implementation a linkage between Saudi nuclear cooperation and normalization with Israel that comes as Saudi territory faces attacks from an Iran-aligned group opposed to that normalization.

For a state dependent on Gulf-transiting energy shipments, this combination of military exposure, diplomatic hedging, and deepening dependency on Washington is one of the more consequential variables for Sri Lanka’s energy security outlook over the coming year, given that Saudi and Emirati export capacity, more directly than Iranian output, shapes Sri Lanka’s crude and LNG import costs.

The Greenland settlement offers a contrasting case. Trump’s earlier push to acquire the island, including remarks not ruling out military force, prompted eighteen months of transatlantic concern, French troop deployments to the island, new European consular presence in Nuuk, and strain within NATO discussed at the July Ankara summit. That approach did not produce a territorial transfer.

What followed instead, signed at UN Headquarters on 22 September with Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenlandic Premier Jens-Frederik Nielsen, was a security agreement updating the 1951 US-Greenland Defence framework. It preserves Danish sovereignty and affirms Greenlandic selfdetermination in principle, while granting the US expanded military access including growth of the Pituffik Space Base (formerly known as Thule Air Base), a U.S. Space Force installation in northwestern Greenland, roughly 1,200 km north of the Arctic Circle the U.S. military’s northernmost base), and a guarantee that China and Russia will not station forces or make sensitive investments in Greenland’s mineral sector without US concurrence. Trump’s own description of “permanent control over security” overstates the agreement’s legal effect, according to independent analysis; sovereignty was not transferred. The practical effect reduced Arctic access for rival powers is nonetheless real.

For states negotiating basing, port, or investment-screening arrangements with great powers, including in South Asia and the Indian Ocean, the Greenland agreement is a useful reference point for what “sovereignty-preserving” arrangements concede in practice, and what they do not.

Ten days before Washington, Xi Jinping attended the 18th BRICS Summit in New Delhi (12–13 September), under India’s 2026 chairmanship, alongside Russian President Vladimir Putin, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, and UN Secretary-General António Guterres. The summit adopted the New Delhi Declaration, a 140-paragraph document covering global governance, ongoing conflicts, trade, energy, climate, and technology. The declaration did not name the United States explicitly, despite the Iran war and US tariffs affecting several BRICS members consistent with a sequencing in which Global South positions are stated collectively before bilateral engagement with Washington.

The summit also surfaced an unresolved point relevant to the Saudi analysis above.  India, as chair, described Saudi Arabia as a full BRICS member from January 2024, while Saudi Arabia continued to describe its own participation in terms of an invited country, sending its foreign minister rather than the crown prince. This is consistent with the broader pattern of Saudi hedging already noted military exposure, the Mecca pact, and Washington dependency now extended into multilateral diplomacy: Riyadh has not fully aligned with a bloc often read as counterweighting US influence, while maintaining relations with Beijing and Moscow.

For Sri Lanka, the takeaway is that its principal regional partner, India, is simultaneously positioning as a Global South convener and managing its own balance between China and Washington, a dynamic worth factoring into Colombo’s own planning, given Sri Lanka’s economic and geographic dependence on India.

Xi’s state visit to Washington (23–25 September) was his first to the US capital since 2015, and the third in-person meeting between Xi and Trump in eleven months, following Busan (October 2025) and Beijing (May 2026); two further meetings are scheduled before year’s end, at APEC in Shenzhen and the G20 in Miami. Analysts note that China’s position has changed structurally since Xi’s last Washington visit under President Obama: it now presents itself as a more direct technological competitor, citing narrowing gaps in AI development and citing the leverage demonstrated when Beijing threatened rare-earth export controls during the last major trade dispute. One assessment describes the relationship as now “more evenly matched” a change from earlier characterizations of unambiguous US economic primacy, though this remains a contested and disputed characterisation rather than a consensus view.

Concrete deliverables from the visit were limited to an agreement, reached 20 September, to establish a bilateral AI dialogue; reporting suggests both sides deliberately kept the summit’s scope narrow. This is consistent with a preference, on both sides, for managing the relationship rather than resolving the underlying trade, technology, and security disputes.

On energy and trade exposure, Hormuz and Red Sea disruptions affect Sri Lanka’s import bill and shipping insurance costs for as long as the Iran war continues without resolution a factor for near-term fiscal and energy planning, including BESS deployment timelines that reduce reliance on imported fuel.

On the reliability of great-power security guarantees, reported strain on US materiel stockpiles is a relevant consideration when weighing any single great power’s security assurances in regional planning, whether on Indian Ocean basing, port investment, or infrastructure financing. The Greenland agreement’s terms access and denial rather than full territorial control suggest that even close allies obtain partial, hedged concessions from Washington, and states with less structural leverage than Denmark may obtain correspondingly less favourable terms.

On the China relationship, Washington and Beijing managing their rivalry through scheduled summitry rather than confrontation is, on balance, a stabilizing factor for a state like Sri Lanka with commercial and strategic relationships with both powers, reducing without eliminating the risk of being pressed into a binary alignment, for as long as the current arrangement holds.

On Gulf shipping risk specifically: the Bab-el-Mandeb blockade and continuing Red Sea tanker strikes, alongside the direct missile threat reaching Riyadh, mean Sri Lanka faces risk across two separate maritime corridors Hormuz and Bab-el-Mandeb/Red Sea rather than a single point of exposure. This suggests treating Gulf shipping insurance and freight-rate volatility as a standing line item in fiscal projections rather than a one-off shock, and prioritising diversification of import routing and strategic reserves.

September 2026 does not, on the available evidence, signal the end of US primacy, but it does coincide with more open discussion including from former US officials of the limits of American overstretch. One implication for states navigating this period is less about which great power to align with, and more about how much strategic and economic redundancy to build, so that when overstretched powers cannot deliver on their commitments, the resulting friction does not become a crisis of their own. For Sri Lanka, this suggests a course that favors neither Washington nor Beijing exclusively: deeper energy self-sufficiency, diversified shipping and financing partners, and a foreign policy grounded in non-alignment as a practical strategy rather than a historical position. On present trajectories, a decisive resolution to the Iran war before 2027 appears unlikely; further incremental access agreements, rather than territorial changes, appear more probable in the Arctic and Indo-Pacific; and the US-China relationship appears likely to continue being managed through summitry even as underlying disputes remain unresolved, a pattern Sri Lanka should plan around for at least the next two to three years.
