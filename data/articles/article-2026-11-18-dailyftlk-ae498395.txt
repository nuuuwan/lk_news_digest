2027 Budget: Can the financial sector turn policy ambitions into economic growth?

Banks are expected to mobilise Rs. 174 billion in concessionary lending, but the real test will be whether that money generates productive investment, exports and sustainable employment without compromising financial stability

Sri Lanka’s 2027 Budget places considerable emphasis on investment, enterprise development, exports and the revival of the rural economy. Yet, behind many of these ambitions lies an institution whose contribution could determine whether the proposals translate into meaningful economic outcomes by the financial sector.

The banking and financial system is expected to play a much wider role than simply providing loans. It must mobilise domestic savings, direct capital towards productive activities, support businesses, facilitate international trade, manage financial risks and help channel private investment into the economy.

One of the most significant financial-sector initiatives in the Budget is the allocation of Rs. 67.687 billion for interest subsidies and refinancing, with the expectation of generating approximately Rs. 174 billion in concessionary lending through State and private banks.

This represents a substantial intervention in the credit market. The Government reported that the Rs. 54.136 billion allocated for similar purposes in 2026 supported Rs. 97.1 billion in lending. The higher target for 2027 therefore signals an intention to use the banking system more aggressively as an instrument of economic recovery and transformation.

However, the central question is not simply how much money banks will lend. It is whether that financing will create additional productive capacity, strengthen businesses, generate employment and improve the country’s foreign-exchange earnings.

The proposed concessionary lending programs cover a broad range of economic activities. These include the Re-MSME Plus program, additional financing for micro, small and medium-sized enterprises, loans for paddy millers, financing for women-headed households, rural enterprises, fisheries entrepreneurs and small-scale tourism operators.

The interest rates proposed for selected programs are significantly below prevailing market lending rates. The average weighted rate on new SME lending was 13.17% in September 2026, according to the Budget Economic and Fiscal Position Report. Against this background, concessionary rates of 3, 4 or 5% could provide meaningful relief to businesses facing high financing costs.

For many small enterprises, access to finance remains a fundamental constraint. Businesses may have viable products, established markets and opportunities for expansion but lack sufficient collateral, financial records or the credit history required by conventional lending models.

Banks therefore need to move beyond an approach that relies excessively on fixed-asset collateral. Cash-flow-based lending, sector-specific credit assessment and the use of reliable digital transaction records could help identify viable businesses that remain outside the formal credit system.

The effectiveness of these programs will also depend on the quality of the lending process. Subsidised interest rates should not become a reason to relax credit assessment. Banks must continue to evaluate repayment capacity, business viability and the intended use of funds.

A successful program should ultimately produce stronger enterprises and healthier loan portfolios, rather than merely higher disbursement figures.

The role of banks becomes even more important when the Budget’s financing initiatives are considered against Sri Lanka’s external-sector position.

During January to September 2026, merchandise exports amounted to approximately $10.75 billion, while imports reached $18.67 billion, resulting in a trade deficit of around $7.91 billion. Although workers’ remittances increased to $6.87 billion, tourism earnings declined by 9.2% to $2.25 billion.

These figures underline the importance of directing credit towards activities that strengthen the country’s capacity to earn foreign exchange.

Banks can support this objective through pre-shipment and post-shipment finance, export receivables financing, documentary trade instruments, supply-chain finance and foreign-exchange risk management.

The fisheries sector, agro-processing, food manufacturing, high-value manufacturing, logistics and tourism-related businesses offer opportunities for financing that can contribute to export earnings or reduce dependence on imported products.

Nevertheless, import financing should not be treated as inherently unproductive. Machinery, technology and intermediate goods can increase domestic production and future export capacity. The critical distinction is whether borrowed funds contribute to sustainable economic value.

The banking sector should therefore assess the economic purpose of financing rather than simply categorising transactions as import-related or export-related.

Although commercial banks are assigned a prominent role in the concessionary lending programs, Sri Lanka’s financial-sector contribution should not be confined to traditional bank lending.

Licensed finance companies and leasing institutions can provide financing for machinery, commercial vehicles, equipment, renewable-energy systems and other productive assets. These institutions can serve smaller businesses that may not always fit conventional banking products.

The insurance industry has an equally important role. Agricultural production, fisheries, tourism and infrastructure investment are exposed to weather-related events, natural disasters and business interruptions. Without appropriate insurance protection, a single adverse event can undermine borrowers’ repayment capacity and weaken financial institutions’ asset quality.

The Budget’s reference to public-private partnerships, including the proposed logistics centre at Colombo Port, also creates opportunities for project finance, infrastructure bonds, institutional investment and insurance services.

Commercial banks can arrange project financing and working capital, while insurers can manage construction and operational risks. Pension funds and other institutional investors could contribute longer-term capital through appropriately structured investment instruments.

A more diversified financial system would reduce excessive dependence on bank balance sheets and create additional sources of funding for long-term development.

The Budget’s proposed SME Listing Support Mechanism and the ‘A Share for Everyone’ initiative are positive steps towards broadening capital-market participation.

The Colombo Stock Exchange is expected to deploy Rs. 250 million, with a further Rs. 500 million proposed for these initiatives. The Budget Speech also notes that active stock-market traders represent only around 0.7% of the population above 18 years of age.

For growing businesses, equity capital can be an important alternative to borrowing. Unlike debt, equity financing does not impose fixed interest and principal repayment obligations. It can therefore provide a more suitable funding structure for businesses undertaking expansion or innovation with uncertain short-term cash flows.

However, encouraging SMEs to list requires more than financial incentives. Businesses need support in accounting, corporate governance, auditing, valuation, legal compliance and investor relations. Market liquidity and investor confidence are equally important.

The success of the initiative should consequently be measured not only by the number of companies listed, but also by the amount of new capital raised and the extent to which participating businesses become stronger and more transparent.

The Budget’s ambition to expand affordable credit must be considered alongside the prevailing macroeconomic environment.

The Fiscal Position Report records inflation of 8.3% in October 2026, while the Central Bank’s Overnight Policy Rate was increased by 100 basis points to 8.75% in May and maintained at that level in September. The average weighted new lending rate stood at 12.65% in September.

At the same time, private-sector credit expanded by 23.6% year-on-year by the end of September 2026, with outstanding credit reaching approximately Rs. 11.77 trillion.

This indicates that credit demand is already growing substantially. While that is encouraging for economic recovery, rapid credit expansion must be accompanied by strong underwriting, appropriate provisioning and effective portfolio monitoring.

There is also a potential tension between subsidised lending and the cost of funds. Banks must be confident that interest subsidies and refinancing arrangements will be funded and reimbursed on time. Otherwise, delayed payments could affect lending margins, liquidity planning and the willingness of institutions to participate.

Importantly, an interest subsidy should not automatically be interpreted as a Government guarantee against borrower default. The respective responsibilities of the Treasury and lending institutions must be clearly defined.

Another issue is the relationship between Government borrowing and private-sector financing. The Fiscal Position Report places gross public debt at approximately Rs. 32,977 billion at the end of June 2026. Treasury Bonds represented 83.8% of central government domestic debt at the end of August.

Banks remain important participants in the Government securities market. If public-sector financing requirements absorb a disproportionate share of available financial resources, the capacity to finance private investment could be constrained. Maintaining a sustainable balance between sovereign financing and productive private-sector credit will therefore remain important.

Despite the breadth of the proposed concessionary lending initiatives, several aspects of the program architecture require greater clarity.

The Budget identifies an overall expectation of Rs. 174 billion in concessionary lending. However, the individual program amounts described in the Speech add up to approximately Rs. 140 billion based on the stated figures. A program-by-program reconciliation would help explain the difference, including any existing allocations, overlaps or additional financing components.

More importantly, the published proposals would benefit from a consolidated framework setting out borrower eligibility, participating institutions, lending conditions, subsidy reimbursement arrangements, credit-risk allocation and performance-reporting requirements.

The distinction between additional lending and the relabelling of ordinary bank lending is also important. Public funds should generate financing that would not otherwise have been available on comparable terms.

Performance should therefore be assessed through indicators such as new businesses financed, additional investment generated, jobs created and sustained, export earnings, productivity improvements, repayment performance and the fiscal cost of each program.

The Budget’s own emphasis on accountable institutions, outcome indicators, cash-flow plans and public transparency provides a useful basis for such monitoring.

The need for financial resilience is also highlighted by the estimated Rs. 1,022.5 billion required for Cyclone Ditwah recovery and reconstruction over three years, including an additional expenditure requirement of approximately Rs. 260 billion in 2027. Such events demonstrate why climate-risk assessment, insurance coverage, business continuity and disaster-resilient lending must become integral parts of financial-sector planning.

Sri Lanka’s financial sector now faces an opportunity to become a more active partner in national economic transformation. But this requires a shift in emphasis from the volume of credit disbursed to the economic value created through that credit.

The Government should establish a consolidated concessionary-credit framework, publish clear subsidy and risk-sharing arrangements, and introduce a common reporting mechanism covering participating financial institutions. Independent evaluations after 12 and 24 months would help establish whether the programs are delivering genuine additionality.

Banks, meanwhile, should strengthen sector-specific lending strategies, expand cash-flow-based financing, support export-oriented enterprises and maintain rigorous risk management. Finance companies, leasing institutions, insurers and capital-market participants should also be recognised as complementary sources of finance and risk protection.

Ultimately, the 2027 Budget will not succeed merely because banks achieve a Rs. 174 billion lending target. Its success will depend on whether that financing creates productive businesses, improves competitiveness, generates sustainable employment, strengthens foreign-exchange earnings and remains financially sound.

The financial sector is not simply a channel through which budgetary policies are implemented. It is a strategic component of the country’s economic infrastructure. The challenge is to ensure that public resources, private capital and financial-sector expertise work together to turn the Budget’s policy ambitions into durable economic progress.
