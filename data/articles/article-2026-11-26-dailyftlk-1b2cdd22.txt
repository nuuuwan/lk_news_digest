Third perspective reveals awful truth of Budget 2027, Govt. strategy

As Niccolo Machiavelli observed, only those at the base can see what the apex is like. Only the ruled know what the rulers are like. Centuries later, Marxism elaborated the point philosophically and epistemologically. Capitalism can be understood as a totality only by those who view it, not necessarily from the lived personal experience, but from the standpoint and perspective of the working classes—the majority within the system, at the base

Whenever President AKD and his Cabinet colleagues scoff in Parliament and at public meetings at the notion of a debt burden/repayment problem, saying it is all under control, they simply aren’t telling the truth.

One of our most eminent economists, ANU’s Prof Emeritus Prema-chandra Athukorala blew the whistle, calling the repayment agreement “a sword of Damocles”.

‘…He attributed this to a restructuring that relied on maturity extensions and coupon (interest rate) cuts rather than haircuts, or write-downs, on International Sovereign Bonds (ISBs). Ghana, by contrast, secured a haircut of about 37% from its bondholders in 2024. Sri Lanka’s upfront debt service relief of about $ 3 billion could fall to $ 1.8 billion under the new macro-linked bonds if growth exceeds the IMF baseline, or rise to $ 4.3 billion if it falls short.

“Paradoxically, therefore, stronger-than-anticipated economic growth could itself increase the debt-servicing burden,” he said.

Annual debt service payments (principal and interest) have climbed from $ 2.8 billion in 2023 to $ 4.3 billion in 2024 and $ 5.1 billion in 2025, with the Government accounting for 47% of the 2025 total.

The debt service ratio, debt service as a share of exports, rose from 15.9% to 24.9% over the same period.

Prof. Athukorala said the ratio had already reached the pre-crisis level as repayment of past due interest (PDI) bonds began in 2025. It would rise further once ISB coupon payments resume after 2027.

“The central concern is that borrowing at such high interest rates would perpetuate the debt overhang, leaving a sword of Damocles hanging over the country’s economic future and risking a trajectory of recurrent debt crises similar to Argentina’s,” he said.

President AKD’s unprecedented, needless induction of the Chairman of the Ceylon Chamber of Commerce, which also contained ISB holders, as one of his two Economic advisors and a negotiator with the IMF and private foreign ISB holders, wasn’t smart.

I was amazed and amused to read Dr WA Wijewardena recommending Walt Rostow’s ‘The Stages of Economic Growth: An Anti-Communist Manifesto’, to President Anura Dissanayake, adding that when he was a student of economics in the 1960s, Rostow was a ‘hero’. (https://www.dailymirror.lk/business/Wijewardena-urges-AKD-to-follow-Rostows-Non-Communist-Manifesto-for-take-off/215-355071)

Among the best and brightest of Ceylonese economists of the 1960s, he wasn’t.

Though only a (precocious) boy, I got around quite a bit with my father and was in the room or restaurant when he was in conversation with his personal friends who were top economic thinkers and policy-makers: Gamani Corea, Godfrey Gunatilleke, Lal Jayawardena, Jayantha Kelegama, Nihal Kappagoda, David Loos to name a few. Their main concerns were Development (as distinct from mere ‘Growth’), greater autonomy for the Third World, and social welfare to maintain a decent, rising Quality of Life for the people. JK Galbraith and Raul Prebisch were among the giants in vogue at the time. Back issues of the MARGA journal would trace the intellectual lineage of the economic thinking of the day.

As for university students in the 1960s, they rebelled violently twice, clashing with the Police and the Army at Colombo and Peradeniya. I doubt my old friend, Economics student at Peradeniya in the mid-1960s, N. Shanmugaratnam, currently Professor Emeritus of Development Studies at a Norwegian university, ever regarded Rostow ‘a hero’.

The Ceylonese intelligentsia and university student community of the 1960s opposed America’s Cold War policy as emblematised by the Vietnam War. Rostow’s ‘Stages of Economic Growth’ was recognised as a counter-revolutionary, indeed counter-reform, manifesto for US policy in the Third World during the Cold War.

The 1960s paradigm was, quite rightly, not ‘Growth’ per se, but ‘Development’. The centre of intellectual inspiration for the English-speaking policy intelligentsia—French and Spanish speaking policy intellectuals were way ahead of the Commonwealth ones—was the Institute of Development Studies (IDS), Sussex. It was Dudley Seers, Michael Lipton and Richard Jolly who were most respected by Ceylonese economists.

When Asia had its economic miracle, LSE’s Prof Robert Wade was its most outstanding theoretical interpreter with ‘Governing the Market: Economic Theory and the Role of Government in East Asia’s Industrialisation’ (Princeton 1990). The East Asian miracle bore no resemblance to Walt Rostow’s  ‘Stages of Economic Growth’.

Nobel Prize winners Joe Stiglitz and Paul Krugman never mention Rostow’s book approvingly.

Budget 2027 gave rise to two expected sets of responses: on the one hand that of the Establishment, i.e., the President and Government, top corporates, neoliberal economists, and international investment ‘experts’, and on the other hand, the Parliamentary Opposition.

The economic policy elite which for the first time since 1948 overlaps with the actual economic elite, commended the President and Govt for the Budget, but urged expeditious movement along the same trajectory, to free or minimise exchange controls and liberalise labour and land laws. (https://www.ft.lk/top-story/Sri-Lanka-must-prove-recovery-is-more-than-a-cycle-Hosking/26-800322)

In 2017, under the Yahapalanaya UNP, the foreign exchange controls of 1950s were relaxed with no benefit to the country. Further relaxation to the point of near-absence of foreign exchange controls would facilitate rather than inhibit capital flight.

Opposition Leader Sajith Premadasa directed a withering critique at the President’s Budget and its social impacts.

However, the Opposition as a whole didn’t address and rule out some of the more drastic neoliberal measures lobbied for by the economic elite—such as a return to the international money markets, further liberalisation of exchange controls, and ‘liberalisation’ of labour and land laws. The parameters of the alternative economic policy of the Opposition are as yet unknown.

A Third Perspective from outside the economic and political establishments including the parliamentary Opposition, coming from ‘the Movement’, contains an intellectually serious, impressive analysis. The clearest scan of and sharpest scalpel dissecting the Budget was a substantive statement signed by a network of 38 trade unions and civic organisations, calling itself ‘Trade Unions and People’s Power!’. (2027 Budget: Red herring to neutralise public and relink Sri Lanka with new financial colonialism | Daily FT)

The necessary convergence of academically-trained intellect and organised wage-earners has  commenced. I gather that Dhanusha Pathirana, co-author of the book ‘A History of Underdevelopment and Political Economy of Inflation in Sri Lanka’ (Palgrave Macmillan, UK), is a member of the network and a principal author of this high-quality text.

The analysis contains a penetrating diagnosis and makes a logical, credible, extremely disturbing case.

Here are the key take-aways from the text, in its own words, reproduced as bullet points. All bullet-points in this column are direct excerpts.

The Government is deliberately underspending Budget allocations to create surpluses that would help Sri Lanka return to International Sovereign Bond (ISB) markets under the International Monetary Fund (IMF) program.

While Sri Lanka’s corporate sector benefits by maintaining enormous tax breaks… crumbs [are] thrown to the general public through indirect tax relief on a few essentials. Reducing taxes on sprats, dry fish and exercise books—alongside nominal salary increases for Government servants—will do nothing to halt the worsening living costs or reverse the steep cuts in essential spending mandated by the 2027 proposals.

Hyper-inflationary indirect taxes are set to escalate by Rs. 170 billion, while the increase in direct tax income will be negligible at Rs. 30 billion, worsening the ratio between the two components to around 80% from 78% this year.

Subsidies and transfers are slashed brutally by a staggering Rs. 100 billion (dropping from Rs. 1.57 trillion to Rs. 1.47 trillion)—showing total disregard for mass poverty at 16% according to the Ministry of Health and malnutrition among school children as high as 25% (UNICEF Sri Lanka).

Corporate income tax holidays, on the other hand, which cost a mammoth Rs. 190 billion in 2024/25 according to the Ministry of Finance Tax Expenditure Report, continue unabated.

85% of the burden of the expected tax revenue increase falls squarely on the general public.

The statement sets out the cumulative negative consequences of Budget 2027 policies.

Higher indirect taxes and soaring living costs also restricts productive investments. Higher living costs inevitably fuel wage demands, inflating production costs without raising real wages. This erodes the competitive power of domestic producers in both domestic and global markets, accelerating the flight of manufacturing investments, while driving speculative and foreign-exchange-draining capital into property and real estate and illicit capital flows to hedge against inflation.

‘Trade Unions and People’s Power’ recommend alternative policies which would bring greater benefits.

Shifting towards a higher direct-to-indirect tax ratio by revoking corporate income tax breaks and enforcing strict tax compliance would ease the cost of living, curb inflation, and lift families out of poverty and child malnutrition, while improving fiscal stability.

It would drive down interest rates and hence, Government debt-servicing costs, while stimulating real investments, creating a virtuous cycle that further expands direct tax revenue through rising aggregate incomes.

Yet, the current tax policy remains entirely geared towards speculative and commercial capital that prefers shifting assets and surpluses abroad or into real estate rather than expanding industry.

That the Government has eschewed these options reveals the socioeconomic interests that the AKD-JVP-NPP administration primarily serves.

Going beyond the parliamentary Opposition, the TU Statement argues that the current tax policy actually ‘locks in economic backwardness’ and ‘structural stagnation’.

Consequently, the tax structure locks in economic backwardness. This is underscored by Sri Lanka’s Economic Complexity Index rank remaining stagnant at 83rd globally, with its value trapped in negative territory (-0.045) since the inception of the index in the mid-1990s.

The 2027 Budget will ensure that this structural stagnation in the economy persists.

The ‘Movement’ statement then proceeds, as the parliamentary Opposition does not, to unmask what the Government is actually doing: the deliberate ‘manufacture’ of ‘artificial’ budget surpluses.

Essential public investments (excluding highways and expressways) are choked through relentless and deliberate underspending. This has become the trademark strategy of the current administration: neutralise public outrage through exaggerated spending figures during Budget speeches, and…brutally underspending budgetary allocations to manufacture artificial budget surpluses.

The current Government utilised only 8% of the funds allocated for Cyclone Ditwah victims by mid-2026, according to the World Bank. By overachieving revenue targets and underspending 2026 budgetary allocations by roughly Rs. 1 trillion compared to the proposed Rs. 7 trillion, the Government has manufactured a surplus of Rs. 22 billion on the back of neglected public services.

While the 2026 Budget allocated a combined public investment of Rs. 425 billion to healthcare (Rs. 120 billion), education (Rs. 99 billion), water supply and sanitation (Rs. 95 billion), public transport (Rs. 67 billion) and housing (Rs. 44 billion), the Mid-Year Fiscal Position Report shows that only Rs. 55 billion of these commitments were actually met in the first six months.

Out of the allocated sum of Rs. 33.6 billion for providing meals to school children (reduced to Rs. 33.5 billion in 2027), only Rs. 12.7 billion was utilised in the first half of 2026, even as child malnutrition across the country continues to rise.

The 38 organisations’ statement penetrates way deeper than the Parliamentary Opposition to uncover and name the very logic that runs through and holds together the Government’s economic strategy.

The primary goal of the current regime is to manufacture budget surpluses to appease international speculative finance and regain the capacity to issue new International Sovereign Bonds (ISBs) under IMF mandates.

The fifth and sixth IMF reviews under the External Fund Facility (EFF) for Sri Lanka clearly project $ 3 billion in new ISB borrowings for 2027 and 2028. Slashing Government spending and raising regressive taxes are manoeuvres designed solely to lower the risk premiums demanded by international finance capital.

Given Prof Prema-chandra Athukorala’s dire warning about ‘debt overhang’, this is suicidal.

…the regime’s alliance with a broadly unproductive, hyper-consumerist business elite prevents it from imposing exchange controls, halting illicit capital outflows to boost national savings destroyed through hyper-consumerism and resolving the balance of payments (BoP) crisis without resorting to speculative foreign financing through ISBs (rising BoP issues are indicated by the deficit in the current account recording over $ 400 million in the first nine months of 2026).

The JVP  and much of the NPP, consisting near-exclusively of the petty-bourgeoise in terms of origins, have indeed committed ‘class suicide’, but instead of being reborn with a proletarian consciousness and perspective, have been reborn with the consciousness and perspective of the  ‘broadly unproductive, hyper-consumerist business elite’…‘local and global speculative capital’. Given the lucid diagnostics of the 38-Trade Union statement, what are the political implications and conclusions that can be derived?

Firstly, the crisis will begin to mount, not in 2028-2029, but in 2027, intensifying in 2028-2029.

Secondly, the Government will turn openly repressive and authoritarian, if not dictatorial. The Trade Union statement gives a material explanation for the impending intrusive-coercive legislation:

‘The horrendously repressive Protection of the State from Terrorism Act (PSTA) and Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill (POCA) have been deliberately introduced by the NPP regime to stamp out future public dissent, silence opposing voices against the increasingly unbearable living costs and crumbling state of essential services...’

The JVP-NPP is erecting an autocratic politico-legal ‘superstructure’ to pre-empt/contain the social fallout of its project at the material ‘base’ or ‘substructure’, the economy.

Thirdly, there is no longer any excuse to whitewash the JVP-NPP as somehow better, more progressive than the mainstream Opposition parties consisting of those previously in Government. How can a social safety-net slashing/shrinking, poverty-proliferating JVP-NPP Government be favourably compared with, say, the Premadasa administration which implemented the Janasaviya program, built and assisted the building of houses, and gave free mid-day meals and free school uniforms to schoolchildren?

Fourthly, there is no assurance that the main Opposition parties will not proceed with the same strategy; there is no mainline left alternative as in France (Jean-Luc Melenchon) which could realistically aspire to Presidential or Prime Ministerial office in 2029; and it is vital to dislodge the present AKD-JVP-NPP Government peacefully as soon as possible.

Fifthly, therefore a strong leftwing counter-bloc of social movements which can lobby for progressive policies while a handbrake on retrogressive ones, positioned and operating alongside and within the mainstream Opposition parties (e.g., the DSA and US Democrats) or outside them, is an urgent existential imperative.
