Democracy: Bridge to power or commitment to values?

In politics, democracy is touted as the best form of governance. Regular elections give people the opportunity to choose their rulers and remove them when they fail to meet public expectations.

Be that as it may, democracy is riddled with weaknesses. Political parties often champion democratic rights when they are relegated to the opposition, only to become less enthusiastic about those principles once they assume office. The temptation to consolidate power at the expense of democratic values is a recurring feature of politics. Sri Lanka is no exception.

The ancient Greek philosopher, Plato warned that excessive freedom, coupled with a lack of discipline, could lead to disorder and ultimately create conditions in which a tyrant rises to power. In modern democracies, the danger is not confined to the rise of an individual tyrant. The electoral process itself can be manipulated through misinformation, populist rhetoric and the exploitation of public grievances. In today’s social media era, false or misleading information spreads rapidlyWhen political decisions are driven by misinformation rather than informed debate, democracy risks becoming a contest in which perception matters more than substance.

Manipulation of public opinion can also make it difficult for governments to take unpopular but necessary decisions in the national interest. Yet the greater danger arises when those who win elections begin to regard their mandate as an unrestricted licence to govern.

Sri Lanka’s post-war political experience illustrates this contradiction. In 2010, buoyed by the euphoria surrounding the military victory over the LTTE in 2009, Mahinda Rajapaksa secured a second presidential term with a substantial majority. Over the next five years, concerns about the concentration of executive power became a rallying point for the opposition. In 2015, opposition parties cobbled together an alliance and fielded Maithripala Sirisena, a former minister in the Rajapaksa government, as their common candidate. He successfully unseated Rajapaksa, campaigning on a platform of democratic reform.

However, the Yahapalana government made only marginal progress in fulfilling its promises of democratic transformation. The  draconian Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA)   was expected to be repealed. The task remained unfinished, while the Act’s provisions were also invoked against political opponents.

The Gotabaya Rajapaksa administration, elected in 2019, subsequently faced the COVID-19 pandemic and a devastating economic crisis. The Aragalaya protests eventually forced the President to leave office. The upheaval showed that electoral victories cannot indefinitely shield governments from public discontent. It also underlined the need for political leaders to remain accountable between elections, rather than relying solely on the mandate secured at the ballot box.

The National People’s Power (NPP), which championed democratic rights while in opposition, was at the forefront of demonstrations against the PTA. Now in office, however, its continued resort to the Act against individuals regarded as political opponents raises questions about the consistency of its commitment to reform. The prolonged detention of former Eastern Province Chief Minister Sivanesathurai Chandrakanthan is a case in point. The circumstances and legal grounds of individual detentions must be examined on their merits, but the broader question remains: can a law condemned by a party in opposition become acceptable merely because that party now exercises executive power?

Worse, it has introduced the Prevention of State from Terrorism Bill to be enacted in place of the PTA. It sounds even more draconian.

This is the danger of the tyranny of the majority. An overwhelming electoral mandate can tempt a government to treat dissent as an obstacle rather than an essential feature of democracy. The opposition has voiced fears that the NPP is moving towards one-party rule. Such claims should not be accepted uncritically; political opponents, too, have their own interests. But neither should allegations of democratic backsliding be dismissed simply because they come from the opposition.

British writer George Orwell explored the relationship between political power, misinformation and the distortion of truth. In his essay *Politics and the English Language*, he observed that political language could be used to make falsehoods appear truthful.

Elections provide the route to power, but democratic principles determine how that power should be exercised. The real test of a government is not how loudly it champions civil liberties while in opposition, but how committed  it is to protect  those rights.
