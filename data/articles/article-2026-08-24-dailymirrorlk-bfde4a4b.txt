JR, Judiciary and Political Elimination of Sirimavo

In August 1978, a Special Presidential Commission comprising Supreme Court judges Weeraratne and Sharvananda and High Court judge K.C.E. de Alwis was appointed to investigate events between May 1970 and July 1977, when Bandaranaike had been Prime Minister

Sri Lanka’s Jayewardene era contains a troubling chapter in which constitutional power and State machinery were deployed against rival Sirimavo Bandaranaike.

If Jayewardene was determined to remove Bandaranaike from political life, it was not because he regarded her as incapable. On the contrary, he had acknowledged her abilities and achievements. In 1972, while Opposition leader and Deputy Leader of the UNP, he even suggested that he might leave the party with supporters and join her government if the UNP rejected an invitation to participate. He publicly conceded that Bandaranaike had introduced more socialist reforms during her seven years as Prime Minister than previous leaders had achieved.

The remarks brought criticism within the UNP. In February 1972, its Working Committee appointed a five-member committee to inquire into his conduct, but nothing came of it. After Dudley Senanayake’s death in April 1973, Jayewardene became UNP leader.

His attitude towards Bandaranaike later changed decisively. He understood the electorate’s volatility and feared that, when the political pendulum eventually swung against his government, a charismatic Bandaranaike could become the opposition’s rallying point. The objective went beyond defeating her at an election: to make her legally incapable of contesting or campaigning for another opponent.

Jayewardene personally oversaw the programme. In February 1978, the Special Presidential Commissions Law No. 7 of 1978 was enacted. It empowered the President to appoint commissions to inquire into alleged political victimisation, misuse or abuse of power, corruption and fraud, and to recommend civic disability.

Article 81 of the 1978 Constitution provided that Parliament could impose civic disability for up to seven years and, on the recommendation of a Special Presidential Commission, expel a Member of Parliament by a two-thirds vote of the entire membership. With the government commanding such a majority, the constitutional requirement posed little practical obstacle.  In August 1978, a Special Presidential Commission comprising Supreme Court judges Weeraratne and Sharvananda and High Court judge K.C.E. de Alwis was appointed to investigate events between May 1970 and July 1977, when Bandaranaike had been Prime Minister.

The appointment was followed by an extraordinary restructuring of the Supreme Court. Twelve senior serving judges were removed from their previous positions; four were demoted to the Appeal Court and eight left out altogether. Weeraratne rose from eleventh to fifth in seniority and Sharvananda from fourteenth to sixth. De Alwis was elevated over 18 High Court judges to the Appeal Court. The changes caused concern in legal circles.

When the commission sought to investigate Bandaranaike, she challenged its jurisdiction before the Appeal Court. On November 9, 1978, the court ruled that the commission could not inquire into her 1970-77 administration because those events predated the law establishing the commission. A writ of prohibition followed.

The Special Presidential Commissions of Inquiry (Special Provisions) Act No. 4 of 1978 declared the court judgment null and void and gave retrospective effect to the allegations against Bandaranaike. A constitutional amendment passed on November 20 authorised Parliament to remove jurisdiction in specified categories from the Appeal Court and confer it on the Supreme Court, also retrospectively.

The message was clear: The courts would not obstruct his political programme.

There were, however, voices of opposition. During the constitutional debate on August 3, 1978, an SLFP speaker warned that the establishment of what was described as the “autocracy of JR Jayewardene” would be met by a struggle outside Parliament. The warning anticipated State power, arrests and draconian laws, but insisted that the SLFP would not be intimidated.

Resistance was also reported within government circles. S. Thondaman, Gamini Dissanayake and Ananda Tissa de Alwis spoke against the measures at a Cabinet meeting, while former Prime Minister Sir John Kotelawala, then 85, made personal representations to Jayewardene. Jayewardene’s fear of facing Bandaranaike at a future election was central to his calculations.

The confrontation reached its climax in 1980. In May, Sri Lanka signed the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, undertaking not to create offences retrospectively or impose retrospective penalties. Yet Bandaranaike was summoned before the Special Presidential Commission to answer retrospective allegations carrying retrospective consequences.

She appeared but refused to submit to its jurisdiction, arguing that in a parliamentary democracy a Prime Minister and Cabinet were collectively responsible to Parliament,  and ultimately to the people through a general election. The commission proceeded in her absence and, on September 25, 1980, found her guilty of “abuse and/or misuse of power.”

The Civil Rights Movement objected, arguing that a fact-finding commission should not impose disabilities where ordinary rules of evidence and procedure did not apply, the findings were final and no appeal was available.

On October 16, Parliament passed two resolutions. One deprived Bandaranaike of her civic rights for seven years; the other expelled her from Parliament. Election laws were then amended to prevent a person under such disability from speaking, canvassing or acting as an agent at parliamentary elections. Violations could attract imprisonment or a fine, while candidates at whose instance the disqualified person participated could themselves be disqualified. Similar amendments were made to the Presidential Election Act.

The restrictions were extraordinary. Previously, those barred from campaigning were principally persons convicted by a court of corrupt electoral practices. Critics saw the new provisions as legislation aimed at a person rather than a general principle.

The parliamentary episode was painful. Opposition Leader A. Amirthalingam argued that the voters had already given their verdict by returning Bandaranaike to Parliament and that Parliament had no right retrospectively to sit in judgment on that electoral decision. He described the creation of retrospective offences and punishment as inconsistent with fundamental rights and international human-rights principles.

Bandaranaike then delivered a brief farewell speech. As she left her seat amid indecent remarks and catcalls from government benches, the other six SLFP MPs boycotted the sitting. She was about to leave the chamber unaccompanied when Amirthalingam and Sivasithamparam rose and escorted her out, a striking display of parliamentary courtesy amid political humiliation. The campaign continued. In August 1981, Jayewardene used the Public Security Ordinance to take possession and control of the SLFP headquarters as preparations intensified for the elections expected in 1983.

Long afterwards, Buddhist monks led by the late Kosgoda Dhammawansa appealed to Jayewardene to restore Bandaranaike’s civic rights. He recalled his son’s detention during the 1971 insurgency. Asked whether his actions were revenge, he did not answer. Yet the strategy was larger than personal retaliation: it was aimed at preventing a powerful rival from returning to the political battlefield.

The episode raises a question beyond Bandaranaike or Jayewardene. When constitutional amendments are used to neutralise judicial decisions, when retrospective laws are employed against political opponents, and when electoral law is tailored to silence an individual, the damage can reach far beyond one political contest. It can weaken confidence in the Judiciary, Parliament and the constitutional order itself.

“Unjust, unfair, and revengeful” actions can ultimately boomerang on those who wield power. The old warning remains relevant: when the One Great Scorer writes against your name, he records not whether you won or lost, but how you played the game.
