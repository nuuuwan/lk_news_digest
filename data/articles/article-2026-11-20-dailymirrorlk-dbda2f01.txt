Lessons from   Shenzhen summit  for Sri Lanka

When Chinese President Xi Jinping, U.S. President Donald Trump, and Russian President Vladimir Putin convened in Shenzhen on the sidelines of the APEC summit, the optics alone signaled a profound message in global politics. For the first time in decades, the leaders of Washington, Beijing, and Moscow sat face-to-face not to ratify a universal rules-based charter, but to negotiate directly as supreme arbiters of a fractured world. For small nations watching from the periphery—none more acutely than Sri Lanka—this rare trilateral encounter is not merely diplomatic theater. It is a way of showing  how the international system actually works. It indicates what is in store for the world.

To decode the Shenzhen summit, one must look past the surface-level headlines and examine the competing lenses of international relations theory. At first glance, the meeting appears to be a triumph of realism. Realists argue that international politics is an anarchic struggle for power where states—especially great powers—act solely in their self-interest.

From a liberal perspective—which emphasizes interdependence, international institutions, and shared norms—the Shenzhen meeting exposes the erosion of multilateral guardrails. The post-1945 liberal order was built on the premise that trade and international law could take on great-power rivalry. Instead, we see institutions like the World Trade Organization and the United Nations bypassed in favor of ad-hoc “Boards of Trade” and bilateral tariff splits. However, liberalism is not dead; it is mutating. Even as Trump, Xi, and Putin disdain traditional multilateral constraints, the sheer fact that they must meet to manage economic entanglement, supply chains, and nuclear stability proves that deep interdependence still forces rivals to the negotiating table.

Constructivism, meanwhile, explains why the symbolism of Shenzhen matters just as much as any signed communique. Constructivists remind us that the international order is not fixed by material capabilities alone; it is shaped by shared ideas, identities, and narratives. By standing together as peers, the three leaders are actively constructing a new mental map of the globe: a concert of great powers dividing the world into tacit spheres of influence. When Washington embraces transactional nationalism, Beijing champions a new definition of strategic stability, and Moscow declares Europe in decline, they are rewriting the norms of legitimacy. They are signaling that sovereignty is absolute for the mighty, while conditional for everyone else.

For a small, strategically located island nation like Sri Lanka, the lessons from Shenzhen are urgent and existential. Situated astride the Indian Ocean’s busiest sea lanes, Sri Lanka cannot afford to indulge in ideological nostalgia for a vanished unipolar order, nor can it passively drift into the orbit of a single patron.

First, small states must abandon passive non-alignment in favor of active, principled hedging.  When great powers strike grand bargains over the heads of smaller nations, neutrality without strategy becomes vulnerability. Colombo’s recent calibrated balancing.

Second, domestic resilience is the ultimate foreign policy, particularly for a nation like Sri Lanka.  Realist power politics punishes internal fragility. Sri Lanka’s 2022 economic collapse demonstrated how swiftly debt distress translates into geopolitical leverage for external creditors. Sustaining macroeconomic stability, diversifying exports, and insulating national institutions from partisan capture are not merely domestic goals—they are the prerequisites for diplomatic autonomy.

Third, small states must remain fierce defenders of international law, even when great powers flout it.  Through a constructivist lens, small nations lack aircraft carriers, so their primary shield is the normative authority of treaties like the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). Sri Lanka must actively champion multilateralism in regional forums, ensuring that maritime rules and debt-restructuring frameworks are governed by transparent standards rather than backroom coercion. In fact, it should regain its role played in the past under the successive governments.

The Shenzhen trilateral summit confirms that the world has entered an era of unapologetic great-power bargaining. For Sri Lanka, the imperative is clear: build domestic resilience according to a well concerted plan.
