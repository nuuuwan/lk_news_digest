Price of Looking Powerful: Trump’s Uneasy Summit in Shenzhen

Donald Trump came back from Shenzhen calling his meeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin “historic”. In one way, he was right. The leaders of the United States, China and Russia had never before sat around the same table. The photographs were striking, the meaning was plain, and the chance for talks was significant. Yet photographs do not make history, and a meeting is not a success just because everyone was in the room.

The more important question is what, if anything, came out of it. On what we know so far, the answer is disappointing. There was no breakthrough on Ukraine, no real agreement on nuclear weapons and no announced settlement of the wider tensions over Iran. A declaration towards peace used familiar words about cooperation and calm, but offered little that can be checked against firm promises. For a president who put so much political weight on this meeting, the gain looks small.

That does not mean the summit was worthless. When three nuclear-armed countries are led by men with sharply different aims, keeping the lines of talk open is valuable in itself. A mistake can be more dangerous than a quarrel, and talks often begin with conversations that settle nothing straight away. But opening a door is one thing. Letting others walk through it carrying gains for themselves is another.

Trump’s visit mattered even more because of his troubles at home after a hard midterm election. An election loss does not automatically weaken America abroad; the country is still a giant in wealth, military strength and technology. Even so, a president’s need for a clear win can affect how he bargains with other leaders. That may have given Xi Jinping an opening. By hosting the summit, he could show China as a centre of world diplomacy, able to bring Washington and Moscow together. Beijing did not need to settle every dispute. Showing that Washington could not run world affairs without China was a win in itself.

Putin wanted something different. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine has cost it heavily in lives and money, while Western governments have tried to shrink Moscow’s standing in the world. Yet there was Putin, seated beside the leaders of the two richest and strongest countries. The picture let the Kremlin say that Russia was neither alone nor stripped of its place among the great powers. The danger is mistaking being seen for achieving something. Putin may have gained respect without giving up much. If so, the result is troubling: Russia gets recognition while Ukraine keeps suffering from a war it did not start.

Europe has good reason to feel uneasy. While the Shenzhen meeting was going on, European leaders met in Berlin to discuss how to respond to a changing world. Their worry was not simply that Trump was talking to Putin. It was that Washington might strike a deal with Moscow and then expect Europe and Ukraine to live with the results.

According to reports of Trump’s call with European leaders, he described Putin as tired of the war and keen for a deal. That may be true. But hopeful talk should not be mistaken for proof that Russia has changed course. Putin’s public words gave little sign that Moscow has dropped its main demands. Indeed, his insistence that European officials must come to Moscow and win him over if they want a part in future talks hardly suggests a man ready to meet European worries.

Ukraine makes the stakes impossible to ignore. A deal forced through in the name of ending the war could reward aggression and set a dangerous example. If force lets a country grab land and win concessions, what comfort is left for smaller countries that rely on their borders being respected? Peace is essential, but a peace that makes the victim pay for the attacker’s ambitions may only delay the next war. Europe must avoid two mistakes: thinking every American move is harmful, and believing Washington will always defend European interests. The alliance across the Atlantic still matters, but it cannot rest on blind trust. European governments need a clear plan, armed forces they can count on and steady support for Ukraine. They cannot demand a seat at the table while expecting others to guard their interests.

Technology adds another side to Shenzhen. America’s lead over China goes beyond military and economic size to include advanced computer chips, artificial intelligence and key public networks. Limits on advanced American chips are therefore not just trade rules; they are tools of national security.

Any decision to ease those limits needs close checking. If Washington lets China get technology that could strengthen its military or speed up its plans, it may be swapping a long-term edge for short-term profit or goodwill. But claims about Nvidia and American chip sales must be proven with care. Which products are involved? What rules have changed? What safeguards remain? These are questions for evidence, not slogans.

Trump’s defenders have a fair argument. Personal diplomacy can achieve what formal talks cannot. A president willing to speak straight to rivals may find openings that career diplomats, tied to fixed positions, cannot easily explore. Also, there is no public proof that Washington gave anything away at Shenzhen. Critics should not mistake an awkward photograph or a friendly remark for proof of surrender.

Yet defenders must answer a key question: what is the point of such talks if they bring no pressure on the other side, no concessions in return and no drop in danger? Personal friendships cannot replace national interests. Putin and Xi are seasoned political players, unlikely to put their own goals below their bond with Trump. Indeed, the more eagerly Trump seeks a triumph, the more carefully his counterparts may work out how to use that eagerness.

This is the central puzzle of the Shenzhen summit. Trump wants to show strength by bringing powerful leaders together. But looking influential is not the same as using influence. A president can hold the world’s attention and still leave a meeting with nothing of lasting value.

The weeks ahead will show what really happened. Did Ukraine receive real promises of protection? Were European governments truly included in talks that shape their future? Did Russia show willingness to compromise, rather than simply demand more? And did Washington keep its technology lead while dealing with China? These questions matter more than the words used to describe the summit. Words such as historic, successful and unprecedented come easily to politicians; real gains are harder to prove.

The real test of diplomacy is not who smiled, hosted or stood beside whom, but who gained, who gave way and what dangers were lessened. If Trump won talks without giving up American interests, he may have achieved something worthwhile. But if Xi and Putin gained respect, influence and a free hand while Washington got little beyond applause and photographs, the picture will look different. In Shenzhen, three leaders met, and the world watched. The final verdict will depend not on the show, but on the price America, Europe and Ukraine may pay for it.
