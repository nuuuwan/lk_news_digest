Welikada gallows ready if death penalty imposed

Colombo, Aug. 12 (Daily Mirror) - The Welikada Prison gallows remain ready for use if the government decides to resume executions, with two executioners currently attached to the prison and on standby, the Daily Mirror learns.

The disclosure comes amid renewed government attention on the possible reintroduction of the death penalty in Sri Lanka.

According to a prison official, the two executioners are attached to the Welikada Prison. While they are currently permitted to remain at their respective residences, they are receiving standard salaries and are on standby for duty. The identities of both individuals are being kept strictly confidential for their safety.

The Welikada Prison in Colombo currently holds Sri Lanka’s only structurally operational gallows. While a second set historically existed at the Bogambara Prison in Kandy, that facility has been closed, leaving Welikada as the sole location maintaining the execution infrastructure.

According to prison authorities, 806 inmates are currently on death row. Of them, 58 have been convicted in drug-related cases, comprising 52 males and six females. Prison authorities also said that one foreign national is also among the death row.

The continued readiness of the prison system has gained renewed significance following recent discussions on the possible reintroduction of capital punishment for drug-related offences. The issue was discussed at the “Ratama Ekata” National Operations Council meeting held on Monday (10) at the Presidential Secretariat under the chairmanship of President Anura Kumara Dissanayake.

Meanwhile, the existence of capital punishment in statutory books, though not implemented, also remains a hindrance when seeking the extradition of Sri Lankan organized criminals hiding in European countries.

The Daily Mirror learns that bringing suspects from overseas to Sri Lanka can indeed pose legal and logistical challenges due to several reasons, including international legal principles and extradition laws.

Many European countries have abolished the death penalty and consider it a violation of human rights. As a result, they are often hesitant to extradite individuals to countries that have capital punishment due to concerns about the possibility of the suspect facing the death penalty. Some treaties explicitly prohibit extradition if the requested country intends to impose the death penalty, while others require assurances from the requesting country that the death penalty will not be sought or imposed.

Sri Lanka has maintained a de facto moratorium on executions for nearly five decades, although courts continue to impose death sentences for serious offences, including murder and drug-related crimes. No execution has been carried out since 1976, with successive governments refraining from implementing death sentences.

The last judicial execution in Sri Lanka was carried out on June 23, 1976, at Welikada Prison, when convicted criminal Embilipitiye Chandradasa, also known as “Honda Papuwa”, was hanged.

The issue of resuming executions has been raised by successive governments, most notably during the administration of former President Maithripala Sirisena. In 2018 and 2019, Sirisena pushed for the death penalty to be implemented against convicted drug traffickers, citing the need for tougher measures against the illegal drug trade.

The move led to the recruitment of executioners and, in June 2019, death warrants were signed for four convicted drug traffickers.

However, the initiative faced massive domestic and international roadblocks. It fractured the government, drawing open opposition from then Prime Minister Ranil Wickremesinghe and other key lawmakers. Internationally, human rights organizations condemned the move, and the European Union warned that executing prisoners could cost Sri Lanka its lucrative GSP+ trade concessions. Ultimately, the Supreme Court of Sri Lanka intervened, issuing interim stay orders against the executions following numerous fundamental rights petitions, ensuring the historic moratorium remained unbroken.

As a result, the country’s long-standing moratorium remained intact, despite death sentences continuing to be handed down by the courts.
