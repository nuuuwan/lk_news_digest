Constitution: How Much Longer Can We Wait?

The question is no longer whether Sri Lanka needs constitutional reform. It is how much longer its people must wait for a constitutional order that genuinely reflects democracy, equality, human dignity, the rule of law and accountable government

One cannot look at Sri Lanka’s constitutional history without a profound sense of regret. Opportunities for meaningful constitutional reform have repeatedly appeared and been squandered. Governments have promised new constitutions; political leaders have spoken of reform; commissions have been appointed, and proposals have been produced. Yet the 1978 Constitution remains.

The reasons are depressingly familiar: political opportunism, indecisiveness, lack of political will and, above all, the failure to build consensus. Successive political leaders have too often behaved as politicians protecting their immediate interests rather than as statesmen thinking about the country they will leave behind. The result is that Sri Lanka has repeatedly made the same constitutional mistakes while expecting different outcomes.

The question is no longer whether Sri Lanka needs constitutional reform. It is how much longer its people must wait for a constitutional order that genuinely reflects democracy, equality, human dignity, the rule of law and accountable government.

The Founding Constitutional Principles and Values articulated by the Collective for Democracy and Rule of Law on the eve of the 2024 Presidential Election provide an important starting point: human dignity; ethnic, gender and social equality; constitutional supremacy; the rule of law; and, crucially, a rejection of the concentration of state power in one individual or institution. A new Constitution must also be capable of evolving. It must advance justice and equality, promote social justice and participatory democracy, and respond to changing social realities rather than become another rigid political monument.

But before drafting another constitutional text, Sri Lanka must confront a more uncomfortable truth: democracy itself is not enough to protect democracy. An election can tell us who will govern. It cannot guarantee how they will govern.

This is the lesson democracies repeatedly forget. When a government secures an overwhelming mandate, it’s understandable to say: let them govern. They have been elected, have the numbers, and have the people’s support.

Then comes the dangerous next step: perhaps they can be trusted with fewer checks and balances. That is precisely when constitutionalism matters most.

Sri Lanka’s own history should have cured us of the belief that large electoral or parliamentary majorities necessarily produce good government. Some of the greatest assaults on democratic institutions have occurred under governments possessing overwhelming parliamentary majorities.

The 1972 Constitution established a majoritarian constitutional order and abolished post-enactment judicial review. The 1978 Constitution fundamentally altered the balance of power by creating an extraordinarily powerful executive presidency. The 18th Amendment rolled back important safeguards introduced by the 17th Amendment. The 20th Amendment similarly weakened many of the checks restored by the 19th.

The pattern is unmistakable: when political power becomes concentrated, constitutional restraints become vulnerable. There is an equally important counter-example. The 17th, 19th and 21st Amendments, despite their differences and imperfections, were products of Parliaments in which governments did not possess the same kind of overwhelming supermajority and constitutional change required a greater degree of political consensus.

That is not an accident. It is a lesson. Majorities are necessary for democratic government. But unchecked majorities can become a danger to democratic government. This is where constitutionalism enters the picture. Democracy gives citizens the power to choose their rulers. Constitutionalism tells those rulers that they are not entitled to do whatever they wish once they have won an election.

It could weaken the courts, remove safeguards, undermine institutional checks or erode protections for minorities. And provided the prescribed legal procedures are followed, such actions could remain perfectly legal. But something can be legal and still be fundamentally wrong. Sri Lanka must stop treating limits on majority power as somehow anti-democratic. The purpose of constitutional restraints is precisely to make majority rule safe. Nowhere is this more urgent than with the executive presidency. For decades, Sri Lanka has lived with an extraordinary concentration of political power in one office. The executive presidency has weakened Parliament, distorted institutional accountability and disturbed the constitutional balance. It has encouraged the politics of personality rather than the politics of institutions. The answer cannot be another minor adjustment to the machinery of executive power.

Meaningful democratic consolidation requires structural change: a stronger Parliament, genuine collective responsibility, collegial decision-making and an executive structure in which power is shared rather than concentrated. That is why the abolition of the executive presidency must remain at the centre of serious constitutional reform.

Courts are pressured. Emergency powers become normal. Legislatures stop asking difficult questions. Media become partisan mouthpieces. Constitutional restraints are dismissed as obstacles to getting things done. Democracy can be hollowed out while elections continue to be held. Public opinion changes. Governments change. Political loyalties change. Institutions must therefore be strong enough to withstand those changes.

A Constitution that limits power on paper is worthless if there is no institution independent enough to enforce those limits. This is why an independent judiciary matters. Sri Lanka’s own courts have, at critical moments, demonstrated the importance of judicial independence. The Supreme Court’s Kalawana Determination and the decisions of the Court of Appeal and Supreme Court concerning the attempted dissolution of Parliament in 2018 stand as important examples of the judiciary asserting constitutional limits. But courts cannot defend democracy alone.

A resilient constitutional democracy requires a Parliament with genuine oversight powers rather than a rubber stamp; independent commissions protected from executive interference; a professional public service and police accountable to law rather than political patronage; a free and independent media capable of exposing abuses of power; an independent legal profession; and a vibrant civil society willing to challenge those in authority. There is one final dimension Sri Lanka cannot afford to ignore: power-sharing. A state that concentrates authority in one office is not only vulnerable to authoritarianism. It is also vulnerable to majoritarianism. In a deeply plural society, unchecked central power can become power exercised in the interests of the majority community at the expense of minorities.

Meaningful devolution and power-sharing are therefore not concessions to particular communities. They are constitutional safeguards, essential for national unity, equality and lasting peace.

Sri Lanka does not lack lessons. It lacks the political courage to act on them. The country has spent decades watching constitutional safeguards strengthened, weakened and strengthened again. It has experienced the consequences of excessive executive power, the dangers of overwhelming parliamentary majorities and the importance of independent institutions. The choice before Sri Lanka is therefore not simply between one Constitution and another. It is between a political culture in which winning an election is treated as permission to exercise unlimited power, and a constitutional culture in which every government, popular or unpopular, powerful or weak, remains subject to law.

Democracy gives us the right to choose who governs us. Constitutionalism ensures that those we choose cannot govern us without limits. Sri Lanka has learned this lesson repeatedly. The tragedy would be to learn it yet again and still refuse to act.
