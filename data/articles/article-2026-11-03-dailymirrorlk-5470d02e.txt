‘Please allow us to tell our stories’:  Theepachelvan on the struggles of Tamil writers

Only a novel can comprehensively record the deep roots of politics, the multifaceted contradictions of society, and the scars of history across time and space

The Human Rights Commission of Sri Lanka (HRCSL) issued a landmark ruling recently on the release of two books by the prominent Tamil writer B. Pradeepan—also known as Theepachelvan—which had been detained by the Customs for months. The commission stated that the detention of the books was a direct violation of the Tamil author’s Fundamental Rights. In the following conversation, Theepachelvan, a novelist and poet living in Kilinochchi, reflects on the grievances of Tamil-speaking writers against the backdrop of what he calls State Oppression and the marginalization of ethnic minorities.

QThe Human Rights Commission (HRC) recently recommended releasing your books detained by the Customs, yet they are still being held. Furthermore, another Tamil writer recently faced the same issue when copies of his novel were detained by Customs in Jaffna. How do you respond to these recent developments?

This is not just a problem of a single writer, nor is it the first time this issue has happened. It is part of a systematic suppression directed against Tamil books and Tamil writers. Whenever a book is written by Tamil writers like us, the State machinery approaches it with the prejudice that it should be against the State and the armed forces.

In this context, even though the Human Rights Commission recommended the immediate release of my books, the government has not released them.

However, the continuous confiscation of books by the Customs Department undermines the need for a strong change, approach, and solution, so that no book or writer has to face such oppression, harassment, and misery in the future.

QWe are aware that detaining books is not the first obstacle your freedom of expression has encountered. Can you recall previous incidents where you were questioned by the authorities about your creative work?

Surveillance and interrogations have been present ever since I started writing. Initially, I was threatened through indirect inquiries. Later, in 2024, the Terrorism Investigation Division in Kilinochchi conducted an inquiry based on whether the novel Paiyankaravathi (Terrorist) was written with the aim of reviving the Liberation Tigers of Tamil Eelam (LTTE). It was a matter of suspecting and questioning our stories and the characters within them. They inquired whether the characters appearing in the novel were real people and where they were. I informed the authorities that they were fictional characters created in writing based on actual real-life events. Although such interrogations created pressure on a writer, they also gave me the drive to keep writing. In fact, it was during the days when that investigation took place that I was writing my subsequent novel, Cyanide.

QYou and most Tamil-speaking writers publish your work in India, yet almost all literary awards in Colombo for the best Tamil literary works only consider books published in Sri Lanka. How do you interpret this issue?

Generally, I do not send my books for awards. Many Tamil writers from Sri Lanka publish their books in Tamil Nadu. We publish our books in Tamil Nadu because there are more Tamil readers there, the quality of printing is better, cost is relatively low and it is easier for books to reach readers worldwide from there. The correct approach is to award prizes by looking at the author’s country of origin, since Tamils from their ancestral lands of Sri Lanka, are considered as a transnational community, where we maintain active, continuous social, cultural, or political ties across national borders and all our creative work are based on our homeland.

QIn one of your previous media interviews, you mentioned an unstoppable compulsion to write through any hardship. Looking back, has writing been a form of healing for you? And what is the impact of war and state oppression on your creative work?

Just as I could never live in another country outside this land, I cannot live without writing. As Tamils, we have endured severe systemic oppression on our ancestral land. Writing is the medicine that soothes our pain; writing serves justice for our pain. Even today, we suffer from painful memories. Our future generations must live a life of self-respect and equality. We only pick up pens. We hold onto writing. Through writing fiction and non-fiction, we can tell our stories to the Sinhala people and to the world.

QYou have always spoken about the importance of bringing your work to Sinhala readers in the south. Given that you and other Tamil writers constantly face discrimination, do you still have hope that the Sinhala community will empathize with the grievances you address in your poetry and novels?

Surely, we must tell our stories to the Sinhala society. I believe that through our stories, they will certainly understand us. Literature possesses that magnificent power.

Sinhala readers who read my novel ‘Nadugal’ understood with great pain and emotional resonance the feelings of a Tamil mother and a Tamil brother yearning for the memory of a freedom fighter who died in the war. At the same time, ethnic discrimination, oppression, and threats still prevail. I still have strong hope that one day, the Sinhala people will fully understand and acknowledge the political struggle of the Tamil people.

QAs both a poet and a novelist, how would you describe these two mediums? Do you think poetry can be used more effectively than novels in political communication?

As both a poet and a novelist, I am compelled to handle both of these forms through their unique structures and impacts. Poetry is like a flash of lightning; it instantly awakens emotions and creates a deep impact in the reader’s heart. A novel is like a river; it is capable of detailing and patiently constructing the many layers of society, life, history, and human psychology.

Poems and novels are powerful in two different dimensions. Poetry serves as a voice against oppression and as a medium that instantly records the pain of the oppressed. Only a novel can comprehensively record the deep roots of politics, the multifaceted contradictions of society, and the scars of history across time and space. It is only through the form of a novel that we can describe across a broad canvas—through multiple characters—how human beings are shattered and how they survive in an oppressive society. The two are not alternatives to one another, but rather complement each other.

QLooking ahead, what can readers expect from your forthcoming book, and what future literary paths you are hoping to explore?

Right now, I am writing a novel titled Kuzhandhai Veeran (Child Hero). It is the story of a young boy who was severely affected by the war. Additionally, my short story collection titled Yaazh Sumandha Siruvan (The Boy Who Carried the Yaazh) is scheduled to be released at the end of this year. This collection also mostly features stories about women and children affected by the war.

However, to continue our writing, freedom of expression must be granted to Tamil writers without discrimination. The number of Tamil writers turning to creative writing has sharply decreased due to the war and oppression against us.

Today, even a Tamil writer who writes a book for school children is under intense surveillance by military intelligence. Shadow book bans and the surveillance of writers are the major factors damaging Tamil literature today.

Therefore, I would like to make a request to the Government and the authorities. Please allow us to remember our memories. Please allow us to tell our stories. Please allow the Sinhala readers to read our stories. They are certainly the stories that Sinhala people in the south and the global community need to know.
