Development banking needs tax relief and sharper aim to take off, says Bingumal Thewarathanthri

Banking taxes absorbing 50-60% of profits seen deterring capital into development finance

Fiscal incentives needed at outset, a stance at odds with IMF thinking

Tighter directed lending and targeted agri-finance urged, citing India’s model

Sri Lanka will struggle to draw fresh capital into development banking unless the state offers fiscal incentives to offset the taxes that swallow as much as 60 percent of the bank profits, a stance that cuts against the International Monetary Fund (IMF) thinking, Standard Chartered Bank Sri Lanka Chief Executive Officer Bingumal Thewarathanthri said.

Thewarathanthri, who is also Vice Chairperson of the Ceylon Chamber of Commerce, was speaking on a panel on Sri Lanka’s financial landscape, at the 49th Annual Meetings of the Association of Development Financing Institutions in Asia and the Pacific, hosted by DFCC Bank, in Colombo this week.

He framed the debate less around who should own a development bank than around why anyone would fund one.

“The reality is, why should anybody put money into a market where the banking tax is something like 50 to 60 percent of your profits?” he asked.

At that rate, an investor keeps between Rs.40 and Rs.50 of every Rs.100 earned before tax. Thewarathanthri contended that such a return would struggle to attract foreign capital, whether into a new development bank, a public-private partnership or a dedicated development-banking subsidiary of an existing lender. He was candid that his prescription sits uneasily with the country’s IMF programme.

“This is completely against the IMF thinking but the reality is that a lot of countries, when they started some of these, have given those,” he acknowledged.

He added that the tax rates are typically raised to normal banking levels as institutions and markets mature.

Sri Lanka’s SME base, by his count, stretches to more than 160,000 registered companies and upwards of half a million informal enterprises. That amounts to more than three informal businesses for every registered one, together employing an estimated 3.5 to 4 million people. Across the emerging markets, he noted, the SMEs account for more than half of GDP, making the case for nurturing the segment difficult to dispute.

However, he cautioned that capital alone would not fix a model whose past lending, in his view, followed the priorities of the funders rather than those of the economy.

“Our business strategy was always driven by the DFIs’ objectives. We have not looked at what the country needs, what are the country’s economic pillars,” he observed.

He stressed that where the state backs such institutions, it is the taxpayers’ money that must be steered into the right sectors.

Thewarathanthri stressed that the decisions on which sectors, segments, demographics and geographies to finance must be grounded in data, warning against the lending choices shaped by instinct or individual preference.

“It can’t be based on gut feeling; it can’t be based on someone’s thinking,” he told the gathering.

He went on to cite portfolio-based lending, digital platforms and tools such as IoT and blockchain as means of monitoring the borrowers without relying on constant physical visits.

Pointing out that several banks and non-bank financial institutions already extend collateral-free credit through programme-based loans and community cross-guarantees, he expressed confidence that the state-backed credit guarantee scheme was gaining traction, although he conceded he had no data on its take-up.

As per the available data, the National Credit Guarantee Institution, which commenced operations in January 2025, had issued 2,000 guarantees, facilitating over Rs.14 billion in SME loans by July this year, averaging roughly Rs.7 million per loan, while it expects to double its beneficiaries to 4,000 by end-2026.

Beyond sectoral choices, he called for financing for specialised crops to be ring-fenced to specific regions, arguing that such an approach would allow warehousing, collection and transport networks to be built around them. Loosely directed credit, he cautioned, tends to flood local markets with the same produce, leaving the farmers with harvests they struggle to sell.

“When there is funding available, people just get into it and grow mangoes or something like that. Then suddenly you realise you have no market for it,” he remarked, recalling the watermelons and mangoes sold at depressed prices in markets around Kurunegala and Bibile.

Meanwhile, he urged the Central Bank to take a firmer hand in directing credit. He set Sri Lanka’s 10 percent priority sector requirement against the Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) target of around 40 percent. The two figures are not strictly comparable.

The RBI’s 40 percent spans all priority sectors, whereas the 10 percent he cited is Sri Lanka’s mandatory allocation to agriculture, a requirement dating back to 2008. Even on a like-for-like basis, however, India’s agriculture sub-target of 18 percent is close to double Sri Lanka’s.

The banks in India that miss their targets, which he noted include global lenders such as Standard Chartered, must place the shortfall in designated funds such as the Rural Infrastructure Development Fund managed by NABARD.

“Those are good practices that have worked for India. We have to all agree, it’s 6, 7 percent growth; it has worked for India,” he pointed out.

Agriculture, he argued, is where directed finance could do the most good. He put the sector’s share of GDP at 6 to 7 percent and its share of the labour force at 27 percent. The official data point to a narrower, though still stark, gap.

As per the Department of Census and Statistics (DCS), agriculture accounted for 8.4 percent of GDP at the current prices in the second quarter of 2026, while employing 23.7 percent of the workforce or about 1.9 million people, in the first quarter. On those numbers, each farm worker produces barely a third of the output of the average worker. The sector also shrank 2.3 percent in the second quarter, even as the wider economy expanded 4.2 percent.

Raising agriculture’s contribution to 12 to 13 percent of GDP would, in his reckoning, lifts much of the farming population out of poverty. Many farmers, he noted, live on less than Rs.17,000 to Rs.18,000 a month per person. That range brackets the DCS official poverty line, which rose to Rs.17,679 per person per month in July 2026 or about Rs.590 a day.

He added that a quarter of Sri Lankans live in poverty and a further quarter are vulnerable, leaving half the population exposed.

Beyond incomes, he cast the sector as a question of national security in a multipolar world unsettled by the Middle East crisis. He recalled the wheat export curbs during the Russia-Ukraine war and India’s periodic bans on crop exports.

“Getting food security right is critical, forget the development and growth,” he remarked.

Such gains, he insisted, can come only through technology and mechanisation, from geo-fencing and climate projections to crop monitoring. These tools, he said, must be tailored to farms that typically span one to two acres and support several families.

He saw room for the banks to go well beyond the 10 percent now directed to agriculture, pointing to the export potential for coconut, cinnamon, fruit and organic produce in the nearby markets such as the UAE and Singapore.

His call for state incentives sat alongside a note of caution from fellow panellist and DFCC Bank Director and Chief Executive Officer Thimal Perera. Perera argued that any development bank must be run independently, free of political interference and able to stand on its own feet.

“Otherwise, it becomes a burden on the taxpayer at the end of the day, if the development banks are loss-making,” Perera warned.

He recalled that DFCC itself, Sri Lanka’s first development bank, pivoted to commercial banking after 60 years, largely because it could not secure long-term, low-cost funding. (NF)
