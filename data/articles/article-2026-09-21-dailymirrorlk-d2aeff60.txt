When common sense crosses a border

A Sri Lankan passport does not carry with it an exemption from the laws, customs or religious sensitivities of another country.

That basic truth appears to have been forgotten in the heated debate surrounding the case of Sri Lankan national Anojan, who is facing judicial proceedings in Saudi Arabia following a social media post reportedly deemed insulting to Islam and the Prophet Muhammad.

The case has caused concern in Sri Lanka. A citizen facing severe punishment in a foreign country deserves consular attention, legal assistance and diplomatic efforts that can lawfully be made on his behalf. On September 18, the Foreign Ministry said the Sri Lankan Embassy in Riyadh was monitoring developments, coordinating with the Saudi Embassy in Colombo and engaging Saudi authorities.

But there is another uncomfortable conversation Sri Lanka must have.

When Sri Lankans leave the country to seek employment in Saudi Arabia, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, Kuwait or elsewhere in the Middle East, they are not entering an extension of Sri Lankan territory. They are entering sovereign countries with their own laws, cultures, religious traditions and red lines.

Anyone travelling overseas for work should make an effort to understand the destination country. That includes knowing what can be said publicly, what can be posted on social media and what conduct may carry criminal consequences.

Saudi Arabia is not a country where criticism or ridicule of Islam can be treated as ordinary free expression. International reporting on Saudi law has documented restrictions on expression considered offensive to Islam and the Prophet Muhammad, including criminal penalties for certain online content. Saudi Arabia’s Anti-Cybercrime Law also provides penalties for certain forms of online defamation and harmful content.

The world is full of countries where citizens and foreign workers are expected to respect local laws. A person cannot travel to another jurisdiction, deliberately or carelessly cross a legal boundary, and then expect Colombo to somehow erase the consequences.

Nor should this become a contest in which social media keyboard warriors accuse the Foreign Ministry of abandoning a citizen before establishing the facts.

The Ministry has not officially confirmed that a death sentence has been imposed on Sivarasa. It said that his case is being closely followed and that appropriate steps are being taken. Reports have said that his lawyer informed the Sri Lankan Embassy that the punishment had reportedly been enhanced to capital punishment following an appeal. Those details need to be established through the legal and diplomatic channels rather than through social media outrage.

There is, however, a legitimate question for the Foreign Ministry: whether Sri Lankan migrant workers receive adequate pre-departure information about the laws and social sensitivities of their destination countries.

Sri Lanka sends hundreds of thousands of people overseas for employment. These workers contribute greatly to the national economy. They deserve proper training before departure, including clear warnings about local laws, social media use, religious sensitivities, employment contracts and avenues for seeking help.

The tragedy of a Sri Lankan worker getting into serious legal trouble abroad should not be used to suggest that the host country has no right to enforce its laws. Nor should it be used to suggest that every consequence is automatically the responsibility of the Sri Lankan Government.

There is a difference between defending a citizen’s rights through diplomatic and legal channels and defending the conduct that brought a case before a foreign court.

A Sri Lankan working in a Muslim-majority country cannot reasonably assume that religious insults posted online will be treated in exactly the same way as they might be at home. The same principle applies in reverse: foreigners working in Sri Lanka are expected to respect Sri Lankan law and the country’s religious and cultural sensitivities.

The Sivarasa case should therefore prompt two conversations, not one. Sri Lanka must examine whether its migrant workers receive sufficient information before departure. And Sri Lankans themselves must accept that freedom carries responsibility, when exercising it abroad.

That is not a lesson for the Foreign Ministry alone. It is a lesson in common sense for Sri Lankans travelling abroad.
