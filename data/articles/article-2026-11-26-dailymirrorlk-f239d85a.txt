An uncertain ruling party and a confused opposition

But now, when Namal Rajapaksa is brought to court and taken back to prison, only a few supporters show up at the court premises (DM photo)

There is no sign that the government will take action soon to conduct the provincial council elections. The argument of the government leaders is that the law has to be changed to hold the election.

True, the United National Party (UNP) led government in 2017 has created an impasse preventing the provincial council election from being held without amending the Provincial Council Elections Act.

The government appointed a parliamentary select committee last February for this purpose, yet it has not come out with any proposal acceptable to all political parties. Not only the government, but the opposition parties also do not seem to be in a hurry regarding this matter.

However, conducting the elections is the responsibility of the government. Therefore, a perception is growing that the government is not interested in holding the election in fear of a possible defeat. That cannot be completely rejected either. Similarly, if the government is in fact afraid to face the election, it is not without justification.

Despite coming to power by securing the largest ever vote share in history during the 2024 general election, the current ruling party, the National People’s Power (NPP), lost two million of those votes in the local government elections held within six months. That would have prompted the leaders of the NPP to be cautious. Their strategy now may be to create a situation favourable to them through welfare measures before holding the election.  However, despite the NPP’s LG election setback, people do not seem to be rallying around the opposition parties either. They did not participate in large numbers in the protests organised by those parties against the government for the past 18 months. No consistency was also observed in those protests. Despite the uncertainty in public support, the government also did not seem to take the Opposition activities very seriously.

The prime reason for the setback suffered by the NPP during the last local government elections was the non-fulfillment of the excessive expectations that the people had about the party. For several decades, the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP), and since 2019, the NPP, heavily criticised the ruling parties and created a perception in people’s minds that the problems faced by the public could be solved very easily.

However, no matter how honest a government is, a country’s problems cannot be solved so easily. Many thought that a revolutionary change would take place in the country as soon as the NPP came to power. They thought that fuel prices and electricity tariffs would drop significantly, the cost of living would decrease, and the country’s development would take place dynamically.

Yet, even if the leaders wish for it, in a country that has faced an unprecedented economic crisis, reached bankruptcy, and is surviving on an International Monetary Fund (IMF) bailout package, such a massive change is not possible in a short period. The government has failed to bring about many administrative changes that people desired to see under an NPP rule mainly because of the negative mindset of the officialdom. Hence, in spite of the considerable change in the political culture, the overall picture remained unchanged and that led to the major setback of the NPP in the local government elections held in May last year.

Yet, the NPP still remains the strongest single party in the country. Ordinary people do not seem to be blaming the government for the recent increase in fuel prices and the resulting hike in prices of certain other goods. It is not clear whether they have realised that the conflict in the West Asian region is mainly to blame for it.

Even without such an economic situation, the United National Party-led government that came to power in the 2015 election was rejected by the people very soon. It can be attributed to a systematic campaign against that government by the opposition. Within six weeks of the Maithripala Sirisena – Ranil Wickremesinghe government coming to power, an opposition movement named “Mahinda Sulanga” (Mahinda Wind), launched under the leadership of Mahinda Rajapaksa loyalists, Wimal Weerawansa and Udaya Gammanpila, advanced successfully.

Within a short period of time, that protest wave transformed into one with a nationalist/racist tone. The attempts by the Maithri–Ranil government to enact a new constitution and the willingness of the then-government to co-sponsor resolutions passed in the UN Human Rights Council (UNHRC) on wartime human rights violations in Sri Lanka facilitated this.

It was a consistent series of anti-government protests. “Mahinda Sulanga” meetings continued to take place in several parts of the country. A massive campaign was carried out alleging that a conspiracy was underway to grant a federal system of governance to the Tamils through the proposed new constitution. It was also stated in these meetings that a conspiracy had been hatched to imprison military leaders through courts consisting of foreign judges instituted under the UNHRC resolutions.

Finally, three years later, the Sri Lanka Podujana Peramuna (SLPP) led by Mahinda Rajapaksa captured power in majority of local authorities at the elections held in February 2018. That victory became a trendsetter and turned into a massive wave, facilitating Gotabaya Rajapaksa to become President in the 2019 presidential election. In the 2020 general election, the SLPP won 145 seats.

However, the current opposition parties do not seem to be gaining such growing public support. The leadership of the SLPP is facing multiple court cases involving high profile corruption. Initially, when Namal Rajapaksa and Gotabaya Rajapaksa were summoned for investigations, huge crowds gathered in front of the Bribery or Corruption Investigation Commission. But now, when Namal is brought to court and taken back to prison, only a few supporters show up at the court premises.

The main opposition party, the Samagi Jana Balawegaya (SJB), does not seem to want to join hands with other opposition parties to launch any struggle against the government. The leaders of that party seem to think that other parties will gain political mileage from their party’s strength. At the same time, SJB is not a fighting force like the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna or Wimal Weerawansa’s National Freedom Front.

The issues chosen by the current opposition parties to fight against the government also do not provoke public interest. Some issues they centered their protests around are completely incomprehensible to the general public and they hardly felt their relevance.

People did not bother much about the protest they staged against the government’s education reforms. Later, the opposition parties were only able to point out a link to an inappropriate website in the Year-6 English textbook as a flaw of the whole project. When the government postponed the reforms for Year-6 due to that error, the SJB who until then demonised the entire reform project said that they did not ask to postpone it. The people could not understand what they were really saying.

The people did not understand the Opposition’s protest against the 22nd Amendment to the Constitution either. Therefore, their struggle did not receive much public support. Hence, it appears that just as the ruling party currently looks at the future with anxiety, the opposition parties are also confused as to what to do and what to demand to win over the people.
