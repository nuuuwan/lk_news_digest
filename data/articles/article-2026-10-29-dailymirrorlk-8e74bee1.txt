Today is the International Day of Care and Support Who Cares? Why  Sri Lanka Cannot Leave Care to Families Alone

For generations, care for children, the elderly, those with disabilities, and those living with chronic illness has been treated as a family responsibility

An ageing parent has a fall. A hospital visit must be arranged, medicines collected, and someone found to help at home. The daughter is at work, the son lives overseas, and the other parent is elderly too. Who steps in? For many Sri Lankan families, the issue is rarely a lack of love or willingness. It is a shortage of time, money, energy and sometimes the skills needed to provide care.

For generations, care for children, the elderly, those with disabilities, and those living with chronic illness has been treated as a family responsibility. This has kept essential work largely out of sight, even when it requires someone - mostly women - to give up earnings, rest or opportunities of their own. The 2017 Time Use Survey conducted by the Department of Census and Statistics highlighted the extent of women’s contribution: 86.4% of women undertook unpaid domestic services and care work compared with 54% of men, while the national Labour Force Survey data records 58.6% of economically inactive women citing housework as their main reason for not participating in the labour force.

But what happens when the care required becomes more than families can manage? This question is becoming more urgent. Sri Lanka’s 2024 Census found that 18% of the population is aged 60 or above. This share is projected to grow, and the number of older people relative to those of working age will increase. Recent migration trends and changing household arrangements have also left fewer family members available to provide care.

The need for assistance is already visible to the public. In a nationally representative public opinion survey Verité Research conducted in July 2026, 48.4% of respondents said they were aware of households needing government or community support to care adequately for older people. While this does not measure how many households need help, it suggests that the pressures of providing care are visible beyond the families directly experiencing them.

Families will always have a central role in providing care, but expecting them to meet growing needs alone is not sustainable. Care must become a shared responsibility, with the government playing three essential roles: providing services where they are needed, enabling others to deliver safe and reliable care, and ensuring that financial constraints do not prevent families from accessing support. These responsibilities are complementary. Providing services alone will not be sufficient if there are too few trained workers, just as expanding private services will achieve little if families cannot afford them. Such an environment of shared responsibility will give families somewhere to turn when their own capacity is stretched, while allowing those who wish to provide care themselves the capacity to continue doing so.

As a provider, the government must ensure that essential care services are available, particularly where families have few alternatives. Community-based elder day centres, for example, offer participants physical and social activities, opportunities for mental stimulation, preventive health screenings and referrals. This contributes to the continued independence of participants while giving families more flexibility during the day. These centres also help older people remain connected to their communities and identify health concerns early, before more intensive care becomes necessary.

As a facilitator, the government must create the conditions for communities, cooperatives, businesses and other providers to deliver care that families can trust. This requires clear and enforceable standards for quality and safety, recognised qualifications, coordinated training and appropriate labour protections developed in consultation with workers. Private providers can help expand services and create employment opportunities, but they need a skilled workforce. An International Labour Organization study projected a shortfall of approximately 110,544 long-term care workers by 2027, based on a benchmark of five workers for every 100 people aged 65 and above. Addressing this gap requires more than increasing training opportunities. Care work must become a viable and respected profession within Sri Lanka, with fair working conditions and opportunities for career progression. Care workers cannot be expected to provide dignity to others while being denied it themselves.

As a financier, the government must ensure that care is not accessible only to families who can afford to pay for it. Even where services are provided by businesses or community organisations, affordability can remain a significant barrier. Public investment will therefore be necessary to establish services where they are lacking. Targeted subsidies can help families who would otherwise be unable to access services, as seen in an intervention piloted by UN Women in Bangladesh among women factory workers and partners. This does not mean that everyone receives the same level of financial support. Families with greater means can contribute more, allowing public resources to be directed towards those who need them most. The objective should be to make reliable care accessible across income groups, rather than allowing a family’s financial circumstances to determine whether adequate support is available.

Sri Lanka now has an opportunity to make these shared responsibilities a reality, through the recently approved National Action Plan on Social Care (2026–2035). While approval is a starting point, delivering results will require clear institutional ownership, coordination across stakeholders, budget allocations, and accountability. It requires a realistic approach to implementation. It also requires us to consider how to reshape social norms within and across institutions through dialoguing with all stakeholders, including government, care workers, the private sector and communities, such that we can meaningfully focus on where and how these would need to change.

On this International Day of Care and Support, Sri Lanka must take steps towards recognising the importance of care and building a system that provides meaningful support when needed. This way, when the next family faces a care crisis, the answer to “Who steps in?” should not depend solely on which family member is available but on what options they can choose from.

Nadhiya Najab is a Lead Analyst for the Socio-Economics team at Verité Research.
