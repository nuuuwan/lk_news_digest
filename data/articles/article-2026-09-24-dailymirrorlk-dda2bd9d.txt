Why are ministers  still complaining about bureaucratic bottlenecks?

Former Elections Commissioner Mahinda Deshapriya warned of the damage the ‘deep state’ could do if it is not kept in check

The public grants a mandate to the government during elections to execute its policies and programmes. No official has the right to subvert or act against this popular democratic mandate

The Deputy Minister of Transport and Highways, Dr. Prasanna Gunasena, has echoed the complaints made some time ago by Ministers K.D. Lalkantha and Sunil Handunnetti regarding the bureaucracy’s lack of cooperation with government programmes.

In December 2024 – right after the landslide victory of the National People’s Power (NPP) at the Parliamentary election -- Minister Lalkantha accused these state officials of creating bottlenecks to implementing the government’s policies and plans.

Minister Handunnetthi remarked that officials who grew accustomed to following the corrupt policies and practices of previous regimes are hesitant to abandon them. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake also warned state officials during a speech at a meeting in April last year, saying, “Change your practices, or else we will change them.”

And now, Dr. Gunasena has stated during a recent rally that although the (NPP) obtained government power through the 2024 Presidential and Parliamentary elections, it has not yet fully acquired state power. He made these remarks while responding to criticisms from some quarters that the government’s performance is inadequate.

It is obvious that the policies and laws formulated by any government must be implemented through  state officials. A majority of the officials now serving within Sri Lanka’s state bureaucracy were appointed by previous governments. Many of those holding senior positions politically assisted the parties that ruled the country in the past,  and secured those posts as political appointees. Consequently, they may not favour the NPP government.

Some opposition politicians, who  remained silent when Lalkantha and Handunnetti complained about bureaucratic road blocks, now claim that Deputy Minister Prasanna Gunasena’s remark on “capturing” state power indicates a drift toward authoritarianism and an attempt to establish one-party rule. They argue that state officials must be allowed to function independently.

However, in a practical sense, the Deputy Minister’s assertion appears valid. Despite many factors contributing to the voters’ choice of political parties at elections, it is widely accepted that the public votes a party into power based on its policies and programmes. Therefore, once that party takes office, it is obligated to fulfill the policies, promises, and plans it presented to the electorate.

This can only be achieved through the officials operating within the administrative machinery. Therefore, the independence of state officials is relative. No matter what their personal political affiliations are,  or which past regime appointed them, officials must take all possible measures to execute the policies and plans of the incumbent government.

Hence, there is a clear justification when a new government takes office and appoints new officials to top positions within state corporations and institutions. Even if these appear to be political appointments, there is nothing wrong with appointing trustworthy officials to senior posts to execute people’s mandate. One cannot implement government’s policies by leaving officials who oppose the administration in those critical roles. Yet, the possibility of this view leading to nepotism and cronyism is also not to be ruled out.

It remains highly doubtful whether there were enough senior officials within the public service who supported the NPP to fill top institutional posts when they assumed office. At the same time, because the NPP government enforces anti-corruption policies, a large segment of top bureaucrats within the state machinery might dislike the current administration. Therefore, it is not difficult to comprehend why they might create hurdles to stall government plans.

This is likely what Ministers Lalkantha, Handunnetthi, and Deputy Minister Prasanna Gunasena are referring to. Furthermore, Prasanna Gunasena pointed out that if the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP) had come to power through the insurrections of 1971 or 1988, this problem would not have arisen. This is also true: if a party captures power through an armed revolution or a rebellion, bureaucrats are unlikely to push back.

The public grants a mandate to the government during elections to execute its policies and programmes. No official has the right to subvert or act against this popular democratic mandate.

At the same time, if officials genuinely perceive that certain government actions could severely harm the nation or its people, channels should exist for them to raise their concerns directly with the President or relevant Ministers.

However, in the past, neither did ministers come forward to point out the shortcomings of presidents, nor did bureaucrats step up to critique the flaws of presidents or ministers. When Gotabaya Rajapaksa, upon taking office, decided to slash taxes equivalent to one-third of state revenues, no one opposed it. Even former minister Bandula Gunawardane, hailed as an economic expert, justified and defended the move.

Similarly, neither the ruling party ministers nor MPs representing agrarian rural electorates opposed Gotabaya’s chemical fertiliser ban, which was implemented without a scientific feasibility study. Although they were fully aware of the catastrophic consequences, they justified it out of fear of losing their positions. Because power and corruption were deeply intertwined, they preferred their current portfolios over the future of the nation’s children.

On the other hand, certain past leaders managed to bully and control not only the state machinery but the judiciary as well. Public officials were regularly coerced into carrying out illegal or inappropriate acts. President Ranil Wickremesinghe publicly threatening the officials who hesitated to convert the historic Nuwara Eliya post office into a hotel is a classic example.

For this reason, conflicts between the bureaucratic apparatus and political leadership seldom occurred in the past. But now, the situation has changed. Opportunities for corruption within the state machinery are shrinking. Against this backdrop, officials might cause friction, delays, and complications in executing government plans. It is then that the ministers complain that they lack real state power. Former Election Commission Chairman Mahinda Deshapriya highlighted the danger of this dynamic on his Facebook page last year.

Deshapriya noted in that post that if the ‘deep state’ is not kept in check, what happened to the governments of Patrice Lumumba (Congo), Sukarno (Indonesia), and Salvador Allende (Chile) will happen here as well. All of these progressive leaders were ousted from power through internal armed coups.

This is precisely what Deshapriya was warning against. However, the fact that ministers are complaining about bureaucratic bottlenecks even two years after assuming power suggests that something is fundamentally missing within the government.

Political leadership guiding the state machinery to execute its lawful policies cannot be framed as an infringement on the independence of the public service. In fact, they must direct it. However, inducing or forcing state officials to engage in unlawful activities can never be accepted. That is precisely where the boundary of a public servant’s independence lies.
