PST and POC:  A recipe for abuse in their current form

This article is not meant to be yet another run-of-the-mill civil society protest over two bills: Protection of the State from Terrorism (PST), submitted to Parliament on October 6,  and Prevention of Organised Crimes (POC), submitted on October 20. The PST  is intended to replace the existing draconian Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA), and POC is presented as an Act to give effect to the UN Convention against Transnational Organised Crime.

There is no gainsaying that Sri Lanka needs special criminal laws to combat terrorism and organised crime. The latter should be focused on the local underworld nexus with class A narcotics trafficking, which is the most consequential form of organised criminality in Sri Lanka.

Crime scene at the Colombo Magistrate’s Court where Ganemulle Sanjeewa was gunned down (Pic courtesy: Tamil Guardian)

Existing legal provisions provide inadequate prosecutorial space to combat the local underworld or fight terrorism if the PTA is to be repealed without a replacement bill. The lacuna of the existing penal code provisions in fighting organised crime was on display when a large number of high-profile underworld kingpins fled the country immediately after being granted bail.  Of twenty-five underworld criminals, held in Bossa detention centre and subsequently released on bail, 16 have already fled the country. The underworld has money to splash, and the majority of the escaped underworld members were duly represented by  President’s Counsels.

Among the high-profile figures are Mohammad Najim Mohammad Imaran alias Kanjipani Imran, who fled the country five days after being granted bail on December 20k 2022,  Madushanka de Silva alias Podi Lassie, Ganemulle Sanjeewa, who was later gunned down after being repatriated, Kawuma and Army Sampath.

Therefore, the proposed maximum detention period of up to one year and severe bail restrictions are measures to address this particular lacuna in the existing legal provisions. However, while objective conditions that demand such measures are understandable, the absence of judicial oversight coupled with potential abuse of the law could lead to serious rights violations. What is required is not scrapping the detention period or  bail conditions, but creating space for magisterial discretion, including due emphasis on the potential flight risk posed by high-profile underworld members.

Many civil society groups have opposed both bills. However, the question lies not in the necessity for either of the proposed Acts, but that they are overblown and a recipe for abuse. The POC has lumped together every imaginary offence, sufficiently covered under other penal code provisions. And the PST has introduced an overly broad and ambiguous definition of an act of terrorism, which could criminalise legitimate public protests and demonstrations.

The Bill states a terrorism offence as: Any person who intentionally or knowingly commits any act which causes a consequence specified in sub-section (2), with the purpose of:  (A) Provoking a state of terror in the minds of the public or a group of persons or (B) unduly compelling a government or international organisation to do or to abstain from doing any act in order to advance a political or ideological objective, committing the offence of terrorism.

However, the broad interpretation of (B) risks any public demonstration being termed as an act of terrorism, though the act specifically states that the fact that a person engages in a protest or industrial action or a humanitarian organisation providing humanitarian or medical assistance does not itself provide a sufficient basis for such an act to constitute a terrorism offence. Yet, this overblown and ambiguous definition carries an enhanced risk of misuse.

It must, however, be noted that similar legislation in other countries also contains a broad definition of terrorism. Under Section 1 of the UK Terrorism Act 2000, terrorism is defined as the use or threat of action designed to influence a government/international organisation or intimidate the public, advancing a political, religious, racial, or ideological cause, involving serious violence, property damage, life endangerment, public health risks, or electronic disruption.

The United States Federal Statutory Definition of terrorism defines acts of terrorism as activities involving acts dangerous to human life violating U.S. or state criminal laws, intended to intimidate/coerce a civilian population, influence government policy, or affect conduct via mass destruction, assassination, or kidnapping primarily within U.S. jurisdiction.

All politics is local, so are laws. Any Act introduced in Sri Lanka should take into account the previous incidents of grotesque abuses of PTA, which saw, among many other abuses, the trumped-up charges against journalist Tissanayagam and  Lawyer Hajaaz Hizbullah, and resulted in the prolonged detention without trial of a number of individuals.

Similarly, the provisions that empower the military and coast guard to conduct searches and execute arrests without a warrant are a matter of concern, and should be exercised sparingly. Assuming they are only intended for extreme circumstances, they should also entail additional safeguards. However, as with the rest, one cannot rule out the prospect of such measures being normalised by a future government, as in the past.  In a separate note, reorienting the Police Special Task Force into a specialised counter-terrorism and organised crime fighting unit could overcome some of these limitations.

Also, given the past track record, the executive authority for detention up to one year of a terrorism suspect should be followed with a greater degree of judicial oversight. If the past is any guide, such leeway granted to enforcement authorities has cultivated a culture of detention, and much less helped proactive investigations.

The POC has in some ways caricatured itself into an absurdity. Its Schedule 1 lumps together 53 offences as diverse as discovery of antiques (treasure hunting), computer crimes, already covered by the Computer Crime Act of 2007,  prohibition of import of fishes, mammals, and birds, etc., clearing forest land, gambling advertisements and gambling with underage individuals, patent infringements, mining without licence, harbouring a military deserter and wearing the dress of a soldier, sailor or airman.

Similarly, Clause 4(2)(c) stipulates that style of dress, language, hand signs, or tattoos could be used to infer membership in a criminal gang. Despite the denial by the Justice Minister, there is no guarantee that such provisions could lead to grotesque abuses.

The absurdities in the POC are largely due to it having apparently been implanted from  El Salvador and Singapore. El Salvador has, until recently, been beset with organised transnational criminal gangs such as MS-13, which originated among Salvadoran migrant youth in Los Angeles in the 1980s, and Barrio 18 and many other small gangs. Those gangs possessed quasi-military capabilities and entrapped the state. It took Nayib Bukele, the president, to lock up 2 per cent of the adult population to rid the country of gangs.

Sri Lanka’s domestic conditions are different. Our gangs are convent nuns in comparison to their international counterparts. Two companies of STF or a specialised army unit could liquidate the entire criminal apparatus of Sri Lanka in six months, though that might create inconvenient headlines.  The POC should be reoriented to address the Sri Lankan conditions and Sri Lankan crimes, primarily drug trafficking. Instead, the bill is everywhere, which fails its purpose.

Sri Lanka needs both bills, but they should come with adequate safeguards and common sense.
