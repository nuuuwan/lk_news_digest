Proposed Organised Crimes Bill faces mounting challenges before Supreme Court

Colombo, Nov. 2 (Daily Mirror) - Special Determination petitions are mounting before the Supreme Court challenging the constitutionality of the proposed Prevention of Organised Crimes Bill, with petitioners raising concerns that several provisions could undermine fundamental rights, judicial oversight and media freedom.

Among these petitions, two journalists, Tharindu Uduwaragedara and Mohamed Fazeer, have filed another Special Determination petition seeking a declaration that several clauses of the proposed Bill are inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution.

Filing this petition through Gowry Shankari Law Associates, the petitioners contend that Clause 56 of the proposed Bill, which criminalises the disclosure of information likely to prejudice an investigation conducted or proposed to be conducted under the Act, could have serious implications for journalists reporting on matters of public interest.

They argue that although the provision contains certain protections for legal communications and a defence based on the absence of knowledge or reasonable grounds for suspicion, it does not expressly provide protection for public-interest reporting.

Accordingly, they contend that the broad scope of the provision could create uncertainty for journalists, family members and others seeking to disclose information concerning an investigation, and that it is inconsistent with Articles 12(1) and 14(1)(a) of the Constitution.

The petitioners have also challenged several other provisions of the Bill, arguing that they confer excessive powers on law enforcement authorities and executive officials while restricting judicial supervision over investigations and detention.

They contend that Clause 3, which defines serious crimes to include offences punishable by imprisonment of four years or more and offences listed in the First Schedule, is excessively broad and could result in the application of exceptional investigative and detention procedures to offences of varying degrees of seriousness.

Bill allows organised crime links to be inferred from dress, tattoos and associations

They further challenge Clause 4(2), which permits membership of an organised criminal group to be inferred from factors such as association with known members, dress, hand signs, language, tattoos, visits to certain premises and repeated arrests while in the company of such members.

The petitioners argue that these factors do not, by themselves, establish membership of an organised criminal group and could lead to arbitrary treatment.

They have also questioned Clause 17, which empowers police officers, on reasonable suspicion of a serious crime, to stop and search, question and arrest individuals, search premises without a warrant and seize relevant items.

With regard to detention, the petitioners challenge Clause 20, which permits a suspect to be held for up to 72 hours before being produced before a Magistrate, excluding journey time, with a further 24 hours of police custody if authorised.

They argue that the provision unduly restricts the Magistrate's judicial discretion by limiting the available options and preventing an independent determination of whether a suspect should instead be released on bail or discharged.

The petitioners have also challenged Clause 21, which empowers the Secretary to the Ministry responsible for Police to order the detention of a suspect for periods of up to two months at a time, for a maximum period of one year, to facilitate investigations, obtain evidence or question the suspect.

They contend that the provision allows an executive official, rather than a court, to determine whether a person should remain in detention for investigative purposes.

The petition further questions provisions relating to bail, arguing that Clause 23 restricts the granting of bail to the High Court in certain circumstances and permits suspects to remain in remand custody for up to one year before indictment.

Police empowers to obtain information from service providers without court order

Concerns have also been raised over Clause 44(4), which empowers a Deputy Inspector General of Police or a higher-ranking officer to obtain subscriber identity details, call records and tower-location information from service providers without obtaining prior authorisation from a Magistrate.

The petitioners argue that this could result in the surveillance of individuals who have no connection whatsoever with organised criminal activity, without adequate safeguards to protect their privacy.

They have also challenged provisions relating to access to encrypted services, interception of communications, the admissibility of statements and other forms of evidence, arguing that these could undermine procedural safeguards and the rights of accused persons.

The petitioners are seeking a determination that the proposed Bill, as presently constituted, or one or more of its provisions, is inconsistent with the Constitution and that it could be enacted only with the special majority required under Article 84(2) of the Constitution and approval by the People at a referendum to become law.

The petitioners are represented by Counsel Hejaaz Hizbullah with Shifan Mahroof, Shalini Roshana Fernando, Nehansa Weerasinghe, Uvindu Kuruwitabandara, Marshadha Mackie, Oshadhi Ganisha Hapuarachchi and Alagaratnam Jinoshan instructed by Tharmarajah Tharmaja.
