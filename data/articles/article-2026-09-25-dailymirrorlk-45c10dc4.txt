22nd Amendment to the Constitution: Arguments for and against

Colombo, Sep. 26 (Daily Mirror) - A central issue in the debate over Sri Lanka’s proposed 22nd Amendment to the Constitution has been the retirement age and tenure of Superior Court judges, with arguments focusing on judicial independence, the retention of experienced judges and the possible impact on delays in the administration of justice.

The Supreme Court has determined that the Bill does not require approval at a referendum and can be passed by a special majority in Parliament. During the hearings, the Government and Government-backed intervenient petitioners highlighted what they described as the potential benefits of the proposed changes, particularly the contribution of senior judges’ experience and expertise to judicial efficiency. However, the Bar Association of Sri Lanka (BASL) and several opposition parties raised concerns over the possible implications for judicial independence.

Against this backdrop, the Daily Mirror examines the principal arguments presented for and against the proposed 22nd Amendment during the Supreme Court proceedings.

The principal proposals of the 22nd Amendment to the Constitution are:

Increase the retirement age of Supreme Court judges to 67 years from 65.

Increase the retirement age of Court of Appeal judges to 65 years from 63.

Chief Justice would retire upon reaching 67 years or upon completion of six years from the date of appointment.

Six-year limitation on the term of office of Chief Justice would not apply to incumbent Chief Justice.

Increase the maximum number of other judges of the Court of Appeal from 19 to 24.

Extending retirement by two years would allow the judiciary to retain that experience rather than losing judges at 63 or 65.

The Government’s central argument is that Sri Lanka has a substantial backlog of cases.

Keeping experienced judges in service could provide additional judicial capacity while other reforms are implemented.

3. Increasing the number of Court of Appeal judges from 19 to 24 to provide additional capacity

4. Establishing sittings of the Court of Appeal outside Colombo to ease access to appellate justice

The Government has argued that the retirement age established decades ago should be reconsidered in light of increased life expectancy and changing social conditions.

BASL argues that judicial retirement provisions are connected with the independence of the Judiciary

It says extending the tenure of judges, particularly sitting judges, could affect public confidence in judicial independence.

BASL has described the proposal as a potential interference with judicial independence.

2. The connection between retirement age and reducing case delays is disputed

BASL argues that mere increase judges’ retirement age would not solve case backlog

Opponents argue that delays can also be addressed through: appointing additional judges, filling vacancies, increasing the number of courts, increasing court staff, improving infrastructure, digitalisation, and better case management.

Extending the tenure of sitting judges could raise questions concerning judicial independence and the constitutional position of judicial power.

4. Perception of an amendment designed to benefit particular individuals

BASL has highlighted the fact that the incumbent Chief Justice would be among those potentially affected by the proposed changes.

Opponents argue that the Judiciary must remain institutionally independent from the Executive and Legislature, and this is an attempt by the Executive to interfere with the Judiciary
