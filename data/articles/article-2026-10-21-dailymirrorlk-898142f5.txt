The Basis for Drafting a New Constitution: Independence of the Judiciary, Democratic Governance,   and Dismantling of the Executive Presidency

“We have had deficiencies, but we have had Democratic governance.”

In a Democracy you do not have a single leader, you do not have a legislature—if you forgive the Freudian slip—which trumps the other institutions.  You don’t have a legislature or an executive which exercise unlimited power or what is now called “Rajya Balaya”

Today there is so much confusion: what is Constitutionalism? What is a Constitution? Why do we need a Constitution? We must recognise that it has been civil society, including professionals in this country, who have always articulated a voice on issues of public concern. If you remember the time of COVID, we had the medical professionals articulating a voice because this was a matter of public concern. So the Bar Association too,  has expressed views  on public concerns. We have with us this evening  President’s Counsels Mr. M.A. Sumanthiran and Mr. Geoffrey Alagaratnam, and we will have  President’s Counsel Mr. Saliya Pieris who will join us later.

They and many other leading lawyers have always led from the front. And in that regard, in the case of the legal profession, inevitably there are areas of public concern that necessarily mean that they have to call government to account. When that happens, the Bar Association is required to   question government’s action or inaction including their policies.

Now, in the past, I don’t think it was as bad as today, because today we have such adversarial, confrontational politics that if by any chance—and an unlucky chance—the Opposition’s stand on a matter happens to coincide with that of professionals, then there’s a delegitimisation of the professionals. That  is wrong because professionals must have the courage to speak for their profession, even when they question the government. Constitutionalism and Constitution-making, to be functional, must have bipartisan consensus. When that is not there, even professionals who articulate a voice are seen as either anti-government, against government, or worse, lackeys of the government.

I think citizens should be aware of these realities, and therefore reflect on what it means to have a Constitution. What is a Constitution? A Constitution is, after all, the basic law of a country which will regulate the governance system. And to regulate a governance system, you have to have a framework for governance.  The framework for governance also then will have to have institutions of governance. That would be what you call the system of governance. Now, those institutions of governance also have to be embedded and linked with some values, and a values framework.

In the 20th and 21st centuries, we have basically two forms or frameworks of governance. One is Democracy. The other is what we call centralised power with perhaps a single leader. That kind of governance does not focus in the way that Democracy does on the Sovereignty of the people, the rights and liberties of citizens of the country. Therefore, we need to understand in Constitution-making: Where do we stand? Is it going to be for Democracy or is it going to be for another form of government? And I say this because today we’re constantly talking of the Democratic deficit, the failures of Democracy. Contrasted with that are countries which have strong leadership, single leadership, supposed to zoom our counties into economic growth and a wonderful future.

In that context, therefore, we need to ask ourselves: What is the value in Democracy? Now Democracy, we must understand, was born of an evolutionary framework developed from a time when there was single leadership by a king, a monarch.  Democracy didn’t come overnight. It had to dismantle monarchy and single leadership, and that’s what it did.

Historically,  we know that basic ideas on Democracy as a form of governance can be traced  to the Greek civilization and  time of what we call the Western Enlightenment.  It was the Western Enlightenment that brought together these ideas of the Sovereignty of the People, a social contract between government and the People who are governed, and the central importance of the people of a country. That is Democracy. That did not come overnight. It took centuries. It took ideologies. It took thought. It required struggle born also of blood and tears.

Today we dismiss Democracy and this history and say, “Oh, it is elitist liberalism.” But we must not forget that Democracy was born as an evolution from monarchy and single leadership, replacing this with the concept of government that respected the rights of the Sovereign people. The central importance of the Sovereign Peoples  Rights to be protected from abuse of state power is the foundation of democracy as a form of governance. The European countries and the UK engaged with that system of governance.

By 1948, when the UN Charter and the International instruments were  drafted, there were two different approaches to governance. One was the Western tradition  born of their experience evolving from monarchy to People’s rights, and People’s sovereignty that focused on civil liberties: Freedom from torture, freedom of speech, freedom of expression, freedom to protest—those are civil liberties. But the weakness was that there was no recognition of social and economic rights as the rights of the people: That was absent in that tradition.

On the other hand, there were  member states from the Communist countries, which had the centralised, authoritarian system of governance. They did not  recognise civil liberties, but focused on meeting all their people’s social and economic needs as rights: Health, education, shelter.

In 1948, we must remember that there were therefore two great international treaties or covenants:  one, a Covenant on Civil and Political rights, and another one  on Economic and Social rights.  That was the Cold War period. But then subsequently through the years, there were changes. In the 1990s, there was the flowering of the human rights tradition. Member states of the UN came together at the Vienna World Conference in 1993, which then recognised that ALL  these rights should be human rights of peoples across the globe.

So the Western liberal civil liberties tradition,  linked with that of the countries of the Global South like Malaysia, China, and Russia—which recognised and gave importance to social and economic rights. And then a consensus developed that ALL these rights are important; They are “indivisible.” We witnessed this in the 1990s, and the early years of this millennium  in the development of  many international Conventions which declared human rights on the basis of both civil liberties and social and economic  rights.

One would have thought that we would have had achieved a global consensus on the broadening of the meaning of Democracy and a  movement away from to this concept of strong leadership without civil liberties. But we have not had that.  In place of that, we have had a decline in multilateralism in the UN system, giving way to bilateralism, once again back to this focus on two systems: Democracy or strong single leadership.

In this global context, let us compare the situation in countries like ours, which experienced  decolonisation: countries which knew the impact of colonialism. What happened in those countries? These civil liberties the legacy of the colonial powers were used in freedom struggles. They were used to dismantle colonialism. That is why the idea that this liberal tradition of civil rights is a colonial white man’s sudda-law should be challenged. Because this is not so. These countries of South Asia and Africa internalised the values of Democracy, the systems of governance known as Democracy, and built for their nations Constitutions which recognised the importance of civil liberties and control of state power. And you see this in India, you see this in Nepal, you see this in Sri Lanka: We had the same concepts of the importance of civil liberties in Constitutionalism. But we focused on civil liberties. Social and  economic rights in the Vienna Convention were considered only as social policies, following the Western tradition on people’s rights.

And what is the meaning of Constitutionalism? The Sovereign people have given their consent to the rulers to rule subject to protection of THEIR rights. And that means that Constitutionalism must prevent the abuse of State and Government power.  Today sometimes we talk of authoritarian Constitutionalism, we talk of abusive Constitutionalism. That is  a contradiction. If Constitutionalism is to prevent abuse of state, government power, you can’t have authoritarian constitutionalism or abusive constitutionalism. What you can say is,  you can have abuse of constitution making to embed an authoritarian system of governance.

So we must now, therefore, recognise that even today, if we say there’s a Democratic deficit, this system of governance has been used in our countries to give a better quality of life to our people. There have been failures; there are dysfunctional areas, but that does not mean you have to reject something which has been a powerful force for recognising the importance of the citizens of a country.

I think today when we are challenged with all this talk on the deficit of democracy, the importance of single leadership and authoritarian governance to deliver development,  we must stay the course and say: “No, for Sri Lanka, from 1948 we have had Democratic governance.”

Governments  have not delivered in many areas—true—so the challenge is to focus on Constitutionalism in a new Constitution. Priority must be given to help government  to deliver on the gaps that we have, and ensure the well-being of our people. Constitutionalism must be the basis for drafting a new constitution.

I think we have to also challenge this often-repeated statement that we have a “78-year curse” in governance. We have had deficiencies, but we have had Democratic governance. Central to that has been regular elections and the resilience of the People. Also the contribution of the courts of this country, the lawyers of this country in ensuring that there has been some accountability in governance.

Some of you may not remember, but I have lived through dark times. I have witnessed the horror of the murder of a dear student, a politician whose head was cut off by the JVP—the Deshapremi at that time—and stuck on his gate. He had young children. I have gone with my husband in dark curfew streets to help clear a  body for burial, where the first person who opened the poll,   a relative was killed at the General  Election the JVP prohibited. .

So we in this country have lived through dark times. But we also have moved from that dark past to a point where those involved in an armed struggle,  the JVP, have come into the democratic political process.  They did so in 1994. Having come into that political process, the Democratic process, it is a commitment made to the people. We  must hold this government with its JVP partner to deliver on Democracy,  and not any other system of governance.

Let us look at our history of Constitutionalism and Constitution making. What is our history? 1948: we had the parliamentary system.  We had, particularly in relation to the minorities, decisions of the appeal courts which have been criticised, which did not take into account minority rights. So there were deficiencies.  But we had a written constitution, we had courts, we had public administration and law enforcement systems that functioned. That was the dividend of post-independence freedom and political autonomy in Sri Lanka.

Then in 1972: with a massive mandate, we had a government which again brought in the 1972 “autochthonous” or home grown constitution. But what did it do? In fact, it interpreted the Sovereignty of the People as the Sovereignty of Parliament and parliamentarians. And that made Democracy dysfunctional.  There is a recorded history of the abuse of power that took place at the time, including the conflict with the courts.

Now change was promised in the 1978 Constitution.  The people gave a massive mandate for a Dharmishta Aaanduwa. And then what happened ? Again, we put in place all the democratic institutions as we know them, the system of democracy familiar in the liberal tradition.  But a huge gap was the aggrandisement of power in the strong Executive Presidency, which, has led to decades of problems in the governance of this country.

The Executive Presidency of 1978 has destabilised what were good public institutions. It has contributed to an ethos where, in institutions, the person who is considered the boss is supposed to lead, and everybody else does not question him/her. That happens in the public sector today in many institutions, and I’m told (and it looks like it does) that sometimes this also happens in the private sector. So we have developed what you call a boss-man culture which seeps down to society, not just public institutions and governance. The Executive Presidency, therefore, has contributed to the weakening of many public institutions and also, to a large extent, a culture of corruption, and abuse of power.

This decline has been understood, and because it has been understood, since the year 2000 we have been asking for a new Constitution that will eliminate the Executive Presidency. Successive political leaders including the current President and their political parties have pledged in election manifestoes to do this. At least the Chandrika Bandaranaike government fulfilled that promise and brought such a constitution in 2000 to abolish the Executive Presidency and strengthen the democratic institutions to achieve accountable governance to the People.   Unfortunately, a subsequent and recent executive President and his party tore that Constitution  to shreds in Parliament. That was in 2000.

Then in 2015—again, and I think Mr. Sumanthiran P.C.  was part of this initiative—there was a whole bipartisan process for constitutional reform. Excellent reports were prepared by public servants and experts on the public service, on the judiciary, etc., etc. There were consultative arrangements that included civil society and citizens. But then that also came to nothing.

Mr. Ranil Wickremesinghe, I think, led that process of the Constitutional reform of 2015, which was supposed to have been completed but never was completed. Then surprisingly, when Mr. Wickremesinghe became the first unelected president, we witnessed another period of   authoritarian Executive Presidency.

The dismantling of the Executive Presidency has to be the bottom line, the baseline of constitutional reform. We do not want anything but that. We have to dismantle this governance approach. We have to return to a system of governance with an elected Prime Minister and an Opposition in Parliament. But the baseline is we cannot have amendment after amendment after amendment—amendments from 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021, and now 2022.

The time has come to say “no more amendments!” What we want is a new Constitution with the dismantling of the Executive Presidency. And for that, we want all-party consensus. We need to demand that. We must also demand this of the NPP, because this was their commitment to us the People. It was a commitment made in their election manifesto: Bringing a new constitution which would dismantle the executive Presidency.

We have an enormous wealth of information, reports, draft constitutions, documented materials that can be a rich resource, and are easily available. A small group of people can sit down and draft a good constitution using those resources, working in a consultative manner.  The voice of the people was heard during the 2015 consultative process. That material is available. New consultations must and can  address the key areas of concern for us.  Preparing a new constitution in this consultative manner is something which should not be postponed, because it is doable. It is not something that is going to take years because all the resources, the experience  for drafting a new Constitution. is available. All it needs is the political will to do so.

You can also have transitional provisions—transitional provisions where the current elected President becomes, for the period of the term of office, a Prime Minister who heads government. Those are possibilities which can and  must be put into place.

We must recall  that the JVP as a party came into the political process many years ago, and from 1994 and accepted a commitment to uphold Democracy. Over the years of the JVP members elected  to Parliament demonstrated that they understood Democracy as a system of governance. The 17th Amendment can be traced to the contribution of the JVP. They  were with the government at that time.

Subsequently, again, the JVP brought a 20th Amendment to repeal the Executive Presidency. Then again when there was a Constitutional crisis in 2018, the JVP participated in an all-party opposition consensus, and a court case was filed by all—the Sampanthan case. The case was decided in the Supreme Court and the constitutional crisis was resolved with the government respecting the decision of the Supreme Court. So that record must be maintained by this government and it must not be undermined.

My colleagues here, I think, will speak on the pillars of Constitutionalism and Democracy.  I just want to flag a few issues because we need to understand that any new Constitution has to address  all those key areas.

One is the franchise—we have enough experience and must not forget that we have addressed and eliminated electoral violence. Our recent elections have shown that our People want peaceful, violence free elections. But that franchise must also be combined with an effort to solve our national question, with effective power-sharing. The Constitution must reflect a commitment to understand that we respect diversity, pluralism and the long tradition of peaceful relations in our communities,  rejecting the extremism, the fundamentalism which cannot make us think as Sri Lankans who belong to one country. So that is key.

Then, of course, the separation of powers. We have been speaking of that recently with the 22nd Amendment. What does separation of powers mean? It means, in simple terms (to the young lawyers, all that you learned in your classroom), that in a Democracy you do not have a single leader, you do not have a legislature—if you forgive the Freudian slip—which trumps the other institutions.  You don’t have a legislature or an executive which exercise unlimited power or what is now called “Rajya Balaya”.

You have courts which ultimately are the arbiters of the Legislature and the Executive who can scrutinise the conduct of these institutions. This is what you mean by the core concept of an independent  judiciary that is empowered to protect citizens’ rights, prevents abuse of State power or rajya balaya, with freedom from interference by government.

Today on social media, ministers, members of Parliament ask, “What is independence of the judiciary? Nobody is telling us: what is independence of the judiciary?”  What is independence of the judiciary if not linked to the separation of powers of the three institutions of governance?  So how can we have a Minister of Justice, lawyers in Parliament, say, “What is this independence of the judiciary?”  Have they left their knowledge in the classroom? They as lawyers should know that the Independence of the judiciary has served this country well. It has done so over decades, and that is something which we have to ensure.

What are, then, the specificities of independence of the judiciary? Even in the 1978 Constitution, the Preamble says, “We, the people of this country, give to ourselves the Constitution, recognising our fundamental rights and the independence of the judiciary.” So this norm, it is central,  fundamental. The 1978 Constitution has separate provisions —and I am sure these distinguished lawyers have argued this — called  “Independence of the Judiciary”.

And those provisions are born of experience in Democracies. These are three basic norms:  One, you don’t change the age of retirement of sitting judges; two, you ensure that their salaries are paid from the Consolidated Fund—that is, you can change the salaries, but their payment has to be assured; and three, you have an impeachment procedure if you want to terminate the appointment of a judge.

So those are three basic norms on ensuring that the independence of the judiciary is recognised. This is why others with democratic traditions, like the regional organisation LawAsia, and Commonwealth lawyers have focused on these norms of democratic Constitutionalism. Because the judiciary is not like the public service. It is  not like other institutions. In governance called democracy with separation of powers, it is the one institution that monitors and ensures that the legislature and the executive in government respect the rights of the Sovereign People.

What about the other concepts that have been floating around again with much confusion? What is audi alteram partem? People are saying, “What is audi alteram partem?” “What is conflict of interest?”

I am  really surprised that there is such misunderstanding on the concept of conflict of interest and duty. When I was teaching in the university, if our students didn’t understand that concept, we would have been quite shocked. My colleagues understood what this meant, and we followed what we taught in the class room.  When we sat on boards, if we had that special interest in decision-making, what would we do? We would say, “My daughter is an applicant for this post. Therefore, there is a conflict of interest and duty. I will recuse myself. I will withdraw.” That was what we said and did. Colleagues did that. When we sat on Boards and Committees, colleagues followed these norms.

The idea is that you yourself may not decide in favour of your daughter who is  an applicant, is less important than the perception of your conduct. The perception is that you the mother, sat in the decision making seat,  and, therefore, she got the job. So perception is important. Now, sometimes we are told on social media, on TV—some people say—“You can’t go on these abstract ideas about what can happen. These are abstract ideas, they’re not concrete.” Of course perceptions are ideas and not concrete facts. We must avoid possible conflict of interest and duty, because public perception is that it will happen.

What is the legal norm?: Audi alteram partem—hearing the other side and the importance of doing so.  Is this idea something foreign or colonial? We have Buddhist monks this evening in the audience—what did the Buddha say? Freedom of thought and speech is fundamental. He said, “Do not accept what I have said. Do not accept what the sages have said. Listen, understand, and then believe.”  We have this norm of hearing the other side, not shouting each other down, in our own society, among our own citizens. Politicians often disregard this tradition which is embedded in the legal norm.

In conclusion, I want to say, for myself, what I would like to see in a new Constitution: The dismantling of the executive Presidency; clarity with regard to the Sovereignty of the people.

Today we have Article 3. Sovereignty is in the People, is inalienable, and includes government powers, fundamental rights, and the franchise. This article should be linked to Article 4,  exercise of sovereignty: the division of sovereign power across the legislature, the  executive and the  judiciary,  the three pillars. There is jurisprudence on this topic, but that has been  challenged. Article 3—Sovereignty—is then delinked from Article 4, which is the institutional framework of governance. I think we need clarity on this matter, and that is very important. Those clauses have to be drafted very carefully.

I think also we should have clarity on the referendum article. We need to address important issues on the balance of power between the legislature and the executive—that is an area of concern. Strengthening the Independence of the judiciary must be a priority. Provisions on human rights must incorporate the approach on the  indivisibility of fundamental rights, recognising civil liberties and socio-economic rights.

That requires a shift away from the idea that the latter are government’s social policies and not enforceable rights of a Sovereign People. A new Constitution must also address a current concern -- the immunity of a President from being sued, and his power to pardon persons for offences that they have been convicted in the courts.

(This is an edited version of the transcript of a talk given at a public seminar on ‘Why should we rally for a new people’s constitution?’, held on  September 11, 2026 at the National Library Auditorium and organised by the Young Lawyers’ Association. )
