Cancer drug reportedly sold for over Rs. 25,000 amid shortage

An essential medicine used to protect cancer patients from infections is reportedly being sold for more than Rs. 25,000 on the black market amid a severe shortage in the country, the Doctors’ Trade Union Alliance for Medical and Civil Rights said.

Trade Union Alliance Chairman and Specialist Dr Chamal Sanjeewa alleged that the medicine, available under brand names including Neup, Neupogen, GCadF and Filgrastim, normally costs around Rs. 900 but has become extremely expensive and difficult to obtain due to the shortage.

He said the medicine plays an important role in the treatment of cancer patients, including children, as well as those undergoing chemotherapy.

According to Dr Sanjeewa, the medicine is administered to stimulate the production of white blood cells and strengthen the body's immune system, thereby helping protect vulnerable patients from infections during cancer treatment.

He warned that patients who depend on the medicine are now facing serious difficulties in obtaining it, with some being forced to seek supplies through unofficial channels.

“Even at prices exceeding Rs. 25,000, the medicine is extremely difficult to find,” Dr Sanjeewa alleged.

According to him, the shortage was linked to a dispute over the acceptance of an imported consignment of the medicine. According to him, a large quantity of the drug has already been imported but remains in warehouses because the Medical Supplies Division has reportedly refused to accept the stock over an issue concerning the labelling of the medicine containers.

Dr Sanjeewa said nearly 100,000 units of the medicine were reportedly being held in warehouses without being released to the health system.

He alleged that the State Pharmaceuticals Corporation had previously granted the relevant company permission to import the medicine without the particular label, following instructions issued by the National Medicines Regulatory Authority regarding labelling requirements.

He argued that clearer instructions should have been provided to the importer at the initial stage and that the current situation could have been avoided.

The doctor warned that the issue was particularly serious because around 25,000 patients require the medicine every month. If the shortage continues, he said, more than 75,000 patients could face difficulties obtaining the medicine over the next three months.

He also alleged that several other categories of cancer medicines are currently in short supply in the country's health system, affecting both government hospitals and the private sector.

Dr Sanjeewa said the continued shortage of essential medicines was placing an additional financial burden on cancer patients and their families, particularly those who cannot afford to purchase medicines at highly inflated prices on the black market.

He urged the Ministry of Health, the National Cancer Institute, the National Institute of Blood Diseases and other relevant authorities to intervene immediately and resolve the dispute over the imported stock.

He stressed that administrative and regulatory issues should not be allowed to disrupt the supply of life-saving medicines to patients undergoing critical cancer treatment.

Dr Sanjeewa called for the existing stocks to be assessed and released as soon as possible, subject to all necessary safety and regulatory requirements, while urging authorities to establish a continuous supply of essential cancer medicines to prevent similar shortages in the future.
