‘No dogs left behind’ key to ending rabies: Vet

Colombo, September 18 (Daily Mirror) - Eradicating human rabies in Sri Lanka would be impossible without eliminating animal-mediated rabies, Veterinary Surgeon and Animal Welfare Coalition (AWC) Executive Member Dr. Chamith Nanayakkara said, challenging concerns raised by the Public Health Inspectors’ (PHI) Union over the proposed new rabies legislation.

Responding to claims that the proposed Elimination of Rabies and Registration of Dogs Bill could disrupt the country’s dog vaccination programme, Dr. Nanayakkara said Sri Lanka was approaching the end of the 73rd year of its national rabies eradication programme, which began in 1953, yet vaccination coverage remained around 50 per cent.

He said dog bites accounted for nearly 95 per cent of animal-mediated rabies cases and that 20 human deaths were recorded due to rabies in 2024.

Dr. Nanayakkara questioned why the national rabies eradication programme remained under the Health Ministry when vaccination coverage had failed to reach the 70 per cent threshold recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO).

He said the responsibility for controlling other zoonotic diseases remained with the Department of Animal Production and Health (DAPH), while effective control of the canine population was essential to maintaining adequate vaccination coverage.

According to him, the disruption of the national sterilisation programme during the past three years had further aggravated the problem.

He also criticised the expenditure on post-exposure treatment for people bitten by animals, saying nearly Rs. 1 billion in public funds was being spent on such treatment without sufficiently involving the nearly 700 DAPH veterinary surgeons stationed at regional veterinary offices to address the source of the problem.

Dr. Nanayakkara said the internationally recognised approach among rabies experts was to ensure uniform vaccination coverage nationwide, often described as “no dogs left behind.”

He argued that the Health Ministry lacked an adequate mechanism to procure and distribute vaccines, maintain cold chains, identify high-risk areas and provide emergency veterinary responses required for a comprehensive animal rabies control programme.

He said the proposed Bill’s requirement for veterinary supervision of vaccination programmes was intended to ensure the quality of vaccination and animal welfare.

“Having a veterinarian available in rare but possible cases of complications following vaccination also ensures the well-being of animals,” he said.

The Bill was gazetted on September 7 and seeks to establish a new legal framework covering rabies vaccination, dog registration, dog population control and commercial dog breeding. Cabinet had earlier approved the preparation and publication of the legislation.

Dr. Nanayakkara also disputed claims that the rabies elimination programme previously handed over to the DAPH in 2018 had to be taken back by the Health Ministry because of a lack of progress.

He described that account as a “half-truth”, claiming that the programme had been handed over without adequate arrangements and was reclaimed within six months.

He further alleged that the Health Ministry had not updated its database with vaccination and sterilisation figures from that period despite repeated reminders, while continuing to attribute shortcomings to the DAPH.

At the same time, Dr. Nanayakkara acknowledged the contribution of PHIs to rabies control at grassroots level.

“We gratefully acknowledge the invaluable service performed by the Public Health Inspectors at the grassroots level,” he said, stressing that his comments were not intended to diminish their contribution.

He said the proposed system was based on the One Health approach, which seeks to bring together human health, animal health and environmental interventions.

Under the proposed arrangement, veterinarians would have primary responsibility for eliminating animal-mediated rabies, while PHIs could continue educating pet owners and property owners about the importance of vaccinating dogs kept on private and public premises, including offices and schools.

The proposed Bill also places responsibilities on local authorities for dog registration and the management of stray dogs, including sterilisation and vaccination measures.

Dr. Nanayakkara’s comments come amid an ongoing debate over how responsibilities for rabies prevention should be divided among the Health Ministry, veterinary authorities, local government institutions and PHIs.
