Will the PSC on ‘Black July’ be just another fruitless probe?

Would a Presidential Commission of inquiry (PCoI) or a Parliamentary Select Committee (PSC) serve better to investigate the Anti-Tamil pogrom of 1983, better known as Black July?

Democratic Tamil National Alliance MP Selvam Adaikalanathan has presented a private member’s motion demanding that a Presidential Commission of Inquiry be appointed to establish the truth about the 1983 ethnic violence and recommend measures to prevent its recurrence. In response, 51 government Members of Parliament submitted on October 9 a formal resolution to the Deputy Speaker demanding the appointment of a PSC for the same purpose.

Unlike a Special Presidential Commission appointed under the Special Presidential Commissions of Inquiry,  Law No. 7 of 1978  holds broader powers including powers for Enforceability & Immunity with significant legal weight such as recommending severe consequences, both PCoIs and PSCs would be just fact-finding missions.  Yet, a PCoI would most probably be a professional mechanism than a PSC which is comprised of MPs some of whom might not be competent enough to do the job.

However, a PSC provides a say for every political party and independent group in the Parliament of the day when it probes an issue of political nature, despite the ruling party gaining an upper hand with the advantage of appointing the majority members of it.

Nevertheless, Sri Lanka has a well-documented history of establishing commissions, committees and PSCs that frequently produce extensive reports without leading to actionable reform or accountability. Successive Sri Lankan governments have relied heavily on such mechanisms which often end in shelved reports rather than actual justice.

The best case in point is the various commissions, committees and PSCs appointed by the past governments to probe the 2021 Easter Sunday terrorist attacks.  President Maithripala Sirisena appointed a Presidential committee and a Special Presidential Commission while the Parliament had appointed a PSC in 2019. Then in 2023, President Ranil Wickremesinghe appointed two Presidential committees on the same matter.

He had then suggested to appoint another PSC over a remark made by the former Attorney General Dappula de Livera. However, only the report of the Presidential Commission had produced a report that was accepted by the Catholics, the community that affected most by the horrific terrorist attacks.

Allegations have been levelled against the state armed forces and pro-government vigilante groups over the disappearances of people since the first insurrection of the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP) in 1971. However, incidents of abductions and disappearances peaked with an estimated 60,000 incidents during the Presidencies of J.R. Jayewardene and Ranasinghe Premadasa.

President Premadasa in a move to counter the allegations appointed a 3-member commission to investigate cases of “disappearance” which occurred from January 11, 1991 onwards. President Chandrika Kumaratunga then appointed three regional (zonal) commissions in late November 1994 to investigate disappearances that occurred since January 1, 1988 before appointing a follow-up all-island commission in 1998 to review the remaining un-inquired complaints left over from the Zonal Commissions.

Maxwell Paranagama Commission appointed by President Mahinda Rajapaksa on August 15, 2013, was tasked to deal with the complaints on missing persons mainly from Northern and Eastern Provinces from June 1990 to May 2009. This was followed by the Office on Missing Persons (OMP) appointed by the so-called Yahapalana Government through a Parliamentary Act in 2016 and was operationalised in 2018. However,  it is common knowledge how many missing people were traced by these commissions and the OMP.

Although President Chandrika Kumaratunga appointed the famous Batalanda Commission during her tenure; its report was presented in Parliament only last year by the present government. Kumaratunga also appointed a three-member truth seeking commission headed by retired Chief Justice S. Sharvananda in 2001 to investigate the 1983 ‘Black July’ incidents. The only outcome of the report of that commission was Kumaratunga’s apology to the Tamil community at a special occasion in 2004 to commemorate the victims of “Black July.”

The ‘Black July’ is worth investigating as it internationalised the separatist war. Will the proposed PSC on ‘Black July’ be just another investigation that fails to take fruitful steps toward preventing a future recurrence? On the other hand, will the probe open the old wounds instead of healing them?
