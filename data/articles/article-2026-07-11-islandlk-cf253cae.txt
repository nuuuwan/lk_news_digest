Building a Society Where Ability, Not Connections, Determines Opportunity

When Lee Kuan Yew transformed Singapore from a resource-poor island into one of the world’s most successful economies, he consistently championed one principle above many others: meritocracy. His conviction was simple but profound—a nation’s prosperity depends on ensuring that the most capable people occupy positions where they can contribute most effectively.

For countries seeking economic recovery, good governance, and public trust, meritocracy is not an optional ideal; it is an essential foundation.

Yet meritocracy remains widely misunderstood. Many assume it means academic qualifications, examination results, or years of experience alone. In reality, merit has no universal definition. It can only be assessed against the requirements of a particular role.

Qualifications, technical competence, experience, judgement, integrity, leadership, and personal qualities matter only when they match the responsibilities of the position. A degree in music has little relevance to becoming an electrical engineer, just as a medical degree does not qualify someone to be a skilled carpenter. However, designing and crafting high-quality musical instruments may require expertise in both music and carpentry. Merit, therefore, is always role-specific.

A genuine merit-based system begins with clearly defined job descriptions. Every position should specify the qualifications, technical competence, experience, communication skills, language proficiency, professional standards, and personal qualities required to perform the role successfully. Every qualification and claim of experience should be independently verifiable.

This principle applies equally to the public and private sectors.

A parliamentary representative who cannot adequately read, analyse, or communicate legislation is unlikely to discharge the responsibilities entrusted to elected office. A paediatrician found guilty of serious professional misconduct should never be responsible for treating children. Likewise, anyone who knowingly submits fraudulent qualifications or fabricated experience should face dismissal once those claims have been established through due process.

Appointments should therefore be based on objective assessments against transparent, predetermined criteria.

At its core, meritocracy means that appointments, promotions, and opportunities are determined by competence, achievement, qualifications, skills, judgement, integrity, and demonstrated potential—not by family background, wealth, ethnicity, religion, social status, political patronage, or personal connections.

This does not mean everyone is equally qualified for every position. It means everyone should have an equal opportunity to compete for positions for which they possess the required abilities. Without this distinction, meritocracy can easily be distorted to protect privilege rather than recognise genuine talent.

The greatest challenge to meritocracy is that people do not begin life with equal opportunities.

Access to quality education, language training, technology, mentoring, and professional networks often depends on family circumstances and socioeconomic background. As a result, societies sometimes mistake inherited advantage for genuine merit.

The solution is not to lower professional standards but to widen opportunities. Governments should invest in better schools, vocational education, language training, digital access, and lifelong learning so that talent from every community can flourish while maintaining the standards society depends upon.

Sri Lanka should therefore focus on raising people’s capabilities rather than lowering standards. This promotes both equality of opportunity and excellence—the two pillars of a genuine meritocracy.

One practical solution is the establishment of a nationwide network of Skills Development Centres aligned with labour market needs. Working closely with employers, universities, technical colleges, and industry, these centres should identify skills shortages and provide targeted training, apprenticeships, professional certification, and reskilling programmes. Their purpose should be to bridge competence gaps, prepare individuals for employment, and ensure employers have access to a competent and fairly qualified workforce.

English-language proficiency provides a good example. In many professions, English is not a social advantage but an essential working skill, providing access to scientific knowledge, international collaboration, and professional communication. Where English is genuinely necessary, it should remain part of the selection criteria. The appropriate response to unequal access is to improve English education—not to reduce professional standards.

The same principle applies to university admissions. Sri Lanka’s district quota system and Z-score mechanism were introduced to improve educational equity. However, after decades of operation, these policies deserve an independent review to determine whether they continue to balance fairness with academic excellence or whether reforms are needed to ensure that talented students are not unfairly disadvantaged.

A successful society achieves both objectives: expanding opportunity while ensuring that positions of responsibility are filled by those best equipped to perform them.

Merit is not fixed at recruitment. It evolves through continuous learning, professional development, experience, performance, and leadership.

A graduate entering a bank with a degree in physics may eventually become an outstanding chief executive—not because physics alone prepared them for the role, but because they acquired financial expertise, organisational knowledge, leadership skills, strategic thinking, and sound judgement throughout their career.

As responsibility increases, technical knowledge alone becomes insufficient. Leadership, integrity, communication, adaptability, emotional intelligence, and sound decision-making become equally important.

This explains why organisations often conduct international searches when recruiting senior executives. They seek individuals who have already demonstrated the ability to lead complex organisations, manage change, inspire teams, and make sound decisions under pressure.

Neil Armstrong’s selection to command the first Moon landing perfectly illustrates this principle. NASA selected him not only because he was an exceptional pilot, but because he combined engineering expertise, technical competence, calm judgement, discipline, emotional stability, and the ability to make critical decisions under extraordinary circumstances.

Meritocracy should guide recruitment, promotion, leadership appointments, and succession planning.

Every profession—including healthcare, education, engineering, policing, the armed forces, the judiciary, universities, and the public service—should establish transparent criteria for recruitment, appraisal, promotion, and leadership selection.

Consider a bus driver. The appropriate licence and driving competence are essential. Equally important are professionalism, ethical conduct, responsibility, and concern for passenger safety because every journey places lives in the driver’s hands. If maintaining the vehicle forms part of the role, mechanical competence should also be considered.

The principle is universal: define the job first, then appoint the individual who best meets its requirements.

Sometimes this requires recruiting internationally while simultaneously developing local talent. Singapore has successfully followed this approach for decades, attracting expertise from around the world while investing heavily in educating its own citizens.

Sri Lanka has occasionally demonstrated similar pragmatism. When the newly established Faculty of Nursing and Allied Health Sciences at the University of Peradeniya urgently required experienced nursing academics, it legitimately recruited a highly qualified lecturer from India with the approval of the immigration authorities. The appointment made a valuable contribution while local expertise continued to develop.

That is meritocracy in practice: selecting the best available person today while building the nation’s capacity for tomorrow.

Ultimately, meritocracy means selecting the right person for the right job through transparent, objective, evidence-based assessment.

· Integrity, accountability, and independent oversight throughout recruitment and promotion.

For Sri Lanka, this is more than an administrative principle—it is a national necessity.

Public appointments must never become rewards for political loyalty, family influence, or personal favour. Every public office should have legally defined selection criteria, transparent recruitment processes, independent oversight, and meaningful accountability.

Strong institutions are built by competent people. Competent people inspire public confidence. Public confidence attracts investment, strengthens democracy, improves public services, and drives sustainable economic growth.

Meritocracy is not about creating an elite class. It is about ensuring that every citizen has a fair opportunity to develop their talents and that every important position is entrusted to the individual best qualified to fulfil its responsibilities.

Only then can Sri Lanka unlock the full potential of its people and build a society where opportunity depends not on whom you know, but on what you can contribute.
