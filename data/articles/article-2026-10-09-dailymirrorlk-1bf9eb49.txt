CBSL warns weak lending decisions can weaken banks’ capacity to finance growth

Governor calls for sound credit assessments as country seeks to turn wider financial access into productive investment

Warns weak lending decisions can create financial distress and impair loan quality

Weak lending decisions can leave borrowers in financial distress and weaken the capacity of financial institutions to fund economic activity, Central Bank Governor Dr. Nandalal Weerasinghe warned yesterday, calling for sound credit assessments as Sri Lanka seeks to turn wider financial access into productive investment.

Delivering the keynote address at the Fin Lit Expo in Colombo, Weerasinghe said expanding access to finance must be accompanied by a realistic assessment of repayment capacity, clear disclosure of borrowing costs and stronger consumer protection.

“Weak lending decisions can create financial distress and impair loan quality, with consequences for the capacity of financial institutions to serve the economy,” he said.

“The quality of credit decisions therefore matters beyond the individual borrower.”

The Governor said credit could support education, business expansion and other productive opportunities, provided repayment obligations remained consistent with borrowers’ ability to meet them. Financial literacy should help households and businesses assess the full cost of borrowing and understand how changes in income or interest rates could affect their commitments.

Lenders, in turn, must undertake sound credit assessments and explain obligations clearly, supported by effective supervision and consumer protection.

His remarks placed the quality of financial participation at the centre of the inclusion agenda, as new survey findings indicated that access to formal services had reached a large share of the population.

The National Financial Inclusion Survey conducted in 2026, with support from the Alliance for Financial Inclusion, indicated that 86 percent had access to formal financial services and 82 percent had access to a savings account.

“The policy task is to convert access into participation that improves financial well-being,” Weerasinghe said.

“This requires suitable, affordable services and the capability to use them.”

He acknowledged that income and economic circumstances shaped financial choices, requiring financial education to reflect the realities facing households.

A separate financial literacy survey conducted with the International Finance Corporation in 2021 found that 58 percent of Sri Lankans were financially literate, highlighting the need to strengthen the knowledge and skills required for informed decisions.

For small businesses, the Governor linked financial capability directly to productivity. Enterprises that understood their cash flow and financing costs were better placed to assess investments and manage repayments, while reliable records helped lenders evaluate funding needs.

Appropriate finance, used effectively, could support investment in equipment, higher production and employment, he said.

Women entrepreneurs, young people and businesses outside major commercial centres required particular attention, with financial education accompanying improvements in products and opportunities so viable enterprises could obtain funding on suitable terms.

Weerasinghe also stressed that the economic benefits of digital finance depended on confidence in both the service and its provider.

Reliable digital payments could reduce transaction time and costs and help businesses manage receipts and payments. Users needed to understand how to authorise transactions, recognise suspicious requests and protect personal information.

Providers had a corresponding responsibility to maintain secure systems, disclose charges transparently and offer accessible support and effective remedies.

“Financial literacy must be integrated with digital finance and payments, MSME finance and consumer protection,” he said.

“Our objective is to connect access to suitable services with stronger financial health and the productive use of finance.”

The Governor said these priorities should guide the next phase of the National Financial Inclusion Strategy.

He also linked sound financial decisions to an understanding of inflation and monetary policy. The current framework aimed to maintain inflation around 5 percent over the medium term, providing a more predictable basis for household spending and business investment.

However, consumers needed to distinguish between slower inflation and a decline in prices.

Weerasinghe called for financial education to become a regular part of schools, workplaces and communities, with guidance reaching people when they obtained loans, invested in businesses or planned for retirement.

He said financial literacy and entrepreneurship had been introduced into the national education curriculum under the National Financial Inclusion Strategy, with support from the Ministry of Education and other stakeholders.

The effectiveness of these efforts should be assessed through improvements in financial capability and behaviour alongside outreach, including whether people planned more effectively, assessed commitments realistically, used digital services safely and prepared for financial shocks.
