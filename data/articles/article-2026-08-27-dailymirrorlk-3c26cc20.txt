Is Nanda Malini to blame for the 1988-89 bloodbath?

They called her the unmatched Sri Lankan nightingale. Some affectionately called her the “Queen of Song” or the “Voice of the Nation.” Her voice was hailed as  supremely melodious. She was praised as “a national treasure and a gift to this land.”

National newspapers printed many full-page articles about her when she passed away on August 20. Electronic media aired numerous programmes highlighting her voice, talent, and artistic excellence. They also focused on the courage she showed by using her music to fight social injustice. Together, these stories are a clear testimony to the love and respect people had for legendary Sri Lankan singer Nanda Malini.

Nanda Malini started to address poverty, systemic failures and social injustice through her powerful lyrics

However, not all those who listened to her songs celebrated her artistry or beauty of her songs. There was a very small group of people who were not prepared to value her contribution to Sri Lankan music, appreciate her courage to stand against injustice, or even respect her as an artiste.

The rationale behind their aversion towards Nanda Malini is purely political, born out of her protest songs against injustice that led critics to launch a scathing attack on her. Correspondingly, they want such arguments to justify especially the macabre bloodbath orchestrated by the state that claimed over 60,000 lives towards the end of the nineteen eighties.

While the singer was fighting for her life in the hospital during her final days, online critics attempted to discredit her, blaming her for the political turbulences in the past. Another group of critics on Facebook actively tried to discredit the 2022 Aragalaya, the youth-led public uprising against the unprecedented economic crisis which Nanda Malini also participated in. As Nanda Malini, in most cases, aligned with public struggles and anti-establishment movements since the early eighties, these groups used her passing as an opportunity to target her legacy.

A female social media activist launched a harsh attack against Nanda Malini days before she breathed her last. The attacker’s social media profile picture indicated her affiliation to the United National Party (UNP). She bluntly blamed the singer for the death of over 60,000 youth during the crackdown on the Janatha Vimukthi Peramuna (JVP) by the UNP government, first led by President J.R. Jayewardene and later by President Ranasinghe Premadasa. This has now triggered a social media debate and a bitter exchange of insults between pro- and anti-JVP groups.

The pro-JVP groups questioned this female critic as to why  regular preaching by various religious leaders has not had a major impact on the youth if Nanda’s songs provoked them to rebel against the government. This is, in a way, a total denial of the impact Nanda had on the youth. No, her songs had an impact on the people who were frustrated by poverty and oppression by  successive governments.

However, the allegation that Nanda provoked the youth to take up arms is factually incorrect, despite her ideological closeness to the JVP since the early eighties. She started singing protest songs challenging poverty, state oppression and the negative impact of the open economy only in eighties, over a decade after the first insurrection of the JVP in 1971.

Until that time, she mostly sang patriotic songs, devotional Buddhist songs, songs for children, romantic love songs and songs praising mothers.

In that case,  which singer provoked the youth to take up arms in 1971?

The most popular song among the youth who had been detained in several open rehabilitation camps and prisons after the first JVP insurrection was not by Nanda Malini, but W. D. Amaradeva’s “Rathna Deepa Janma Bhoomi.” True, Nanda’s “Udangu liyan” was also a hit among them.

Unlike the case with the first JVP insurrection, it was the UNP government of the eighties that was totally responsible for the 1988-89 bloodbath, despite it  being commonly known as the second insurrection of the JVP.  The top JVP leaders  who had been incarcerated in connection with the first insurrection were released in 1977. Then they conducted their political activities democratically and peacefully, contesting by-elections, District Development Council elections and finally the 1982 Presidential election.

The rampant corruption, lawlessness, and highhandedness of the ruling party became the order of the day during that period. The situation was so appalling that polling agents of opposition parties in many polling booths were chased away and the ballot boxes were stuffed by UNP sponsored thugs during the 1982 Presidential election.

Nanda Malini, at this stage, started to address poverty, systemic failures, and social injustice through her powerful lyrics. Her songs written by intellectual lyricists such as Professor Sunil Ariyaratne became a rallying point for social justice, youth aspirations, and democratic ideals. Her humble origin would have laid the groundwork for her concerns over the pathetic situation the common people were undergoing.

Meanwhile, the UNP government conducted a referendum to extend the term of  Parliament by six more years in December, 1982. It was described by the Opposition parties and independent poll observers as the most rigged poll in  Sri Lankan history. JVP founder leader Rohana Wijeweera challenged the results of the referendum in the Colombo District Court, putting the government led by J.R. Jayewardene in an awkward situation. The JVP became a thorn in the side of the UNP.

It was against this backdrop that the anti-Tamil pogrom in 1983, commonly known as Black July, devastated the country. However, it seemed to be a godsend for the UNP government of the day, as they implicated the JVP and two other leftist parties in the riots without an iota of evidence and proscribed them. After three years of futile attempts to get the proscription lifted and being pushed to the wall, the JVP resorted to arms, which was an imprudent decision. The rest is history.

In short, the UNP government and its vigilante groups pushed a party  peacefully engaged in democratic politics to take up arms, and then killed tens of thousands to crack down on them. Nanda Malini’s songs had no say in triggering this process. She entered the scene when the groundwork for the bloodbath was being laid by the government. Her song “Yadamin Benda Vilangu Laa” explains the brutality of the state crackdown, indicating she was in a middle of a process and not at its beginning.

Therefore, only those who want to justify the social injustice and the brutal oppression which prevailed in the eighties can put the blame on Nanda Malini and other artistes for the violent unrest of 1988-89.
