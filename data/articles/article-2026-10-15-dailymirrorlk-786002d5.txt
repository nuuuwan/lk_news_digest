Sri Lanka needs to gradually shift towards green transition

In the Sri Lankan context, integrating green transition requires a holistic approach

Sri Lanka is currently in the process of integrating green transition concepts into its already existing infrastructure. Green transition is a shift away from fossil fuels towards renewable energy, low carbon systems and sustainable economic practices to achieve net zero emissions by mid-century. The goal of the green transition is to reduce and maintain cumulative impacts of human activity within the Earth’s geological limits.

Already there are several ambitious goals such as increasing Sri Lanka’s forest cover from 29% to 32% by 2030. This is a goal set out under the Nationally Determined Contributions to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). Sri Lanka’s Long Term Generation Expansion Plan (LTGEP) targets 70% renewable energy generation by 2030 and carbon neutral power generation by 2050. The National Policy on Industrial Development (2023-2027) aims to transform Sri Lanka’s manufacturing and industrial economy.

But despite these ambitious goals it is a challenge in itself to integrate green transition. There is a challenge to overcome complex economic, technical, social and regulatory barriers prior to integrating these concepts and facilities.

In the Sri Lankan context, integrating green transition requires a holistic approach. It includes transitioning jobs, wages, upgrading technical skills and many other practical challenges for different audiences.

Sri Lanka has an unresolved solid waste management crisis. According to studies, Sri Lanka generates nearly 7,000 to 9,000 tonnes of municipal solid waste every day, with a per capita generation rate of roughly 0.43 to 0.64 kg per person daily. But green transition has a solution in the form of introducing the circular economy concept. While it happens in an ad hoc manner with certain organisations recycling and reusing discarded plastic items, it needs to be regulated and monitored.

But Sri Lanka can draw examples from many other countries such as Denmark that has adopted circular economy examples to cut down on single use items. In Denmark, a national deposit-return system recycles 93% of all single-use beverage cans and bottles. In Ethiopia, a footwear brand crafts shoes out of recycled automobile tires and organic cotton while generating fair-wage local employment.

Another practical issue with regards to green transition is the fact it would require re-skilling and upskilling people already engaged in various sectors. Even though Sri Lanka is exploring possibilities of transitioning to rooftop solar, there have been delays in connecting these units to the main grid. The same issue persists in mini hydropower projects positioned in different parts of the country.

Large scale projects such as eco industrial parks have been discussed. But Sri Lanka has a questionable history of bringing large scale projects without doing proper environmental impact assessments. As a result many vulnerable groups maybe impacted due to lack of EIAs.

Another downside of green transition projects is that there maybe attempts of green washing. With attractive press releases and large scale CSR projects, certain companies bypass regulations for vested interests. As a result they may pass as  ‘green projects’ and a complete eyewash. This might mislead people, and it is easy to do more harm through misinformation. Already there are companies with a green tag engaged in questionable activities.

A green transition is essential for Sri Lanka to cut its heavy reliance on expensive imported fossil fuels, stabilise its economy, and combat severe climate vulnerabilities. Expanding green infrastructure such as solar parks, wind farms, and community micro grids creates sustainable employment opportunities, particularly in rural and regional areas.

But in the process of green transition there needs to be a just transition to ensure that no community is ignored or impacted. Gender is a main component as women are at risk of being further marginalised as a result of green transition. Therefore, budgets and resources need to be drafted to include women.

Sri Lanka requires a green transition to correct unsustainable approaches, but it cannot be done overnight like certain policies. It has to be done in a gradual and systematic manner.
