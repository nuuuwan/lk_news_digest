Rs. 2.1tn unpaid and up to  Rs. 1tn in yearly BOI and other tax breaks test SL’s 17% tax goal

IMF names tax breaks incl. BOI regimes the primary source of revenue loss

Compliant taxpayers bear the cost of weak enforcement, IMF warns

Tax breaks, including Board of Investment (BOI) incentives, are estimated to cost Sri Lanka Rs.800 billion to Rs.1 trillion a year, while tax defaults nearly doubled to Rs.2.1 trillion, exposing leaks that must be plugged to lift tax revenue to at least 17 percent of GDP by 2031, an International Monetary Fund (IMF) report showed.

The IMF identified policy-based tax exemptions and incentives, including BOI regimes, as the primary source of revenue loss in Sri Lanka. It put the foregone revenue at close to Rs.800 billion to Rs.1,000 billion annually in recent years, or roughly Rs.2.2 billion to Rs.2.7 billion a day.

Within the BOI regime, customs oversight of zone enterprises had rested with the BOI, which carried out no inspections and generally accepted documents as presented by operators.

“The opportunities for revenue leakage are therefore immense,” the report observed, noting that Sri Lanka Customs has made little progress in taking over those controls despite a 2025 agreement, as many companies resist closer scrutiny.

The MTRS objectives pledge to rationalise exemptions across special investment regimes and to limit new corporate income tax incentives to exceptional, narrowly defined cases.

Another section of the same document, however, refers to a commitment to introduce no new incentives at all.

Meanwhile, the stock of tax defaults with the Inland Revenue Department (IRD) stood at about Rs.2.1 trillion as of December 31, 2025, when compared to about Rs.1.1 trillion a year earlier, a rise of roughly 90 percent within twelve months. About Rs.1.6 trillion, or close to 76 percent, of the latest stock was under dispute, leading the Fund to conclude that “appeals therefore appear to be the largest barrier to collection.” On the report’s figures, the undisputed portion of the stock more than doubled within the year.

Debts older than five years “appear to receive little or no collection action, even where they are not formally in dispute,” the report noted. It added that the IRD cannot write off bad debts without referring them to the Finance Ministry, and that the arrears stock “may be overstated or poorly classified.”

The IMF further observed that penalties, although available in law, are not applied consistently to late filing, non-filing or non-payment. That lapse undermines fairness, the report said, “as compliant taxpayers bear the cost of meeting their obligations while persistent non-compliers may avoid timely consequences.”

Compliance gaps extend to VAT, where the IMF estimated that around 41 percent of potential revenue was lost to non-compliance in 2025, equivalent to about 3.6 percent of GDP. The VAT lost was therefore equal to roughly two-thirds of the 5.3 percent of GDP actually collected. Income tax filing was weaker still, with 52.7 percent of corporate returns and 67.8 percent of individual returns not filed at all in 2025, while international survey data for 2024 placed Sri Lanka last among selected South Asian peers on on-time filing.

The Finance Ministry published the diagnostic on October 30 alongside the objectives of the Medium-Term Revenue Strategy (MTRS) 2027-2031, fulfilling a structural benchmark under the Extended Fund Facility (EFF). The strategy targets tax revenue of at least 17 percent of GDP by 2031, when compared to a baseline of about 14.2 percent projected over 2027-2031 under the medium-term fiscal framework, implying roughly 2.8 percentage points of GDP in additional revenue.

However, the target is yet to be backed by costed measures. The MTRS states that a fully costed annual revenue path will only be set out in the full strategy, which will “reconcile the timing, overlap and feasibility of administrative and policy measures, rather than assuming their yields can simply be added to the MTFF baseline.” The planned phase-out of para-tariffs will also reduce existing receipts and require replacement revenue.

The VAT compliance gap alone exceeds that 2.8 percentage point shortfall. Yet the IMF’s own estimate of what stronger inland revenue administration could deliver, about 1.9 percentage points of GDP after five years, would not close the gap on its own. The Fund stressed that the figure is illustrative, excludes Customs, Excise and tax policy measures, and should not be treated as revenue additional to the baseline.

Even so, the strategy leans on the revenue agencies for its early gains. “Near-term gains are expected mainly from stronger revenue administration and compliance,” the MTRS stated.

The IMF, for its part, cautioned that “with many of the easier tax policy gains already realized, the MTRS should not rely mainly on further statutory rate increases.”

Much of the post-crisis recovery has come from consumption taxes, according to the IMF. VAT rose to 5.3 percent of GDP in 2025 from 1.8 percent in 2021 and accounted for about half of the increase in tax revenue. Income tax rose to 3.5 percent of GDP from 1.9 percent over the same period, leaving VAT collections roughly one and a half times those of income tax.

The MTRS commits to “progressive tax policies” that protect vulnerable households. It pledges to assess how VAT affects households across income levels, retain VAT exemptions only where they effectively protect poor and vulnerable households, and review how personal income tax can more effectively reach high-income individuals.

The strategy, however, stops short of a target for rebalancing the tax mix, treating the direct-to-indirect composition as “a structural outcome of these reforms rather than as a mechanical annual target.” It also commits the government to compensating measures where revenue falls short, without specifying what form they would take.

Adding to the vulnerabilities, the vendor maintenance contract for the IRD’s core Revenue Administration Management Information System (RAMIS) expires on January 31, 2027. The IMF found that the planned transition to a local support model had not begun, and Excise’s security sticker contract lapses the same month.

IMF Division Chief Andrew Okello, who led the Fund’s Fiscal Affairs Department mission to Colombo from June 24 to July 7, described the publication as another important step in advancing Sri Lanka’s medium-term revenue reform agenda. “Publication is an important milestone, but ultimately implementation and measurable results will determine the success of the strategy,” he wrote in a LinkedIn post.

The report itself warned that “the principal implementation risk is a loss of momentum once the immediate crisis context fades.”
