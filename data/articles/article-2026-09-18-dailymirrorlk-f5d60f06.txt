Born after 2010; No tobacco for life?

Colombo, September 18 (Daily Mirror) - A proposal to create Sri Lanka’s first Tobacco-Free Generation could prevent everyone born from 2010 onwards from ever legally purchasing tobacco, as health advocates warn of the long-term human and economic cost of tobacco use.

The proposal, backed by the Alcohol and Drug Information Centre (ADIC), would permanently prohibit the sale of tobacco products to people born on or after January 1, 2010. Unlike the current age-based restrictions, the proposed policy would follow the generation throughout its lifetime.

“Establishing a Tobacco-Free Generation to prevent the sale of tobacco products to individuals born after 2010” is among the tobacco-control measures recommended by ADIC.

If introduced, those born in 2010 would turn 21 in 2031 without gaining the legal right to buy cigarettes or other tobacco products. The protected group would then expand with each succeeding generation.

However, Executive Director Sampath De Seram told the Daily Mirror that the mechanism to identify whether a person was born after 2010, which will be introduced by the Health Ministry, will come into effect in 2031.

He said a mechanism would be introduced to identify such persons at the point of sale by checking their identity documents or through any other technical facility to be announced by that time.

The Sri Lanka Medical Association (SLMA) first formally proposed the policy to the Health Minister in 2024 and resubmitted it in 2025. In 2026, the National Authority on Tobacco and Alcohol (NATA), together with the SLMA, again submitted the proposal to the Minister.

ADIC said stronger and more progressive tobacco-control measures were needed to protect present and future generations from tobacco-related harm. In its 2026 campaign, the organization highlighted the Tobacco-Free Generation policy alongside measures including stronger taxation, plain packaging and safeguards against tobacco industry interference.

The issue carries a significant public-health cost. The factsheet promoting the proposal states that more than 15,000 Sri Lankans die from tobacco-related causes each year, while around Rs. 560 million is spent on cigarettes every day.

ADIC’s latest tobacco-industry interference report, meanwhile, cites National Authority on Tobacco and Alcohol figures putting annual tobacco-related deaths at approximately 20,000, while ADIC surveys have estimated daily cigarette expenditure at around Rs. 520 million.

ADIC has also warned that tobacco industry efforts to attract young people can contribute to the creation of new consumers. Its tobacco trend survey has identified the need for measures to prevent or delay smoking initiation, including the implementation of a Tobacco-Free Generation.

The proposed policy is based on the idea that preventing new generations from entering the tobacco market could gradually reduce tobacco use and its associated health costs rather than relying solely on helping established smokers quit.

Sri Lanka already prohibits tobacco sales to people under 21 under the National Authority on Tobacco and Alcohol Act. NATA says its mandate includes discouraging tobacco use, particularly among children, by restricting access to tobacco products.

The proposal would therefore represent a major shift from an age-based restriction to a birth-year-based restriction, with its impact growing as each new generation reaches adulthood.

Internationally, the Maldives has adopted a generational tobacco restriction, while the United Kingdom has also legislated for restrictions based on date of birth. Other countries have considered similar “smoke-free generation” measures.

For Sri Lanka, the debate is now increasingly centred on whether preventing an entire future generation from legally accessing tobacco could become part of the country’s long-term strategy to reduce tobacco-related deaths and disease.

ADIC has highlighted that protecting future generations must also involve preventing tobacco industry interference in public-health policymaking.
