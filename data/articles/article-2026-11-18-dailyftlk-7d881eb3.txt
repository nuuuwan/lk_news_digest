2027 Budget: Navigating Sri Lanka’s pivot from stabilisation to transformation

President and Finance Minister Anura Kumara Dissanayake presents 2027 Budget in

Sri Lanka’s 2027 National Budget, presented on 12 November 2026, by President Anura Kumara Dissanayake in his capacity as of Finance, Planning and Economic Development, represents a decisive inflection point in the nation’s post-crisis economic trajectory. Framed explicitly as an intentional pivot “from economic stability towards transformation,” the Budget seeks to transition the economy from four years of painful austerity and macroeconomic stabilisation into a phase of high-velocity, investment-led expansion. Crucially, this shift occurs at a moment of profound institutional transition: the scheduled March 2027 conclusion of Sri Lanka’s four-year International Monetary Fund (IMF) Extended Fund Facility (EFF) program. Crafting a transformative Budget amidst lingering structural fragilities requires walking a macroeconomic tightrope. The

Rs. 2 trillion public capital expenditure drive while simultaneously absorbing severe exogenous shocks—most notably the economic devastation of Cyclone Ditwah, which inflicted $ 3.4 billion in estimated damages, elevated global crude oil prices driven by Middle Eastern geopolitical conflict, and the looming threat of an El Niño peak in early 2027. For policymakers, international investors, and the broader public, the 2027 Budget is far more than an annual accounting exercise; it is a stress test of whether Sri Lanka has genuinely broken free from its historical cycle of twin deficits, debt distress, and policy slippage, or whether the post-IMF era risks reintroducing the very vulnerabilities that triggered the 2022 sovereign default.

At the core of the 2027 Budget is an attempt to reconcile ambitious growth targets with the statutory discipline mandated by the Public Financial Management Act (PFMA) No. 44 of 2024. Building upon a 5.0% real GDP expansion in 2025 and 5.1% growth in the first quarter of 2026, the Government has set a bold medium-term growth trajectory exceeding 7.0%. To achieve this, total expenditure is projected at Rs. 7,673 billion (19.7% of GDP), rising to Rs. 7,933 billion (20.3% of GDP) once the Rs. 260 billion supplementary estimate for post-Cyclone Ditwah reconstruction is incorporated. On the revenue side, total revenue and grants are estimated at Rs. 6,180 billion (15.8% of GDP), with tax revenue accounting for 14.6% of GDP—moving steadily toward the medium-term statutory floor of 16%. Consequently, the headline Budget deficit is projected at 3.8% of GDP (Rs. 1,493 billion), widening to 4.5% of GDP (Rs. 1,753 billion) inclusive of disaster reconstruction. Crucially, this remains safely below the PFMA’s 5.0% legal ceiling, while the primary balance—the key metric of fiscal solvency—is projected at +2.3% of GDP (and +2.9%, or Rs. 1,150 billion, excluding Ditwah), aligning strictly with IMF program benchmarks. However, beneath these compliant headline ratios lies a significant macroeconomic tension: the elasticity between real GDP growth and tax revenue buoyancy. The Budget simultaneously grants Rs. 28.4 billion in targeted tax relief—raising the Personal Income Tax (PIT) tax-free threshold from Rs. 1.8 million to Rs. 1.95 million annually, introducing a smoother 12% intermediate bracket, and lifting the threshold for the top 36% marginal rate, while attempting to broaden the base via capital gains taxes on cryptocurrencies, telecom licenses, and gems. If real economic growth tracks closer to the World Bank’s October 2026 projection of 4.2%–4.4% rather than the Government’s 7%+ aspiration, tax collections will inevitably underperform. Because interest payments alone consume roughly 43% of total Government revenue (Rs. 2,643 billion) alongside rigid public wages and Aswesuma welfare transfers, any revenue shortfall will force immediate, pro-cyclical cuts to capital expenditure—undermining the very engine required to reach higher growth.

Unlike previous eras in which Sri Lankan public investment was frequently misallocated toward low-return, debt-funded vanity infrastructure, the 2027 Budget channels its Rs. 2 trillion (5.12% of GDP) capital expenditure allocation toward dismantling structural supply-side bottlenecks. A centrepiece of this strategy is energy transition and national grid resilience. Grid-scale Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) are slated to expand from 160 MW to 560 MW in 2027 (en route to 1,245 MW by 2029), backed by Rs. 26.5 billion for grid stabilisation. This is paired with a 500 MW floating solar initiative, a Rs. 3.5 billion household solar battery subsidy (Rs. 25,000 per kW up to Rs. 250,000), and the abolition of the 10% Ports and Airports Development Levy (PAL) on solar batteries. Simultaneously, the Budget allocates Rs. 251 billion to transport and connectivity, including an initial Rs. 80 billion to launch a multi-year railway electrification program, alongside Rs. 67.7 billion in state interest subsidies designed to unlock Rs. 174 billion in concessional credit for SMEs, paddy millers, fisheries exporters, and rural enterprises hiring Aswesuma recipients. Forward-looking modernisation initiatives, such as an AI Excellence Center, electricity tariff concessions for data centres, and Rs. 10,000 stock market portfolios for selected Advanced Level students to address the Colombo Stock Exchange’s abysmal 0.7% adult retail participation, signal a welcome focus on productivity and capital market deepening. Yet policymakers must confront a persistent institutional flaw: Sri Lanka’s historical inability to execute large capital Budgets efficiently. Chronic procurement bottlenecks, land acquisition delays, and bureaucratic inertia routinely depress capital Budget utilisation rates. Conversely, if the state manages to disburse the full Rs. 2 trillion rapidly without commensurate productivity gains or private-sector crowding-in, the sheer fiscal impulse risks overheating domestic demand, stoking inflation, which already spiked to 8.3% in October 2026 due to global fuel shocks and widening the fiscal deficit beyond sustainable bounds.

Fiscal sustainability in Sri Lanka has historically been undermined by quasi-fiscal losses generated by State-Owned Enterprises (SOEs). The 2027 Budget builds upon a remarkable turnaround across 52 key SOEs, which shifted from a catastrophic aggregate loss of Rs. 774.6 billion in 2022 to a pre-tax profit of Rs. 444.4 billion in 2025 through cost-reflective pricing and balance sheet restructuring. To prevent remaining loss-makers from eroding fiscal space, the Government has appointed an expert committee to restructure Sri Lankan Airlines—burdened by Rs. 528 billion in accumulated losses, while opening Mattala Rajapaksa International Airport to international investor proposals. Equally critical is the strict institutional firewall separating fiscal policy from monetary operations. Following the October 2026 inflation surge to 8.3%, the Budget reaffirms absolute adherence to the Central Bank of Sri Lanka’s (CBSL) statutory price-stability mandate under the Central Bank Act, explicitly ruling out monetary financing of the fiscal deficit. Maintaining cost-recovery energy pricing and central bank independence will be the ultimate litmus test of institutional maturity once IMF quarterly reviews conclude in March 2027.

Balance of Payments fragilities: The return of the Current Account deficit

President Dissanayake rightly identified the external sector as Sri Lanka’s primary macroeconomic vulnerability. While gross official reserves have been rebuilt past $ 7 billion and workers’ remittances are projected to exceed $ 9 billion in 2026, the current account swung back into a $ 420 million deficit during January–September 2026, a sharp reversal from the $ 1,853 million surplus recorded during the same period in 2025. This deterioration reflects both rebounding import demand and a surging fuel import bill that reached $ 4.49 billion through September 2026. To bolster external competitiveness, the 2027 Budget introduces targeted trade and export facilitation measures: Input Cost Reduction: A 25% reduction in CESS across 800 import tariff lines (forgoing Rs. 10 billion in revenue) to lower raw material and intermediate input costs for domestic manufacturers and exporters. Exporter Liquidity and Maritime Incentives: Newly VAT-registered exporters whose exports exceed 80% of turnover over a six-month window will qualify as “eligible exporters” for accelerated VAT refunds, easing working capital bottlenecks. Additionally, bunker fuel sold to international shipping is exempted from the Social Security Contribution Levy (SSCL) to enhance Colombo’s maritime hub competitiveness, paired with performance-based foreign exchange payouts for tourism operators. Trade and Investment Integration: Plans to expand the Free Trade Agreement (FTA) with India, explore an FTA with China, and operationalise the newly gazetted Public-Private Partnership (PPP) framework to attract non-debt-creating foreign direct investment (FDI). Despite these welcome reforms, a critical structural vulnerability persists. A Rs. 2 trillion infrastructure push combined with CESS reductions across 800 items is inherently import-intensive. Without an immediate, commensurate surge in merchandise exports, higher imports of capital machinery and intermediate goods will widen the trade deficit. Furthermore, relying on workers’ remittances and tourism to offset a chronic merchandise trade deficit masks underlying weaknesses in tradable-goods competitiveness, leaving the Sri Lankan rupee acutely exposed to global labour market slowdowns or geopolitical shocks.

Exogenous shocks: Navigating oil volatility and the 2027 El Niño

Macroeconomic planning in Sri Lanka can no longer treat climate and geopolitical volatility as tail risks; they are recurring structural realities. The 2027 Budget allocates Rs. 260 billion for post-Cyclone Ditwah reconstruction and establishes a Rs. 5 billion contingency fund to mitigate the projected February 2027 El Niño peak.  However, policymakers must recognise how these exogenous shocks interact with the Balance of Payments. An El Niño drought directly curtails hydropower generation capacity, forcing the Ceylon Electricity Board to rely heavily on imported thermal fuels precisely when Middle Eastern geopolitical tensions are keeping global oil prices elevated. Simultaneously, agricultural disruptions from El Niño risk reducing domestic paddy and food crop yields, necessitating emergency food imports that place dual pressure on headline inflation and CBSL foreign exchange reserves.

Sri Lanka’s debt metrics have improved substantially since the 2022 sovereign default. Central Government debt declined from 114.2% of GDP in 2022 to 95.0% at the end of 2025, is projected at 92.9% in 2026 and, according to the IMF’s Sixth Review—is now on track to reach 87% of GDP by 2032, outperforming the original 95% benchmark. Operationalising the independent Public Debt Management Office (PDMO) provides a vital institutional anchor for professionalising liability management. Nevertheless, the external debt service schedule reveals a deceptive calm in 2027 followed by a steepening maturity wall: 2025 (Actual): $ 3,707 million2026 (Estimate): $ 3,591 million2027 (Projected): $2,986 million (a temporary trough providing fiscal breathing room) 2028 (Projected): $ 3,574 million (a $ 588 million jump as bilateral principal moratoriums expire). Crucially, 2028 marks the end of the bilateral principal moratorium negotiated during restructuring, meaning principal amortisations resume alongside coupon payments on restructured Macro-Linked Bonds (MLBs). Using 2027’s $ 2,986 million trough to fund domestic capital expansion without ring-fencing foreign exchange for the 2028 step-up and the subsequent 2030–2032 peak when IMF EFF principal repayments commence would be a grave strategic error. Furthermore, financing the Rs. 1,753 billion overall deficits predominantly through domestic markets (within a Rs. 3.8 trillion gross borrowing cap) risks keeping 10-year Treasury yields elevated around 10.65%–11.05%, crowding out private-sector credit. At the same time, while Fitch’s upgrade of Sri Lanka’s sovereign rating to ‘B-’ signals progress, re-entering international sovereign bond markets prematurely at ‘B-’ yields would lock in punitive borrowing costs, reintroducing the exact commercial debt-servicing dynamics that caused the 2022 collapse.

Exiting the IMF Extended Fund Facility in March 2027 removes external quarterly conditionality and disbursement discipline. Historically, Sri Lanka has suffered from severe “reform fatigue” immediately following the conclusion of IMF programs, rapidly unwinding tax reforms, restoring energy subsidies, and reverting to populist fiscal expansion.  While the PFMA (No. 44 of 2024) establishes binding domestic rules—capping primary expenditure at 13% of GDP and mandating primary surpluses—laws are only as durable as the political will enforcing them. International rating agencies and bondholders will scrutinise whether the newly appointed presidential committee evaluating post-March 2027 options maintains strict, rules-based transparency. Any perceived slippage in cost-recovery utility pricing or primary surplus targets (+2.3% of GDP) will immediately widen Sri Lanka’s sovereign risk premium.

To bridge the gap between the 2027 Budget’s transformative ambitions and its structural risks, policymakers must execute an integrated mitigation framework across three pillars: Accelerating Energy Import Substitution: Fast-tracking the 560 MW grid-scale BESS rollout and rooftop solar incentives is not merely an environmental goal; it is the single most effective Balance of Payments hedge available. Displacing imported thermal power permanently lowers Sri Lanka’s structural import floor. Enforcing Strict Project Appraisal and PPP Mobilisation: To prevent the Rs. 2 trillion capital program from inflating deficits or crowding out private credit, the PDMO and National Planning Department must subject all state-funded projects to rigorous cost-benefit screening while shifting capital-intensive transport and port projects onto the newly gazetted PPP framework. Flexible Exchange Rate Management: The Central Bank must resist any temptation to defend artificial exchange rate bands in the face of oil spikes or El Niño import surges. Allowing the rupee to adjust smoothly serves as the primary shock absorber protecting gross official reserves above the $ 7 billion threshold.

Rather than passively awaiting the $ 588 million jump in external debt service in 2028, the Public Debt Management Office should execute a disciplined, four-phase liability management strategy: Phase 1 (Q1–Q2 2027) — Reserve Ring-Fencing: Establish an explicit Foreign Exchange Debt-Service Sinking Fund during the 2027 repayment trough ($ 2.98 billion), sterilising and ring-fencing excess CBSL net dollar purchases and multilateral Budget support rather than allowing reserves to be absorbed by import growth. Phase 2 (Q3–Q4 2027) — Concessional Pre-Funding and Climate Swaps: Pre-fund 30%–40% of the 2028 repayment spike using low-cost World Bank (IBRD/IDA) and Asian Development Bank (ADB) Policy-Based Guarantees (PBGs) and development policy operations. Concurrently, leverage post-Cyclone Ditwah climate vulnerability and renewable energy targets to negotiate bilateral Debt-for-Climate swaps that retire near-term maturities at a discount.  Phase 3 (Q1–Q2 2028) — Opportunistic Liability Operations: If restructured sovereign bonds trade below par, utilise sinking fund resources to execute targeted buybacks or switches, extending 2028–2029 amortisations into 2033+ tenors. Any fresh international bond issuance must be capped at a modest benchmark ($ 500 million–$ 750 million) and strictly contingent on achieving at least a ‘B’ credit rating and u global yields. Phase 4 (Q3–Q4 2028) — Macro-Linked Bond (MLB) Sterilisation: Because Sri Lanka’s restructured International Sovereign Bonds contain MLB features that trigger higher payouts if nominal USD GDP outperforms, the Treasury must automatically capture growth-driven tax buoyancy and convert it into FX reserves ahead of MLB test dates. Throughout all four phases, debt managers must enforce two non-negotiable guardrails: keeping annual foreign currency debt service strictly at or below the IMF Debt Sustainability Analysis ceiling of 4.5% of GDP, and prohibiting unhedged external borrowing for domestic rupee-generating projects.

Sri Lanka’s 2027 National Budget is an audacious blueprint that seeks to pivot the nation from austerity-driven stabilisation to infrastructure-led economic transformation. By adhering to the Public Financial Management Act’s deficit and primary surplus guardrails, prioritising renewable energy storage and transport connectivity, and introducing targeted exporter incentives, the Budget lays a credible conceptual foundation for higher medium-term growth. Yet macroeconomic success is never guaranteed by Budget speeches alone; it is forged in execution. The convergence of a re-emerging current account deficit, volatile global oil markets, an impending El Niño shock, and the March 2027 exit from the IMF Extended Fund Facility leaves zero margin for policy complacency. Above all, the temporary trough in 2027 external debt service ($ 2.99 billion) must not lull policymakers into a false sense of security ahead of the 2028 repayment step-up ($ 3.57 billion) and the 2030–2032 IMF principal wall. If the Government pairs its Rs. 2 trillion investment push with ironclad fiscal discipline, aggressive export diversification, and proactive liability management by the Public Debt Management Office, 2027 will be remembered as the year Sri Lanka’s recovery became irreversible. If discipline falters once external oversight recedes, the nation risks reliving the very macroeconomic imbalances it fought so hard to overcome.
