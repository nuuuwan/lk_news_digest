From Ethiopia to the World: What Ethiopian Airline’s rise can teach SriLankan Airlines

Ethiopian Airlines is state-owned and generating US$9.1 billion in annual revenue. SriLankan Airlines is state-owned and still depends on the Treasury. The difference is not ownership. It is execution.

It needs a proven airline turnaround CEO, a clear commercial mandate and the political courage to let that person run the airline.

Sri Lanka has spent years trying to rescue its national carrier. The result? Another restructuring. Another injection of public money. Another management change. Another promise that this time will be different. It is time to stop repeating the same experiment. The question is no longer: How much more money should the Treasury put into SriLankan Airlines?

It is: Why does a strategically located national airline still need the Treasury to survive?

Sri Lanka does not have to look far for proof that a state-owned airline can become a major commercial enterprise.

For the 2025/26 financial year, Ethiopian Airlines Group reported US$9.1 billion in revenue and carried 20.7 million passengers. It transported approximately 897,000 tonnes of cargo and expanded its international network to 150 destinations, alongside 25 domestic destinations.

The previous financial year was already impressive: Ethiopian reported US$7.6 billion in revenue and 19 million passengers in 2024/25.

Ethiopian Airlines is a state-owned African airline. That fact should be impossible for Colombo to ignore.

Because it destroys one of the easiest excuses for SriLankan’s failure: that government ownership inevitably means commercial failure. It does not.

State ownership is not the problem. State management without commercial discipline is.

Former Ethiopian Airlines CEO Tewolde Gebremariam played a central role in building the airline into Africa’s leading carrier.

During his tenure, Ethiopian’s revenue more than quadrupled, while its fleet nearly tripled.

Now comes the part Colombo should pay particular attention to.

Last week Tewolde was appointed CEO of Air India, where he has been brought in to lead another enormous airline transformation. Tewolde takes over from Campbell Wilson, who led the airline following its privatization and the Tata Group’s acquisition.

The management model that transformed an African national carrier into a global aviation player is now being brought to one of the world’s largest aviation markets.

The lesson for Sri Lanka is straightforward. Sri Lanka can learn not only from Ethiopian Airlines but also from what happens next at Air India.

World-class airline turnaround expertise is real. And it is valuable.

Sri Lanka should be looking for that level of expertise. Not political familiarity. Not administrative experience alone. Not another person who knows how to navigate government offices.

A CEO who knows how to build, restructure and commercially operate an airline.

Sri Lanka has an aviation advantage that many countries would love to possess. India is next door. The Maldives is neighbour-and easy reach. The Middle East is a major aviation market. Southeast Asia is expanding. Europe remains critical to tourism, trade and the Sri Lankan diaspora. Colombo has the potential to serve as an Indian Ocean gateway.
