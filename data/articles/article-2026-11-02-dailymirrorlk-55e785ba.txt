Caste Oppression and Struggles in Jaffna

I recently visited one of the primary schools in an oppressed caste village in Karainagar. The coastal village on the lagoon side of the Karainagar Island in northern Jaffna was hard to reach with the road in a disintegrating state.

The little over one hundred students in the school live within walking distance. As I began the discussion with the principal about the childrens’ education and health, it became apparent that the principal had a clear understanding of not just the needs of the school and the students, but also the socio-economic challenges facing the community embedding the school. The social exclusion of this community from transport access to the village, to the lack of livelihood opportunities for the families, and the bleak future for its youth troubled him. I had been going to this village from time to time over the last twelve years. It started with an invitation by a priest who wanted some of us to come see a church-run after school programme in the early 2010s, which was initiated with resettlement after the war’s end. The underlying caste dynamics were already apparent. Bus service for children to reach the secondary school was disrupted, post-war housing schemes were wanting and the local bureaucracy was not forthcoming with the public services necessary in the village. Poverty was visibly high, and older children were seeking wage work. I asked the principal about that after school programme, but he said it was no longer functioning, and that it fell apart after the priest was transferred.

The predicament of this village reminded me of the larger question of the persistence of caste relations in Jaffna. Indeed, caste like other social structures takes different forms over time, but continues to be a source of oppression and exclusion. In the years after the war ended in 2009 caste relations continued by stealth, but there have been sporadic violent incidents in recent times. It is most visible in the economic deprivation of oppressed caste villages and discrimination in social institutions.

Sixty years ago in October 1966, a tremendous movement in Jaffna defeated untouchability. That powerful anti-caste struggle and its legacy of mobilisation, and the eventual emergence of co-operatives for the oppressed caste toddy tappers, have many lessons for the role of youth and social change. Devastated by close to three decades of armed conflict, and a hopeless recovery spanning over a decade and a half, what are the possibilities for social transformation in Jaffna today? I return here to the conjuncture in the 1960s to think about how economic and political changes brought a major shift in caste relations, and consider strategies to address caste oppression and exclusion in Jaffna society today.

Historically, Jaffna had unique social and economic characteristics with highly populated small holding agriculture, where the dominant Vellala caste was also the numerical majority. This provided little room for resistance by the oppressed castes. Rural accumulation with import substitution policies starting in the late 1950s led to further intensified agriculture for cash crops on small plots in Jaffna; many small farmers claim it is from such accumulation that they could build cement houses. In this context, with increased demand for wage labour from the landless oppressed castes, who also began demanding higher wages, the upper castes turned to further repression.

Meanwhile, in the decades after Independence, the left movement  made inroads into Jaffna.  The social and economic changes by the 1960s provided an opening for the Communist Party led by N. Shanmugathasan to frame a major struggle. While issues around land – including for housing and access to cemeteries – were mounting, the Communist Party strategically decided to focus on issues of dignity and demand entry to temples and equal seating in tea shops.

The struggle launched in October 1966 was perhaps the first armed struggle in Sri Lanka’s postcolonial history; as many as thirty people died in pitched battles and murders. While the two armed insurrections in the South in 1971 and 1988, as well as the armed insurgency turned civil war in the North, all of which led to horrendous loss of life, the anti-caste struggle in the late 1960s came to an end with the concrete outcome of more or less eradicating untouchability.

In this context, the leftist Minister of Finance Dr. N. M. Perera of the United Front Government created the Palm Development Co-operative Societies (PDCS) in 1972, and provided them the monopoly of production and sale of toddy. Forty years later, when I interviewed some individuals who as youth were part of the anti-caste movement, I learned that many of them after the struggle had become employees of the newly formed PDCS. However, that momentum to address caste oppression were over-determined by ethno-nationalist politics and the protracted conflict, with the anti-caste work receding to the background.

Five decades later, how can change come to villages such as the one in Karainagar, and what are the possibilities for the youth who not long ago studied as children in that school? A few years ago, there were violent incidents in another oppressed caste village in Karainagar as well as one village in nearby Vaddukottai, where dominant caste people using thugs sought to show their power by humiliating and attacking oppressed caste people. While many of us were part of some discussions in those villages in an act of solidarity, the legal cases themselves did not move forward, as the victims withdrew.

The principal of the school above told me his most recent challenge is that many mothers of his students have just started working long hours in a new seafood processing plant in the village, which means the children get even less attention at home with  higher absenteeism of students. While the families needed additional income, they also required additional support for the welfare of their children.

For the oppressed caste communities, their troubles are inter-twined from lack of land and access to coastal resources resulting in dwindling livelihoods, exploitative working conditions when they go for wage labour, inability to access public services and often outright discrimination. They are even subject to abuse by dominant caste actors including job providers and officials.

In the meantime, Jaffna is also going through social and political changes. A nihilist culture including incidents of gang violence and misogynist attacks against women are rising; even though women are socially advancing with a larger share in higher education and white collar jobs. For most young men and women, job opportunities are few, and the availability of and the remuneration for wage labour are low.

In this context, the Tamil nationalist political discourse is kept at boiling point funded for the most part by sections of the Tamil diaspora. And even though the Tamil nationalist parties took a major beating at the last parliamentary elections for disregarding the livelihood concerns of the people, their discourse and outlook has not changed. They have nothing to say about the economic difficulties facing the people, and are silent if not complicit in caste oppression and exclusion.

The NPP Government got a solid mandate in Jaffna two years ago and they have to deliver some tangible changes in the years ahead. Providing housing and housing land in Jaffna for the landless who are mainly from the oppressed caste is a priority. But, given that almost all the land in the Jaffna peninsula is privately owned, the Government would have to purchase land and redistribute.

Job opportunities and work, including regular employment is, what youth are looking for, which requires investment. Furthermore, labour needs to be organised as trade unions are almost non-existent with little bargaining power of workers including to create a suitable working culture. Trade union work in the seafood processing plants, the few state-owned factories and the large number of unorganised shops and small businesses is a need. Activists from the community, established trade unions and the Labour Department have a role to play in organising workers and promoting labour rights, in a society more or less expunged of a trade union culture over many decades.

The progressive government in the 1970s experimented with formation of the PDCS, which proved to be a major success for the social advancement of the oppressed caste community involved in toddy tapping. It may be time now to organise the large informal sector in Jaffna where much of the oppressed caste people work, including as agricultural labourers, care providers, restaurant and hotel staff, and a range of other day-wage and contract workers. Resources for organising such workers including centres for workers and job seekers could be a start.

There is much to draw from the legacy of trade union and co-operative organising. The strategy for addressing caste oppression and exclusion in Jaffna today must draw inspiration from the anti-caste struggles of the late 1960s, but begin with creating the spaces for the youth and women to organise themselves around their livelihoods.
