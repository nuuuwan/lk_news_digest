SJB Member and newly appointed Chairman of the Sri Lanka Cricket Interim Committee, Eran Wickramaratne, said that many steps are being taken to ensure that cricket lovers in Sri Lanka can enjoy their game once again whilst ensuring that the national cricket players are well selected and corruption is left out of SLC – one of the richest sporting bodies in the country.

In one of his first-ever interviews conducted since taking over the Interim Committee, Eran spoke to Daily Mirror Editor-in-Chief Jamila Husain and explained that indiscipline among players will not be tolerated while all efforts will be taken to transform SLC and bring the national teams back into the top tiers. Following are the excerpts of the interview -

Q. Mr Wickramaratne, we first start with the new bill, the cricket bill. You know, in layman's terms, can you just explain what this bill is actually about?

A - Yeah. So, the bill is about the governance structure of Sri Lanka Cricket. There were lots of questions about who governs Sri Lanka Cricket, how people get into those positions, and how they stay on for 10, 15 or 20 years. That is not desirable in an institution, whether it is politics, cricket or a commercial enterprise.

There were also stories about financial mismanagement and wastage. I use the word “stories” because I am careful not to go by what people say until the facts are established.

Previous governments had been concerned about this, and interim committees were appointed, but structural changes were not taken forward. The new government, following representations from the public and past cricketers, decided to make another attempt at reforms.

The interim committee has a mix of people with legal, management, cricket administration and cricket backgrounds. We have two Presidents' Counsels, Dinal Phillips and Upul Kumarapperuma, people from the management sector, we have Avanthi Colombage, head of a credit card company, and Thushita Raddella, who was a chamber head, and internationally recognised cricketers such as Sidath Wettimuny, Roshan Mahanama and Kumar Sangakkara.

On governance, the foundation is the constitution. We looked at previous attempts at constitutional change and reports, including those by Ali Sabry and Judge Chitrasiri. We also opened the process for three weeks to suggestions from provinces, districts and clubs.

We then had a constitutional committee, headed by two President's Counsels, and took David Becker, the ICC's former head of legal, as our consultant. We wanted the constitution to be internationally benchmarked.

We also had the support of the Auditor General's Department and the Legal Draftsman. The Government approved the constitution, the Cabinet has approved it, and it has been gazetted. It will then go through the parliamentary process, including the opportunity for the public to challenge it.

The fundamental principle is a hybrid system. Sri Lanka previously had an unusually large number of votes, around 145, which was brought down to 65. Seven members will be elected and seven appointed.

The districts will vote for the provinces, and the clubs, which are in two tiers, will also have an opportunity to vote.

For the appointed members, anybody can nominate, but the constitution specifies the qualifications. We need people with backgrounds in international cricket, finance or accounting, management, law and marketing.

The important thing is the selection. I wanted to keep nominations open because otherwise people might feel that we are restricting nominations to put our own people in.

The selection committee is therefore important. It includes representatives from the Bar Association, Chamber of Commerce, Chartered Institute of Accountants, Sri Lanka Institute of Directors, the Ministry of Sports and the National Sports Committee.

The criteria are fixed and the selection committee is fixed. They will review the nominations and select seven people. Seven others will come through the voting structure, and that completes the new board.

A - The term is four years, with a maximum of two terms per person. So, eight years becomes the barrier.

We have also introduced a rotational principle for the appointed members. Everybody doesn't come in at once, and everybody doesn't leave at once. One member will retire and another will come in each year. That provides continuity.

Otherwise, it can become like politics, where one group comes in and carries out its agenda. In commercial organisations, continuity is important. You have a board, senior management and a team, and you can plan for succession.

Q. Mr Eran, this is all fine. This is what the government and what the new interim or transformational committee has come up with. But still, there is a clause by the ICC that Sri Lanka Cricket should remain independent and non-political. How do we explain to them that this has nothing to do with political interference, and that the government won't be making decisions on behalf of SLC?

A - On the ICC issue, we have to distinguish between independence and accountability. We have made it clear that the Government has no role in the management and administration of cricket.

There have been situations in the past where ministers interfered in selections. That should not be possible anymore. There will be no government interference in the administration and management of Sri Lankan cricket. That is what the ICC wants, and we have strengthened that principle.

The ICC knows the constitution and the provisions we have put in. They know it is much stronger than what was there. They don't approve constitutions, but we have been in communication with them, and that is also why we took the ICC's former head of legal as our consultant.

At the same time, government has a responsibility to ensure accountability. The shareholders are actually the citizens and the cricket lovers of this country. Their interests have to be protected.

If those of us who have responsibility and wield power mismanage or are corrupt, we have to be held accountable. So, we need both independence and accountability.

Sri Lanka Cricket will be under the audit of the Auditor General, and we have specifically stated that it is subject to the Right to Information Act.

Q. But how has ICC's reaction been to all the discussions you have had so far about the new adjustments you are trying to make?

A - The public are the shareholders. Everybody doesn't have to agree with our decisions, but we should be able to explain why we have taken them. If the Minister receives information or allegations, he can request the Auditor General to carry out an investigation. I think that is a fair part of the accountability process.

The ICC has been very supportive. We have always been in communication and have been open about what we are doing. They want us to move quickly to establish the new board, which is understandable.

But they also understand that there is a legal process. The constitution has to be open to public consultation and can be challenged in a court of law. Nobody can interfere with those processes.

Hopefully, by November this year, we will have the constitution as law. Then we will need to put the regulations in place, hold elections, conduct nominations and appoint the new board.

Q. So are we looking at elections, obviously, now to be held next year?

Q. Recently, I think we lost our chances to host the Women's Champions Trophy, which was the first of its kind. I think we lost a very good opportunity on the international stage as well, where Sri Lanka cricket is concerned. Why do you think we lost that opportunity?

A - Yes, so I can only speculate because that decision was taken by the ICC. We are on an interim committee bringing in changes, and we don't participate in the ICC's executive decisions.

The ICC wants Sri Lanka Cricket to normalise the process as quickly as possible. While we are in an interim arrangement, there are restrictions, including on opportunities to host ICC events and on financial support. So, we need to normalise the process quickly.

Q. We're going to talk a little bit about what the public really wants to know now. Within the years, Sri Lanka cricket, our national team, has not been able to give performances which people can remember about or people can celebrate about. People are very disappointed with our cricket teams or their participation on the international stage. What can you say about that?

A - I think people's expectations are high, and correctly so. But I don't think it is completely fair to say that Sri Lankan cricket has performed badly. The women's team has done quite well. They reached the final in Dubai, although unfortunately they couldn't win it, and the Under-19 women's team has also done extremely well.

One of the board's priorities is women's cricket. When I was a schoolboy, there were no schools playing women's cricket. Today, more than 200 schools do. Men's cricket has also expanded from around 50-75 schools to more than 1,000.

Women's cricket has some additional needs. We need to look at the welfare and protection of players coming from distant areas, including hostels. We also need to ensure equal access to the best grounds, nets and pitches. We are very conscious of that.

Q. Because we are a country which has produced Kumar Sangakkara, Sanath Jayasuriya, Aravinda De Silva, today, unfortunately, we don't have many notable names in our men's cricket. What can you say about that?

A - What you are saying about men's cricket is correct. There has been a deterioration over a period of time. In 2011, Sri Lanka were ranked fourth in Tests and third in ODIs, and in 2012 we were third in T20s. More recently, the rankings have fallen significantly.

Some things can be fixed quickly. You can remove corruption and reduce wastage. But building cricketers and creating a future takes time.

One area we are addressing is pitches. We recruited Andy Atkinson, the former head curator of the ICC, and brought the curators and five stadiums under Sri Lanka Cricket. We are raising the standard of pitches.

We are also raising the quality of coaching. The emphasis was previously on numbers; now it is on quality. Ashley Ross has been brought in, and new coaches have been appointed at district and provincial levels. We have also undertaken a review of the high-performance centre.

Sports medicine, player welfare and mental health are also being looked at. Alex Kountouris has been brought in, and a psychologist has been added to the sports medicine programme.

We are also trying to improve umpiring and match refereeing. Lyndon Hannibal has been appointed Umpire Development Manager and Ruchira Palliyaguruge as Umpire Coach.

Another weakness is our data systems. Cricket is increasingly data-driven, so we need better data analytics. But AI gives you information; it doesn't give you wisdom. Ultimately, selectors have to look at the data, assess the players and decide what combination makes the best team.

I agree with the public sentiment that we want results. But when you have a decline over 15 years, you can't reverse it in 15 weeks. It will take time. We have to put a good foundation in place, and I have confidence in the people undertaking this process.

Q. One area which the public really has basically been complaining about. And that is the fitness of the players. And also the discipline of the players. We see some stories coming out in recent years that our players have not been able to maintain much discipline, especially during tours. And that has brought shame to the cricket lovers in this country. How do we tackle this matter going forward?

A - Indiscipline has been recognised as an issue, and we will not tolerate it. The first step is to provide players with guidance, but if they violate the rules, the law and the relevant regulations will apply.

This also extends to financial misconduct. Allegations of impropriety will be investigated and necessary action taken.

At the same time, cricket is becoming increasingly commercialised, and we want cricketers to benefit from that. But it cannot come at the expense of their national responsibilities. We are developing a policy on this.

A - Fitness, both physical and mental, has also been identified as an area that needs attention. Specialists have been brought in to look at it. We also have to recognise that Test, ODI and T20 cricket are fundamentally different, and fitness requirements have to be geared accordingly.

Q. This is a sensitive question. In some other countries, cricketers are put through drug tests. Are we going to see our cricketers in the future be put through drug testing as well?

A - We will not tolerate anything illegal or anything that violates sports laws and international sporting practice. Anyone representing Sri Lanka at national level will have to comply with the relevant requirements, with their consent. If they don't consent, Sri Lanka Cricket will reserve the right to make the appropriate decision in the national interest.

Q. In the past. We have had allegations that if you are close to someone in the cricket world or if you are close to a politician, only then do they get selected for the national team. Because of that, a lot of talent, especially at the district level, may have been lost. Now you are heading the interim committee. It’s a huge task in front of you. How are you going to ensure that this doesn't repeat going forward?

A - I won't comment on allegations about the past. But I can tell you that it will be a waste of time approaching board members, senior management or cricket officials to bypass the regular selection process. We will make sure that does not happen, and if there are allegations, we will investigate them.

If there is mismanagement, we will correct it. We already have a finance committee headed by our treasurer, Dinal Phillips, with senior people from Sri Lankan accounting firms, and an international firm is also involved. They are tightening our financial controls and processes.

Our responsibility is to improve governance and fix Sri Lanka Cricket from the present into the future. If there are allegations of corruption or impropriety, there are other institutions responsible for investigating those matters. We will not get distracted from our primary task.

You are a very popular name when it comes to politics, especially as a man who maintained a lot of discipline while practising your politics, and now you've suddenly been pulled into clearing up the mess at Sri Lanka Cricket. Couldn’t you have just done your politics and enjoyed life?

A - I didn't get into politics because I enjoyed it. I was invited three times before I finally entered Parliament. I was first invited to become the mayor of the city, then to become the general secretary of a party, and finally to enter Parliament.

I had spent years in the private sector and had built NDB Bank. While in the private sector, I would hear all the complaints about what was wrong with the country and offer solutions. But when I entered politics, I realised there were other variables, race, religion and class, that had to be managed.

I came into politics because I believed that if you want to change something, you have to get into it. A minister I was advising once told me, “You haven't understood that this system works only for those who are in it.” That opened my mind.

So, I decided that if you want to change the system, you have to be part of it. You can't approach it with only a private-sector mentality of quarterly results, annual results and shareholder returns. You have to do what you believe is right and plant the seeds. You may see the results in a year or five years, or you may not see them in your lifetime.

That's how I look at this challenge at Sri Lanka Cricket. We need to put the systems in place and let the cricketers provide the leadership. Reversing these problems will take time, but if we are on the right path, we will eventually see results.

We should always be aiming to be among the best. I would certainly say that should be our aim in Tests and ODIs. With T20, I think there are still things we need to understand better.
