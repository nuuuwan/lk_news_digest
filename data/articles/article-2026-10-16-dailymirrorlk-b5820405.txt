Lassana bets a single melon can carry weight of a gift

Sampath Bank COO Deepal De Silva, Nations Trust Bank Chief Executive Officer Hemantha Gunetilleke, Lassana Brand Ambassador Anudi Gunasekera, Lassana Group of Companies Founder and Chairman Dr. Lasantha Malavige, Hatton National Bank Managing Director and Chief Executive Officer Damith Pallewatte and Commercial Bank of Ceylon Managing Director and Chief Executive Officer Sanath Manatunge

In Japan, a pair of melons can change hands for more than the price of a new car. This May, a box of two Yubari melons fetched a record ¥5.8 million or roughly US $ 36,500, at the season’s opening auction in Sapporo, a sum that has little to do with appetite and a great deal to do with what a flawless fruit signifies when it is given away.

At a launch held in Colombo on October 6, Lassana Group Founder, Chairman and Managing Director Dr. Lasantha Malavige set out to bring the same idea home. His company will now deliver a boxed honeydew or cantaloupe, grown in its Divulapitiya greenhouses and graded by hand, to a recipient of the sender’s choosing, under a concept unveiled as Japanese premium melon gifting.

“When you give melon as a gift, it’s not another gift. It’s a gift of care,” Dr. Malavige told a room of corporate heads, doctors and media.

Fruit occupies the place in Japanese gift-giving that hampers and chocolates hold elsewhere. Seasonal obligations such as ochugen, the midsummer custom of sending a present to those to whom one owes a debt of gratitude, have long sustained a market for immaculate produce sold in presentation boxes, with Tokyo fruit house Sembikiya having traded since 1834.

Muskmelon sits at the top of that hierarchy. The growers of Shizuoka’s Crown Melon have for about a century followed a “one vine, one fruit” discipline, pruning each plant down to a single melon over roughly 100 days and a single boxed muskmelon at Sembikiya is priced upwards of ¥10,000. The record-setting auction bids are a separate matter, generally regarded as promotional gestures by wholesalers who buy national publicity along with the first melons of the season.

Dr. Malavige made no secret of the lineage.“Melon gifting is not a new thing globally. It’s a very popular thing in Japan and many other countries,” he noted, adding that the Sri Lankan buyers would pay only a fraction of what such a gift costs there.

The melons come from Lassana Agri Innovations’ 38-acre site in Divulapitiya, which the group describes as the country’s largest high-tech greenhouse project. Of its 770,000 square feet under cover, 550,000 square feet or a little over 71 percent, is given over to two Japanese varieties, a cantaloupe and a honeydew, raised in soilless culture, with hydroponic lettuce, herbs and more than 100,000 square feet of orchids accounting for much of the balance.

Protected agriculture of that scale remains a rarity in Sri Lanka. Dr. Malavige has previously estimated the country’s entire greenhouse extent at under 200 hectares, against more than 40,000 hectares in the Netherlands, a smaller country that ranks among the world’s largest exporters of fresh produce. At roughly seven hectares under cover, the Divulapitiya houses alone would make up at least 3.5 percent of that national estimate.

With no bees inside the sealed houses, the workers hand-pollinate four to five female flowers on each plant and wait until the fruitlets reach the size of a duck egg, at which point all but the most promising are cut away. Only about 5 percent of plants are permitted to carry a second fruit. The discarded young melons, as it turned out, were the centrepieces on the tables at the launch.

“If we had several fruits, they’ll be in different stages of maturity, so you can’t get consistent quality,” Dr. Malavige explained, adding that the plant is deprived of water towards the end of the cycle so that the melon ripens on the vine.

“We don’t ask the plant how many fruits it can produce. We ask how good one fruit can be,” read a slide in his presentation.

However, the method carries an obvious cost. A greenhouse that cuts away as many as four of every five fruits it sets is trading yield for uniformity, which is the reverse of commodity farming logic and can only pay if the surviving melon commands a premium.

Only the top 5 percent of the harvest is considered for gifting, with the company’s chief agronomist grading each candidate on maturity, sweetness as measured in Brix, weight, shape, skin quality and aroma before it is pre-cooled and packed.

“The gift box does not make the melon premium. The melon earns its place in the gift box,” read another of the evening’s slides.

The remainder of the crop is sold in various sizes through Lassana.com, the group’s showrooms and selected Cargills, Keells, Arpico and SPAR outlets in Colombo and its suburbs. The gift box, in effect, tiers a single crop, with the top grade carrying the brand and the rest feeding volume retail.

Meanwhile, the last mile is one the group already owns. Founded in 1998 by Dr. Malavige when he was a second-year medical student at the University of Colombo, Lassana built its name delivering flowers and cakes and the melon now travels the same cold chain. He claimed that 97 percent of deliveries arrive within the slot the sender selects, with the recipient told that morning of the one-hour window in which to expect the gift.

“It’s a major pain point that we have resolved. You don’t have to wait the whole day waiting for the gift,” he remarked.

For corporate orders, a company needs only hand over a list of staff birthdays for the boxes to go out on the day, according to Dr. Malavige.

A recipient unhappy with the sweetness or crispness of the fruit will be sent two melons, one of each variety, in place of the one, while each box carries a batch number and harvest date, with the fruit good for 14 days if kept refrigerated.

A medical doctor by training, Dr. Malavige devoted more of his presentation to calorie density than to the product itself. A 100-gram serving of cantaloupe carries 34 calories and honeydew 36, he pointed out, against 350 to 425 calories in the same weight of butter ribbon cake, which makes the cake 10 to 12 times as calorie-dense. The comparison was drawn at his own expense, given that the Lassana.com bakery turns out such cakes by the hundred.

He went on to link the antioxidants found in fruit, among them vitamin C and beta-carotene, to the health of the blood vessel lining, yet took care to stop short of a medical claim.

“This is not that kind of drug or food, miracle food,” he cautioned and was equally guarded on gut health, stressing that he was claiming no benefit from melons beyond that of a varied plant-based diet.

The cultural case was left to Lassana.com brand ambassador and former Miss World Sri Lanka Anudi Gunasekara, who invoked the Buddhist maxim ‘Arogya Parama Labha’, which she rendered as health is wealth, to argue that gifts are too often chosen for how they reflect on the giver. Sri Lankan hospitality, she suggested, makes the country receptive to the idea.

Affordability was the first question from the floor, with one guest asking how accessible the fruit would be to the broader population. Dr. Malavige, who put the gift box at Rs.2,900, responded that his melons sell at about half the price seen in Thailand, Vietnam and other East Asian markets, even though Sri Lanka’s fruit and vegetable prices generally run well above the region’s, while conceding that they remain expensive next to watermelon.

“It should not be compared with any melon that is grown in open field,” he insisted, pointing to the absence of consistent quality, safety and hygiene assurance in such produce.

A closer answer to the mass market may be the 175-gram cup of cut melon the company plans to place in supermarkets “very soon”, which at roughly 60 calories is being positioned as a snack.

Japan took the better part of a century to turn a melon into a message. Lassana is wagering that a country where, as Gunasekara pointed out, even a borrowed dish is never sent back empty will need rather less persuading.
