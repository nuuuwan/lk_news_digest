Rs. 28bn in Ditwah insurance claims still outstanding at mid-year

Insurers had paid only 44.5% of the Rs.57bn in Ditwah claims by end June

Non-motor claims account for 97.2% of the Rs.28bn still outstanding

Nearly half of the Rs.57 billion in insurance claims lodged after Cyclone Ditwah remained unsettled seven months after the storm, with non-motor policyholders accounting for almost the entire backlog, as per data compiled by the Insurance Regulatory Commission of Sri Lanka (IRCSL) and published by the Central Bank.

Claims arising from the cyclone, which struck in November 2025 and was described in March by IRCSL Chairman Ajith Raveendra De Mel as the largest insurance claim event in the country’s history, amounted to Rs.57.0 billion as at end June 2026. This was equivalent to 152.9 percent of the entire claims outgo of general insurers in the first half of the year, which works out to around Rs.37.3 billion.

Of the Ditwah claims, 44.5 percent, or roughly Rs.25.4 billion, had been paid, while 49.5 percent, or around Rs.28.2 billion, remained outstanding. Non-motor claims accounted for 97.2 percent of the outstanding value, or close to Rs.27.4 billion, indicating that while the motor book has largely been cleared, the larger non-motor claims make up the bulk of the backlog. IRCSL figures released in June had shown 79 percent of motor claims settled as at May 25, against 44 percent of non-motor claims.

The Central Bank cautioned that the settlement of these claims could exert pressure on the underwriting performance, liquidity and capital positions of general insurers. It noted that large catastrophe claims may require insurers to draw on cash and bank deposits while awaiting reinsurance recoveries, which partly explains the slower growth in the sector’s deposits with banks, although these still rose 23.4 percent year-on-year to Rs.171.2 billion.

Higher reinsurance receivables, particularly among general insurers, also reduced the share of government and corporate debt securities in the sector’s total assets to 60.9 percent from 63.6 percent a year earlier, with the Central Bank flagging heightened underwriting, liquidity and counterparty risks.

However, general insurers’ return on equity (ROE) fell to 17.4 percent in the first half from 20.2 percent a year earlier, while their return on assets (ROA) slipped to 7.1 percent from 8.6 percent. Long-term (life) insurers recorded a milder decline, with their ROE easing to 16.8 percent from 17.2 percent. General insurance claims rose 21.4 percent year-on-year in the period, driven by a 40.8 percent jump in motor claims.

The risk-based capital adequacy ratio (CAR) of general insurers declined to 286.2 percent from 296.6 percent a year earlier, while that of life insurers fell to 313.1 percent from 344.9 percent, although both remain comfortably above the regulatory minimum of 120 percent. In December, the IRCSL directed general insurers to refrain from declaring or distributing any dividends for the financial years 2024 and 2025 that had not already been declared, as they had yet to fully assess their exposure to the cyclone.

Meanwhile, the industry’s gross written premium (GWP) rose 20.3 percent year-on-year in the first half, with general insurance GWP growing 21.8 percent, up from 11.2 percent a year earlier, while life insurance GWP growth eased to 19.3 percent from 25.0 percent. Insurance penetration, measured as annualised GWP to GDP, consequently improved to 1.5 percent from 1.3 percent.

The World Bank estimated Ditwah’s direct physical damage at US$ 4.1 billion, around 4 percent of GDP, which means insured claims amount to less than 5 percent of the cyclone’s physical toll, underscoring the country’s wide protection gap. Citing Swiss Re, the Central Bank pointed out that 80 to 90 percent of catastrophe losses in emerging economies typically remain uninsured, and warned that insurers could face higher claims from more frequent or severe climate events, with the Department of Meteorology forecasting a 90 percent probability of a very strong El Niño.
