How do American  sanctions on  Iran affect  Sri Lanka?

Iranian leaders know that control of the Strait of Hormuz is a strategic weapon

The Trump administration’s handling of the conflict with Iran has an apt local simile: That is how Sri Lanka plays cricket these days. Both reveal neither strategy nor conviction, and run on whims. America’s masterful mismanagement of Iran has now turned a containable threat into a global contingency.

President Donald Trump has blown hot and cold against the Islamic Republic of Iran, bombed its nuclear sites while the regime negotiators were at the table, and now the bruised Ayatollahs left with a large stockpile of enriched uranium are ever more determined to pursue their nuclear dream.

Since  Trump is more amenable to rogue leaders with nuclear weapons, such as North Korea’s Kim dynasty, the Ayatollahs would be right to believe a nuclear bomb would not only be a deterrent; it might also open doors to Donald Trump’s good graces.

The American air strikes in February killed Supreme Leader Ayatollah Khamenei, only to be succeeded by his hardliner son, and forced the regime firmly into the hands of hardliners. The war also vanquished any prospect of popular opposition to the regime any time soon. Instead of defanging the regime, America’s war of choice has tightened its grip on the population.

In 2018, the first Trump administration pulled out of the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), ostensibly due to the agreement having a sunset clause and Iran’s ballistic missile programme being kept out of its purview. However, in June, Trump sought to resurrect the JCPOA in all but in its name under the Islamabad Memorandum, mediated by Pakistan and signed by  Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. The agreement heralded a sixty-day ceasefire and offered a framework that included a $300 billion fund for Iran, relief from economic sanctions and the release of frozen Iranian assets.

The ceasefire expired in August, and the Ayatollahs, instead of biting the carrot, realised that control of the Strait of Hormuz is a strategic weapon as consequential as nukes. The Iranian demand for sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz and the right to levy tolls on  ships that sail through the strait,  through which 30 per cent of the world’s seaborne crude oil passes daily,  would shatter the long-existing consensus of maritime commons. It could also set a precedent for the other states to follow — though such a misadventure would entail a disproportionate reputational and economic cost.

Last month, the Trump administration announced ‘Operation Economic Outcast’ to ‘sever every economic lifeline’ that sustains the regime in Tehran. The new sanctions were aimed at crypto, technology, gold, aviation and shipping, and Washington threatened to remove any third-party violator from the US Dollar system.

The efficacy of these last resort sanctions is yet to be seen. President Trump has called the new sanctions ‘Economic D-Day One ’, invoking the Normandy landing of World War II.  However, the success or failure of the latest strategy would be decided far from Washington or Tehran — and obviously not in Normandy — but in Beijing, which buys 80 per cent of Iranian oil at a premium discount. China has not heeded American calls, nor does the Trump administration, which habitually bullies its Western allies,  seem to dare to antagonise the Middle Kingdom. Mr Trump recently announced tariffs on Canadian aircraft maker Bombardier, and accused Canada of treating America as a piggy bank.

Iran is in deep economic throes: the rial has lost 50 per cent against the dollar this year, inflation is hovering over 80 per cent, and Iran’s Treasury chief has claimed all oil revenue has dried up.

Yet, the Islamic regime, which had weathered decades of economic sanctions and incurred an estimated $ 270  billion economic cost of the recent war so far, has an unprecedentedly high pain threshold. After massacring 30,000 protesters in January and terrorising the population into submission, Ayatollahs and regime hardliners have little reason to fear another round of popular protests.

All that portends a protracted crisis: despite the Trump administration’s claims of total control of the Strait of Hormuz, the last ten-day average of ships that had passed through the strait was 13, which is a fraction of the 100-140 ships that passed through the strait before the war. Despite the blockade, the oil price had not yet shot up to the highs of  $120 March this year, at the beginning of the war.  Ironically, the great stabiliser of the global oil price is neither OPEC nor America, but China, whose seaborn crude oil imports have declined by 40% since February. China’s release of its large strategic reserves and the rapid shift to electrification have effectively reined in the runaway oil price.

Yet the economic impact of the crisis is substantial, and the estimated cost varies from US$ 1.3 trillion to 3.5 trillion, depending on how long the blockade continues. The World Bank has lowered the global economic growth forecast to 2.5%. And the cascading effects of the crisis on the cost of living and global food supply would hit the developing countries the worst, especially the ones already under severe financial strain. Sri Lanka’s petroleum import bill for the first six months of 2026 skyrocketed to US$ 3,168 million from US$ 1995 million during the corresponding period in 2025. Inflation hit 8% in August.

Instead of public threats, the Trump administration has reportedly resorted to private channels to implore the countries to comply with its sanctions on Iran.

Sri Lanka’s trade with Iran is $81 million, heavily favouring Colombo.

Iran is Sri Lanka’s 32nd export destination, and Sri Lanka’s exports to Iran amounted to $76 million, comprising mainly tea in 2025. Sri Lanka’s imported goods worth  $ 5 million from Iran. While bilateral trade had reduced to a fraction in the aftermath of US sanctions, previously Iran supplied the majority of Sri Lanka’s crude oil.

Colombo also currently bypasses the banking system through a tea-for-oil barter deal to pay a legacy crude oil debt of $ 250 million.  Under this agreement, Sri Lanka ships $5million worth of tea every month to Iran. Earlier, at the outset of the Iran war in February, the government announced that Sri Lanka would continue to honour the barter agreement.

The latest American sanctions target a broader ecosystem that sustains the Islamic regime, and they may not have an immediate effect on Sri Lanka, given the limited volume. However, the government should keep an eye on the mood swings of the Trump administration, so as not to risk Sri Lanka’s exports to its largest export market.

Irrespective of that, the government should prepare for another oil price shock as the Middle East conflict is set for another escalation.
