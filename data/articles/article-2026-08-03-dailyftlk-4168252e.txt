Less nodding and more prodding essential to making Boards more effective

In an era of hyper-velocity change, the Boardroom has transformed from a predictable theater of governance into a high-stakes crucible where corporate solidity, success, and survival are forged and enabled. Corporate Boards no longer enjoy the luxury of quarterly contemplation. Today, directors are thrust into an unforgiving arena where technological shifts, geopolitical dynamics, intense competition, and close public scrutiny converge and interplay in ways that are difficult to anticipate and decipher. The fundamental challenge facing modern Boards is not merely oversight. It is survival in an environment where the shelf life of a strategy is very short, and the degree of alertness to win and survive is ultra-high. Board membership is no longer a walk in the park or a retirement perk!

The collapse of Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) in 2023 stands as a textbook case of Boardroom complacency. The Board permitted the bank to operate for a year without a Chief Risk Officer during aggressive Federal Reserve interest rate hikes, leaving it vulnerable to unhedged interest-rate risk and a dangerously concentrated depositor base. Furthermore, the Board failed to challenge management’s strategy of locking billions into long-term, low-yield bonds. When rates spiked, massive unrealised losses crippled liquidity. As a bank run materialised, the Board was caught flat-footed. Their inability to bridge the gap between regulatory checklists and real-world vulnerabilities proved that passive governance is an active liability. The recent Rs. 13.4 billion+ fraud at National Development Bank PLC (NDB) exposed serious systemic vulnerabilities, prompting many Boards in corporate Sri Lanka to reassess and strengthen their oversight mechanisms. Although the bank and the Central Bank of Sri Lanka (CBSL) confirmed that customer deposits were protected and capital ratios remained above regulatory thresholds, the scale of the crisis points to “too much nodding” and “too little prodding” at Board level.

The modern Board is compelled to navigate a hyper-politicised corporate landscape. The era of pure shareholder primacy has yielded to a complex web of stakeholder demands. Directors are routinely forced to make high-wire decisions on volatile social and environmental issues. When Disney engaged in a highly public political battle with the Florida governor over state legislation, its Board found itself caught in a cultural crossfire that significantly impacted its tax status, governance structure, and brand equity, demonstrating that reputational risk is a core strategic imperative that Boards must actively monitor.

Compounding these external pressures is a structural, internal crisis created by information asymmetry. Management teams tend to filter data, presenting Boards with polished, highly curated narratives. When Boeing faced global grounding orders for its 737 MAX aircraft following tragic design failures, investigations revealed that the Board had been severely insulated from internal engineering red flags. Modern Boards are learning the hard way that relying solely on management’s perspective is a dereliction of duty.

The modern Board is facing a multi-front crisis of capability, speed, and information. To navigate this landscape, Boards must evolve from reactive compliance bodies into agile, continuous-learning organisms capable of challenging management, anticipating technological disruptions, and balancing competing stakeholder demands under intense pressure. In the modern world, directors must be strategic visionaries capable of navigating rapid digital and market disruptions. They must possess high emotional intelligence, intellectual curiosity, and adaptability. Behaving as empathetic mentors rather than authoritarians, they must drive inclusive collaboration, champion ethical governance, and decisively manage risks to ensure sustainable growth.

Regardless of whether a company is a large banking institution in London, an artificial intelligence (AI) startup in Silicon Valley, a multinational retail giant in Tokyo, a port operator in Colombo, or a small-to-medium-sized enterprise in Hambantota, every director is bound by three core legal principles, these being the Duty of Care, the Duty of Loyalty, and the Duty of Obedience. Under ‘Duty of Care,’ directors must act in good faith, with the care an ordinarily prudent person would exercise under similar circumstances. In the modern era, this includes active inquiry. A director cannot claim ignorance if he/she fails to look at dashboards, ask tough questions, or demand deeper reporting. As per ‘The Duty of Loyalty’ directors must place the interests of the organisation and its shareholders above their own personal or professional gain. This means rigorous management of conflicts of interest and a refusal to engage in self-dealing. In operationalising ‘The Duty of Obedience,’ the Board must ensure the organisation remains compliant with applicable laws and stays true to its charter, bylaws, and mission. When these duties are abandoned, the corporate body rots from the head down.

The historic collapse of Enron serves as a timeless masterclass in the failure of the ‘Duty of Loyalty.’ Enron’s Board infamously voted to waive its own internal conflict-of-interest policy, allowing the Chief Financial Officer, Andrew Fastow to run off-balance-sheet Special Purpose Entities (SPEs) that actively enriched him while hiding billions in corporate debt. The collapse of the Bahamas-based cryptocurrency exchange FTX, the third-largest cryptocurrency exchange in the world at the time, was due to the docility of an experienced and independent Board allowing a small group of insiders to embezzle over $8 billion in customer deposits through a sister trading firm, Alameda Research. The collapse of the Pramuka Bank in Sri Lanka was a result of the passive trust that the directors placed in Rohan Perera, its charismatic founder. All these were clear breaches of ‘The Duty of Care.’

The modern Boardroom can no longer be the quiet sanctuary of retired executives rubber-stamping the chief executive’s annual, medium-term, and long-term business plans over sandwiches, cake, and a cup of Earl Grey. In a business ecosystem defined by technological disruption, extreme geopolitical volatility, shifting regulatory regimes, and hyper-transparent social scrutiny, the modern Board of directors has, in a Darwinian-like fashion, undergone and is undergoing profound mutation. It is compelled to balance its traditional fiduciary obligations with a newly expanded universe of stakeholder expectations. Today, a Board must operate simultaneously as a strategic accelerator, a risk sentinel, and an ethical anchor.

As a strategic accelerator, a Board must challenge management to look beyond simple productivity gains. While management owns the execution of strategy, the Board owns its validation and long-term viability. They must evaluate the company’s competitive positioning against disruptive forces, macroeconomic shifts, and evolving industry dynamics. They must push management to hunt down and disrupt its own cash cows before an agile competitor does it for them. The ultimate deliverable here is ensuring that investments explicitly build a defensible, long-term competitive advantage, rather than just consuming capital on ephemeral trends. The Board must collaborate with the C-suite to define the organisation’s North Star, ensuring that long-term value creation is not sacrificed at the altar of optics and short-term rewards. A modern Board does not manage. It governs. It provides the steady architectural framework, the load-bearing pillars of character and oversight, which enables management to execute with speed, agility, and absolute clarity. The classic corporate tale of Blockbuster illustrates what happens when a Board suffers from strategic myopia. In the early 2000s, Blockbuster’s Board failed to appreciate the threat posed by digital streaming and continued to rent out physical movies rather than offering on-demand home entertainment. By clinging to brick-and-mortar stores and lucrative late fees, the company ignored market trends and changing consumer habits, allowing Netflix to enter and dominate the industry. They famously turned down an opportunity to purchase a nascent Netflix for $50 million!

Contemporary Boards operate in a business environment defined by permanent volatility. The convergence of exponential AI, systemic digital disruptions, and fragmented geopolitical realities has compressed decision-making timelines and inflated the cost of strategic missteps. AI is not just another line item on the technology roadmap. It is a fundamental shift in the architecture of business. In this backdrop, Boards must be AI-savvy and must establish a robust, dynamic AI governance framework that balances aggressive value creation with strict risk mitigation. Boards face an unprecedented cognitive burden governing the complexities of AI. To remain competitive, Boards must balance their ethical duty to mitigate existential risks posed by technology with intense commercial pressures to accelerate innovation.

The Boardroom is the organisation’s risk sentinel. In an era of systemic volatility, cyber threats, and rapid disruption, governance must pivot from passive oversight to active vigilance. The true measure of a Board’s efficacy lies in anticipating vulnerabilities before they crystallise into crises. To function as a true sentinel, a Board must move past tick-the-box exercises and interrogate the underlying health of organisational systems. This requires operational transparency and a robust architecture of internal controls. When basic safeguards break down, billions in value can vanish overnight. A sentinel Board ensures risk management is woven into the fabric of strategy, demanding rigorous reporting and holding executives accountable to the highest standards of integrity. However, a Board must not allow itself to become a theatre of compliance by overzealous regulators. There must be a healthy balance between performance and conformance.

A corporate Board is often celebrated as the architect of strategy, but its truest mandate is to serve as the organisation’s ethical anchor. When market pressures and short-term temptations threaten to drift an enterprise off course, the Board must hold fast. Compliance merely keeps an organisation out of court. An unyielding ethical anchor keeps its soul intact. True loyalty is not demonstrated by silent compliance. It demands the courage to challenge comfortable groupthink and welcome intellectual dissent as a vital safeguard. Authority is never truly possessed unless it is earned through transparency, empathy, and humility. By balancing a sharp, questioning intellect with the willingness to acknowledge blind spots, the modern Board protects more than just the balance sheet. It protects the fundamental trust upon which the entire enterprise stands. Leadership filters from the top down. The tone set in the Boardroom reverberates through every corridor, shaping culture and defining how people act when no one is watching. By serving as an immovable moral compass, the Board ensures that corporate character remains non-negotiable, anchoring the enterprise to principles that survive the test of time.

To deliver on these mandates, the Board must turn its analytical lens inward. The traditional archetype of the monolithic, homogenous Board is ill-equipped for a multi-polar, tech-driven world. The modern Board’s final, and most critical, deliverable is the continuous optimisation of its own composition and culture. A modern Board cannot outsource its understanding of technology or geopolitics to external consultants. It must possess native literacy within its ranks. This does not mean filling every seat with a computer scientist or a former diplomat. Rather, it means achieving a cognitive diversity where directors possess the fluency to ask management the right, uncomfortable questions. The Board must implement rigorous self-evaluation processes and proactive recruitment pipelines to ensure its collective intelligence matches the complexity of the external environment. Finally, the Board must cultivate a culture of psychological safety and constructive friction. In a world of rapid disruption, groupthink is fatal. Directors must feel empowered to challenge long-held corporate orthodoxies, debate executive assumptions, and champion contrarian viewpoints. The modern Board represents a governance environment where skepticism is viewed as an asset, and lack of foresight is viewed as a liability.

The “expert in the room” is one of the most dangerous features in today’s Board dynamics. When a Board defers to a single specialist, it replaces collective oversight with blind trust. In a slower era, outsourcing intellect to a subject matter expert was a convenient shortcut. Today, in an era of hyper-volatility, it is a liability. True oversight is not a relay race of passing the baton to the nearest specialist. It is a contact sport that requires every director to step into the arena.

To remain effective, every Board member must possess three non-negotiable traits, > Strategic Agility: Grounded directors see how isolated decisions ripple across the entire ecosystem. An expert understands the mechanism; a strategic director understands the consequence. Board members must be able to zoom out, connecting disparate dots to anticipate disruption before it arrives, > A Ruthless Risk Mindset: This is the willingness to ask the “dumb,” uncomfortable questions that experts overlook because they are too close to the canvas. It is the ability to stress-test assumptions, probe blind spots, and demand to know what happens if the expert’s “certainty” is wrong, > Street Smartness: Highly academic or technical expertise often lacks real-world intuition. Street smartness is the gut-check. It is the pragmatic, battle-tested instinct that sniffs out execution risks, recognises when a theory will not survive contact with reality, and spots political or operational landmines.

When directors rely on “expert deference,” they do not just silence dissent, they abdicate their fiduciary duty. A Board’s strength lies in intellectual friction, not harmony. By demanding that every director brings strategy, risk awareness, and pragmatism to every single agenda item, a Board transforms from a rubber-stamping committee into a resilient, forward-looking brain trust. True oversight demands a climate where directors feel empowered and obligated to challenge assumptions, stress-test vulnerabilities, and voice uncomfortable truths.

The traditional Boardroom, long dominated by the gentlemanly “bowler-hatted, pinstripe-suited” archetype, was built for an era of stability, linear growth, and predictable risk. Today, that model is dead. In a world redrawn by AI, relentless automation, and shifting digital ecosystems, holding onto legacy Board structures is not just old-fashioned; it is a fiduciary liability. Modern governance requires a different species of director. The modern Board needs digital natives, ethical tech stewards, data translators, and leaders comfortable with exponential change. These individuals do not just stare at historical balance sheets; they look forward, anticipating how algorithmic shifts, cybersecurity threats, and decentralised platforms will disrupt their business model by next quarter. They swap rigid hierarchy for intellectual agility, replacing backward-looking oversight with proactive, strategic foresight. Demolish the ‘old boys’ club before a more agile, digitally literate competitor demolishes your business. Build a Board ready for tomorrow, or watch your organisation become history.

The Board is there to oversee and guide management, not to run the business

Through all this, there is an inviolable principle to follow. The Board is there to oversee and guide management, not to run the business. When a Board crosses the line into micro-management, it paralyses executive execution, stifles agility, and creates deep operational friction. Conversely, if the Board is too deferential, it becomes an echo chamber for management’s hubris. The key to navigating this tension is the relationship between the CEO and the Board Chair (or Lead Independent Director). The Chair must set a Boardroom tone that welcomes constructive dissent. If the Boardroom culture suppresses disagreement, that identical fear-based pattern will inevitably be replicated down through the organisation’s executive ranks.

The modern Board of Directors is no longer an honorary destination for those in the twilight of a corporate career. It is a demanding, high-stakes operational vanguard that requires active engagement, technological literacy, ethical courage, and a relentless focus on long-term enterprise health. By evolving from a historical compliance reviewer into an active, strategic partner that holds management rigorously accountable, the modern Board ensures that an organisation does not just survive the disruptions of the 2020s but actively thrives within them.
