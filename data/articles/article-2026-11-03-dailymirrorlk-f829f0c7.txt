Ex-Presidency: A Promise We Keep Postponing

Yes, I am ready to stand as a single-issue presidential candidate. I will serve for six months only, within which time I will abolish the Executive Presidency and institute a parliamentary system by constitutional methods. The roots of bribery, corruption, nepotism and bad governance lie in the Executive Presidency.”

Those words, spoken by the Rev. Maduluwave Sobitha Thero in 2014, captured a conviction that had been growing for years and has never been properly settled: that placing so much authority in a single office is the central flaw in Sri Lanka’s system of government.

The 1978 Constitution was a decisive departure from the past. The Westminster model, in which the Head of State and the Head of Government are different people, a President and a Prime Minister, gave way to an arrangement that gathered executive power into one pair of hands. J. R. Jayewardene, its chief architect, had nurtured the idea for a long time. He regarded rising crime, theft, burglary, alcoholism, illicit liquor and sexual violence as grave problems obstructing development. Only a strong and stable administration under an Executive President, he believed, could meet the demands of an open economy.

He had made the case in Parliament as early as 1966, as Minister of State. “A strong executive seated in power for a specific period, not subject to the whims and fancies of an elected legislature will not be afraid of taking correct but unpopular decisions,” he said, according to Hansard. “This seems to me a very necessary requirement in a developing country faced with grave problems.”

Not everyone was persuaded. Two of the most respected politicians of the Left, Dr. N. M. Perera and Dr. Colvin R. de Silva, both ministers in Sirimavo Bandaranaike’s government of 1970 to 1977, opposed the idea. They saw it as a threat to democracy, a scheme that concentrated too much authority in one individual. When the Executive Presidency came into being in 1978, with a President empowered to act in many areas without parliamentary approval, they feared a drift towards authoritarianism and the erosion of democratic values and institutions.

Dr. Perera set out his objections at length in his book “A Critical Analysis of the New Constitution of the Sri Lankan Government”, published soon after the new charter took effect. The fifth chapter examines the character of the presidency itself. The eight chapters that follow explain how the office could come to dominate Parliament, the Prime Minister and Cabinet, the judiciary and the electoral process. Many of the abuses he described had not yet occurred in his lifetime, yet his analysis now reads like a forecast.

Perera was no casual critic. He held two doctorates, a PhD and a DSc, for work on comparative constitutional reform and parliamentary procedure, based on his research with European Constitutions. He argued that a constitution in a diverse society needs checks and balances and must seek the widest possible common ground. He foresaw a clash between a Parliament leaning left of centre and an Executive leaning right, especially where the President appoints the Prime Minister from among its members. He also pointed to countries that had adopted presidential systems and then suffered coups, political turmoil and economic hardship. Sri Lanka, he cautioned, should not gamble on an untested model by discarding fifty years of parliamentary democracy that, for all its flaws, had delivered stability and progress.

The debate, then, was always between two visions. Jayewardene’s confidence rested on the hope that the holder of the office would use great power wisely. Perera’s caution rested on a harder truth: A constitution must be designed for the worst officeholder as well as the best.

The question returned with force in the months before the 2015 presidential election. Debate over abolition gathered momentum as the deadline approached, driven by two groups. The first was determined to end Rajapaksa rule. The second, including a few within the governing alliance, sincerely favoured abolishing the post but expected Mahinda Rajapaksa to carry on from within the legislature.

Opposition parties, civil society organisations and professional bodies scrambled for a candidate, rather like prize-hunters at an Avurudu festival. Two obstacles stood in the way. One was finding someone trustworthy enough to relinquish power after winning an office of near-limitless authority. The other was finding a figure who could match the incumbent’s popularity and charisma. Sobitha Thero’s tireless efforts to resolve the constitutional crisis, abolish the Executive Presidency and secure a lasting devolution settlement had made him the favourite of many in opposition ranks for the role of “Common Candidate”.

A country’s democratic strength is ultimately measured by how it exercises power, protects its citizens and keeps those entrusted with public office answerable to the law. Elections determine who governs, but whether governance is truly democratic depends on the Constitution, the independence of public institutions and the freedoms citizens enjoy.

Sovereignty should be understood in the same way, as a relationship between the State and its people. Independence from external control means little if citizens have no meaningful voice in how they are governed, or if public institutions answer to political masters. Real sovereignty means power exercised within constitutional limits, with institutions serving the public rather than those who command them.

Experience has shown that a change of faces alone cannot deliver good governance while the structures of power remain poorly restrained. The task is to build a constitutional order that protects democratic freedoms, strengthens institutional accountability and leaves future generations a country governed by principles rather than by the unchecked authority of individuals.

The National People’s Power must honour its manifesto pledge to introduce such a Constitution. This is a responsibility owed to the people, not merely an election promise.

Nor need the work begin from scratch. Reports, draft constitutions, expert recommendations and records of public consultation already exist, and the 2015 process offers a valuable foundation. A small panel of constitutional experts could prepare a draft, supported by fresh consultations, without taking years. What is required is political will, transparency and consensus among all parties. Carefully designed transitional provisions could safeguard continuity of government. One option would be for the incumbent President to serve as Prime Minister for the remainder of the current term, subject to appropriate safeguards.

More than a decade after Sobitha Thero’s call, the question he raised remains unanswered. The Executive Presidency was built on the promise that a strong hand would take the decisions a legislature could not. Nearly half a century on, the country has had ample time to weigh that promise against its cost. The materials are at hand and the warnings are on record. What remains is the will to finish what 2015 began.
