UN’s top court finds Myanmar guilty of genocide against Rohingya

ALJAZEERA - The United Nations’ top court has ruled that Myanmar committed genocide against the Muslim minority Rohingya population.

The International Court of Justice (ICJ) at The Hague delivered its judgement on Thursday about the military crackdown that drove more than 730,000 mainly Muslim Rohingya to flee to neighbouring Bangladesh.

ICJ President Yuji Iwasawa said the court, in a 9-6 decision, found that Myanmar “has committed genocide against the Rohingya group in northern Rakhine state, in violation of its obligations under the Genocide Convention”.

It is the first time a state has been found responsible for genocide at the court. The ruling marked only the fourth time since the Nazi Holocaust that an international court ruled that the crime had been committed.

The case was brought by the West African nation of The Gambia, which in 2019 accused Myanmar of seeking to destroy the Rohingya people. It asked the ICJ to rule that Myanmar breached the 1948 Genocide Convention, order it to end any illegal activities targeting the Rohingya and ensure those responsible for genocide are punished.

Myanmar claimed its crackdown was in response to attacks on police posts by fighters from the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA).

Evidence shows Myanmar forces killed 10,000 Rohingya, Iwasawa said during a three-hour readout of the verdict.

“The Court finds that Myanmar’s security forces killed thousands of Rohingya ⁠⁠civilians, perpetrated widespread acts ⁠⁠of sexual violence as well as acts of ⁠⁠extreme violence against children and ⁠⁠attacked fleeing Rohingya ⁠⁠as they sought to flee in large numbers to ‌‌Bangladesh,” he added.

In specific cases that he referred to, he said Myanmar had acted with “genocidal intent”.

The ICJ said that the “only reasonable inference” from evidence of widespread atrocities by Myanmar security forces is that they had the intent to destroy a “substantial part” of the Rohingya minority.

It said Myanmar should have known that there was a risk of genocide when armed civilians participated in the military campaign. “Myanmar thus violated its obligation to prevent genocide,” it added.

Myanmar is now obliged to provide reparations and restitution “as a result of Myanmar’s wrongful acts”, Iwasawa said.

The court said appropriate restitution by Myanmar would include: Facilitating the safe and voluntary return of Rohingya; returning or replacing all collective property damaged; facilitating the search for the disappeared and ensuring proper burials; providing for the physical and psychological rehabilitation of those mentally injured; and ensuring the population’s protection from persecution.

The court established that at least four genocidal acts have been proven, said Al Jazeera’s Step Vaessen, reporting from the Hague. They are the mass killing of civilians; the forced displacement of hundreds of thousands of people; serious harm inflicted upon the group; and the prevention of birth.

The Rohingya have long been treated as second-class citizens, well before 2017, according to the judge, Vaessen said. They were also forced to take part in a birth control programme, she added.

“So the judge really saw this as a very strong explanation that … a genocidal act was committed there.”

The verdict is “extremely important for the Rohingya,” our correspondent said. “They’ve been waiting for 10 years for any kind of justice, but it’s also important for the whole international community because genocide is such a difficult act to prove.”

The hardest part of any genocide case is proving specific intent to destroy a group in whole or in part – an extremely high bar – said Toby Cadman, an international human rights lawyer. He noted that the ICJ had never before ruled that a state has specific intent to commit genocide.

“The court is guided by the genocide convention and what the genocide convention states and it is a high threshold,” Cadman told Al Jazeera.

Human rights organisations welcomed the judgement. “We hope this ruling marks the beginning of a long overdue process of accountability in Myanmar,” Amnesty International said in a statement. It added that all crimes under international law “must be effectively investigated and those suspected of responsibility brought to justice”.

Cadman said that the situation does not come to an end with the ICJ’s ruling and a lot will depend on what comes next. This includes whether the issue goes before the UN Security Council and whether there are sanctions, or other remedies that the court can order to ensure it is enforced.

More than 1.3 million Rohingya remain displaced across South and Southeast Asia, including 1.2 million in sprawling refugee camps in Cox’s Bazar in Bangladesh, according to the UN. Conflict, shrinking humanitarian aid and dangerous sea crossings continue to leave many members of the community displaced and vulnerable.
