Decision to extend the retirement age of judges: A well-meaning policy implemented in Gotabaya style

The arbitrary extension of the retirement ages of judges, in the absence of any meaningful consultation, suggests its decision is no less tailor-made for narrow political ends than Gotabaya Rajapaksa’s 20th amendment to the Constitution

The opposition to the government’s proposal to increase the retirement ages of higher appellate court judges reveals a bitter irony of Sri Lankan politics. It is a classic case of how even well-meaning policies could be politicised to extreme ends–– or, worse still, the same seemingly well-intentioned measures could be nothing more than haphazard choices of political expediency and guile.

The Cabinet last week approved extending the retirement age of all judges by two years. The Justice Minister, Harshana Nanayakkara, touted the decision as part of a broader structural reform package to clear an existing backlog of 1.1 million court cases. The Cabinet paper titled “Make the Judicial Process More Efficient and Formal,” envisaged two parallel pieces of draft legislation to extend the retirement of judges across all courts.

First, a Constitutional Amendment Bill will extend the retirement ages of Supreme Court Judges from 65 to 67 and the Appeal Court judges from 63 to 65.

A second bill–The Judicial Act Amendment Bill stipulates extending the retirement of all lower court judges, similarly by two years. That would see the retirement age of High Court judges ( now 61) increased to 63 and District Court and Magistrate Court judges  (now 60) increased to 62.

The two amendments were a judicial necessity, for the retirement ages of higher appellate court judges are hardcoded into Article 107 (5) of the Constitution and have remained unchanged since 1978. Any changes require a constitutional amendment. Conversely, the retirement ages of lower court judges are governed by Statutory Law under the Judicature Act.

Therefore, the insinuation that the government’s decision is concerned only with the Supreme Court (and the Appeal Court) is manifestly false. However, the opposition to the proposed legislation has only highlighted the impact on the Higher Appellate Court, willfully ignoring its across-the-board application. Such selective reasoning is disingenuous and, by extension, descends into disinformation.

Second, it is fair to argue that the extension of the retirement age of the judges of Higher Appellate Courts would not necessarily solve the mammoth 1.1 million unresolved court cases in the country. According to the Ministry of Justice, 818,000 cases are stuck in the magistrate courts, compared to only 5000 cases in the Supreme Court. The Sri Lankan judicial sector suffers from crippling staff shortages. A total of 2,647 vacancies in the judicial service are documented by the Ministerial Consultative Committee on Justice, the vast majority of which exist in clerical work, ranging from stenographers, typists and interpreters. Similarly, the government analyst’s department is beset by staff shortage, antiquated equipment and persistent delays, contributing to the slow pace in the judicial process.

The recent interviews for 50 magistrate trial position manged to recruit only 33, in part owing to lower remuneration. The international benchmark for an efficient judiciary places 50 judges for every 1 million people. Sri Lanka only has 20 judges per every 1 million.  The middle-income country average is 65 judges per million, and advanced developed economies have an average of 150 to 220 judges per million people, according to data from the Council of Europe.

Therefore, it is no brainer that without sector-wide reforms, modernisation and efficiency gains,  the extension of retirement ages of Higher Appellate Court judges would have minimal impact on the efficiency of the judicial process.

One might also add the current status of the judiciary sector is the accumulated effect of decades-long neglect, and one should not blame the party that tries to clear the rot.

The government decision has been opposed not only at home, vehemently by the Bar Association of Sri Lanka, the Judicial Services Association of Sri Lanka, which represents district court judges and magistrates, the political opposition and, according to the president of the Bar Association, Rajiv Amarasuriya, also Mahanayakas, who had written to the president.

Similarly, the decision has brought rebuke from international judicial bodies, including the French National Bar (the retirement age of whose senior judges of the Court de Cassation, equivalent of the Sri Lankan Supreme Court, is 70 years) and the Commonwealth Lawyers Association.

However, the government decision is more in line with international norms than it was presented. Of the Commonwealth countries, the statutory retirement ages of all judges in the UK were increased in 2020 from 70 to 75. In Canada, the Supreme Court and upper-tier judges serve until 75. In Australia, the Federal and High Court judges must retire at age 70 and in New Zealand, too, it is 70.

So far so good. The government’s decision across all these counts appears well-intentioned.

Then alas, it fails a crucial smell test. The arbitrariness and unholy rush to push through a constitutional amendment, after 18 months in power, and having done nothing, suggests that the decision is tailor-made to the extension of the retirement age of the incumbent chief justice, who is due to retire in December should the government fail to pass the amendment.  His retirement could then lead to the potential appointment of another Supreme Court judge, whom the government consider as sympathetic to the deposed Rajapaksa camp. The mad rush for a constitutional amendment is to avert that eventuality.

A way out for the government and to demonstrate its sincerity would be to proceed with the amendment after December or explicitly preclude the current Supreme Court judges from the impending amendment. Whether that would serve the government’s purpose is open to question.

That could, however, save the judiciary from being viewed yet again as an instrument of the party in political power, rather than one of the three pillars of the state that checks the power of the legislature and executive. The independence of the judiciary could not be sustained without public confidence in it. However, the government decision, if proceeded without extensive deliberation with the stakeholders, would risk fostering public perception of the judiciary as influenced and compromised– or worse still, creating plausible judiciary dependence on the government.

The Bar Association of Sri Lanka and others have observed selective interest in the government, observing that 4 vacancies in the Supreme Court (out of 17 authorised capacity) and 4 vacancies of the Court of Appeal (out of 20 authorised capacity) have not been filled for long. However, that enhanced number of the Higher Appellate Courts was also a result of another highly controversial 20th amendment to the Constitution, which hyper-centralised the powers of the state at the office of the Presidency by then President Gotabaya Rajapaksa. Another feature was the revoking of the ban on dual citizenship to enable the political return of Basil Rajapaksa. The same amendment also increased the number of Supreme Court judges from 11 to 17 and Appeal Court judges from 12 to 20 in a not-so-covert move to cultivate a patronage system extending to the highest branches of the judiciary. The current government, which pledged ‘a system change’, seemed to replicate the same system. The arbitrary extension of the retirement ages of judges, in the absence of any meaningful consultation, suggests its decision is no less tailor-made for narrow political ends than Gotabaya Rajapaksa’s 20th amendment to the Constitution.
