Balancing Security and Rights:  The Evolution of Sri Lanka’s Anti-Terrorism Framework

While the memory of the 2019 Easter Sunday attacks drives strong support for heightened security, there is an equally strong demand for the protection of democratic values (Photo credit Ishara S. Kodikara AFP).

As Sri Lanka navigates the complex landscape of national security in a modern, digitised world, the legal mechanisms designed to combat terrorism are undergoing a pivotal transformation. The transition from the long-standing Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA) to the proposed Anti-Terrorism Act (PSTA) represents a critical juncture in the nation’s pursuit of a framework that effectively addresses extremist threats while upholding the fundamental pillars of human rights and the rule of law.

For decades, the PTA has served as the primary instrument for addressing terrorism, yet it has been frequently criticised for its broad, often vague definitions of terrorist offenses. These ambiguities have historically provided authorities with significant discretionary power, leading to concerns regarding arbitrary application.

The proposed PSTA aims to rectify these flaws by introducing a more precise delineation of terrorism-related offenses. By focusing on acts with a clear impact on national security and public order, the PSTA seeks to minimise the potential for misuse. Crucially, the new framework recognises the realities of the digital age, incorporating provisions to address cyberterrorism and online radicalisation, thereby modernising the state’s response to contemporary challenges.

Perhaps the most significant departures from the status quo lie in the procedural changes regarding detention and confession admissibility. The PTA has long been criticised for allowing detention for up to 18 months without trial, a practice that has fostered a culture of uncertainty.

The PSTA introduces a shift toward rigorous judicial oversight. Under the new proposal, detention orders must be based on credible evidence and are subject to strict, court-monitored timelines. Furthermore, the admissibility of confessions is being overhauled; the PSTA mandates that confessions are only valid if made voluntarily in the presence of a legal representative. To ensure transparency, the act emphasises the use of video-recorded interrogations, a move designed to eliminate the risks of coercion and torture that have plagued the enforcement of the PTA.

Judicial oversight is similarly strengthened. Where the PTA allowed for significant law enforcement latitude without prior court approval, the PSTA requires judicial authorization for key actions, including surveillance and detention. This systemic change is aimed at restoring public trust by ensuring that security measures remain subject to independent legal review.

The debate surrounding the PSTA is not merely technical—it is deeply philosophical. Proponents argue that the modernisation of these laws is essential for preemptive action against evolving threats. By streamlining intelligence gathering and inter-agency coordination, the PSTA promises a more proactive security posture.

However, civil liberties advocates remain cautious. The expansive powers granted to the state, even with improved oversight, raise concerns about potential infringements on privacy and freedom of expression. There is a palpable fear that without robust checks and balances, these new tools could be used to suppress dissent rather than to counter genuine threats. Legal experts emphasise that the success of the PSTA will depend not just on the text of the law, but on its implementation and the prevention of discriminatory enforcement, particularly against marginalised communities.

Public opinion remains divided. While the memory of the 2019 Easter Sunday attacks drives strong support for heightened security, there is an equally strong demand for the protection of democratic values. The media and civil society organisations have become vital conduits for this national conversation, pushing for a narrative that views safety and freedom as complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

To ensure that the PSTA achieves its objectives without compromising the rule of law, legal analysts propose several key recommendations:    Narrow  Definitions: Maintain  strict and precise definitions of terrorism to avoid the criminalization  of legitimate dissent.  Mandatory  Due Process: Ensure  that access to legal counsel is an absolute right from the moment of  arrest, and that detention remains subject to constant, independent  judicial review.  Ethical  Enforcement: Prioritise  training for law enforcement on ethical interrogation techniques to ensure  the integrity of evidence.  Inclusive  Consultation: Engage  continuously with civil society and legal bodies to ensure the law evolves  in tandem with societal needs and human rights standards.

As Sri Lanka moves forward, the PSTA stands as an ambitious attempt to balance the necessity of national defense with the sanctity of individual rights. The true test of this legislation will lie in its application—whether it can provide the security the nation demands while steadfastly honoring the freedoms that define a democratic society.

In the meantime, the current government should remember its election promise to repeal the Prevention of Terrorism Act (PTA) and similar laws. As the main stakeholder of the NPP government, JVP members suffered greatly under the PTA and experienced the dark side of this Act. If this government does not repeal the PTA and refrains from introducing a similar act like the PSTA, such a change is unlikely to ever occur under any future government.

Therefore, they should consider how to improve the legal system to address the challenges of terrorism without introducing separate acts like the PSTA. Several countries have transitioned away from standalone emergency or special terrorism legislation, opting instead to integrate terrorism-related offenses into their broader criminal codes to ensure consistency with standard rule-of-law principles. Here are some examples:

The United Kingdom: While the UK maintains a Terrorism Act, it has frequently been criticised for its “exceptional” nature. In response to long-standing legal challenges, the UK has increasingly sought to align its counter-terrorism powers with the Human Rights Act 1998. Recent reforms have focused on bringing detention standards and investigative powers under the oversight of the Courts and the Regulation of Investigatory Powers Act (RIPA), attempting to move these actions closer to the procedural requirements of standard criminal procedure.

Canada:  Following the expiration of the Anti-terrorism Act (which was enacted post-9/11 as a temporary measure), the Canadian government integrated permanent counter-terrorism provisions directly into the Criminal Code of Canada. By doing so, Canada ensured that terrorism offenses are prosecuted within the same judicial framework as other major crimes, subjecting them to the same constitutional standards, rules of evidence, and procedural safeguards provided by the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

Australia: Australia has moved toward consolidating its security laws. While it maintains a robust set of counter-terrorism offenses, the legislative trend has been to ensure that these offenses sit within the Criminal Code Act 1995. This integration allows the judiciary to apply established principles of criminal liability, such as the requirement of mens rea (intent), in a manner consistent with non-terrorism related criminal prosecution.

New Zealand: After the 2019 Christchurch attacks, New Zealand reviewed its counter-terrorism framework. Rather than creating a sprawling, independent “special” act that exists outside the standard legal system, the government focused on amending the Terrorism Suppression Act 2002 to better interface with the Crimes Act 1961. This approach ensures that when the state prosecutes terrorism, it does so use the standard, transparent procedures of the general criminal legal system, thereby preventing the creation of a “parallel” justice system.

Norway: Following the 2011 attacks, Norway resisted the urge to enact “special” emergency terrorism legislation. Instead, the government utilised existing provisions within the Norwegian Penal Code. By relying on standard criminal law to prosecute terrorist acts, Norway maintained the integrity of its judicial system, ensuring that the accused were afforded the same constitutional protections, transparency, and due process as any other defendant, reinforcing that terrorism is a criminal act to be dealt with by the ordinary courts.

All these examples prove that the improvements made to existing criminal litigation frameworks allow countries to litigate terrorism effectively. However, if a country has a hidden agenda to suppress its political opposition, that is the main reason for introducing such anti-terrorism legislation.The pursuit of democracy, the rule of law, and good governance is essential for a stable society. Many advocates for these principles argue that a transparent and accountable government is the most effective framework for addressing complex security challenges, such as terrorism and organised crime.

(The Writer is a Human Rights Activist and Strategy Advisor to International Organisations on Protection of Human Rights  and Right Based Development Approaches).
