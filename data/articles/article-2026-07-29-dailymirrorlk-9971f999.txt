Are we governing for the next election,  or the next generation?

Every government begins its term with a familiar list of urgent priorities. The economy needs attention. Public services demand improvement. The cost of living weighs heavily on households, while businesses seek stability and confidence to invest. These are real and immediate concerns, and no responsible government can afford to ignore them. But there is another question that deserves equal attention, even if it rarely dominates political debate. Are the decisions being made today designed simply to win the next election, or are they laying the foundations for the next generation?

Sri Lanka has emerged from one of the most difficult periods in its post-independence history. Inflation has eased, foreign reserves have improved and the economy has regained a measure of stability that once seemed out of reach. Those achievements should not be underestimated. They reflect painful adjustments and sacrifices made by citizens across the country. Yet stability, important as it is, should never become the final goal.

The next stage of the country’s journey demands a different kind of leadership. Recovering from a crisis requires decisive action. Building a prosperous nation requires the willingness to think beyond the next five years.

But too often, national priorities have shifted with changes in political leadership. Long-term reforms have been abandoned before they had the chance to produce results. Whether it is education, transport, agriculture or economic policy, the country has repeatedly struggled to maintain continuity. This cycle comes at a cost. Businesses hesitate when policies change without warning. Investors prefer countries where long-term plans survive political transitions. Citizens too, lose confidence.

Education offers perhaps the clearest example. The world of work is changing faster than ever before. Technology is transforming industries, artificial intelligence is reshaping workplaces and employers increasingly value practical skills alongside academic qualifications. Yet many young Sri Lankans continue to graduate without the knowledge or experience demanded by a modern economy. Reforming education requires a long-term national commitment that extends well beyond any single government’s term in office.

The labour market tells a similar story. Employers in several industries report growing difficulty in finding skilled workers, even as many young Sri Lankans continue to leave the country in search of better opportunities. When skilled workers, entrepreneurs and professionals increasingly believe their future lies elsewhere, it raises uncomfortable questions about the country’s own ability to create opportunity at home.

The demographic picture makes the challenge even more pressing. Sri Lanka’s population is ageing, birth rates are declining and the workforce will gradually become smaller. These changes may unfold quietly, but their impact will be profound. Healthcare, pensions, productivity and economic growth will all be affected. Preparing for that future requires policies that look decades ahead.

The same principle applies to infrastructure. Development is not measured only by new highways or impressive buildings. Reliable public transport, efficient digital services, modern hospitals, dependable energy supplies and strong water management systems are equally important. These investments rarely produce immediate political dividends, but they shape the quality of life and economic competitiveness of a country for generations.

Climate resilience deserves the same long-term thinking. Responding after disasters occur is no longer enough. Planning ahead is not optional; it is essential.

Perhaps the greatest challenge, however, lies in strengthening public institutions. Strong institutions provide continuity when governments change. They give citizens confidence and reassure investors that policies will remain predictable. None of this means that governments should neglect the immediate concerns of ordinary people. Families struggling to meet monthly expenses cannot be expected to wait years for the benefits of reform. The cost of living, employment and public services require urgent attention.

Sri Lanka has already shown remarkable resilience. The question now is whether the country will settle for recovery or aim for something more lasting.

History rarely remembers governments for short-term political victories. It remembers those that strengthened institutions, invested in people and made choices that continued to benefit the country long after they had left office.

That is the challenge before Sri Lanka today. The decisions made now will shape the opportunities available not only to today’s citizens but also to the children who will inherit this country in the decades ahead.
