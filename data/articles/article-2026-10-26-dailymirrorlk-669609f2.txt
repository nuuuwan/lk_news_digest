Social Harmony as a subject in school

Sri Lanka has won many battles throughout its history, but one of its biggest challenges remains defeating the racism that has divided its people. The Education Ministry’s proposed introduction of Social Harmony as a subject for senior students in state schools, with the subject becoming compulsory for Advanced Level students, offers an opportunity to address this problem at its roots.

Under the leadership of Prime Minister and Education Minister Dr. Harini Amarasuriya, this is a welcome initiative that could help shape a generation that understands a simple but important truth that no person is superior or inferior because of their religion, ethnicity or culture.

The proposal is expected to include practical activities and community projects that bring children of different backgrounds together. If implemented properly, it could achieve something that political speeches and public declarations have repeatedly failed to deliver: Helping children understand and respect one another through experience.

Sri Lanka has paid a heavy price for allowing communal divisions to grow. Politicians have often found it convenient to exploit ethnic and religious sentiments to win votes, while certain religious leaders have used their influence to create suspicion and hostility towards other communities. The consequences have been devastating.

The Black July riots of 1983 remains one of the darkest chapters in the country’s history. Thousands were killed, businesses were destroyed and families were forced to flee. The country then endured a civil war lasting nearly three decades, leaving deep wounds among communities.

The war against the LTTE ended in 2009, but peace cannot be measured simply by the absence of fighting. True reconciliation requires people to feel safe, respected and accepted, regardless of their identity. That is a lesson Sri Lanka must teach its children if it hopes to avoid repeating the mistakes of the past.

Education has a crucial role to play. Children who learn about other communities through stereotypes, rumours and divisive political rhetoric can grow up fearing people they barely know.

Imagine Buddhist, Hindu, Muslim and Christian students working together on a community project. They may speak different languages at home, celebrate different festivals and follow different traditions. Yet they can share the same classroom, solve common problems and work towards the same goal. That experience can teach a lesson no textbook alone can deliver

Social Harmony must therefore be more than another subject students memorise to pass an examination. Schools should organise environmental projects, community service, cultural exchanges and visits to places of worship. Students should learn about different faiths, the contributions of different communities to Sri Lanka and the dangers of misinformation, hate speech and discrimination.

They must also learn how to disagree without insulting another person’s beliefs. However, the success of this initiative will depend on what happens beyond the classroom. It would be pointless to teach children that all people are equal if they hear adults spreading hatred against other communities at home or see public figures using religion and ethnicity to divide society.

Politicians and religious leaders must therefore support this effort through their own conduct. A curriculum promoting tolerance cannot succeed if influential figures continue to encourage prejudice.

The Government must ensure that teachers receive proper training and that the curriculum is balanced, practical and sensitive to Sri Lanka’s history. No community should be portrayed as superior, and no child should feel that their identity is being dismissed.

There must also be accountability when discrimination occurs. Teaching tolerance while ignoring bullying, communal insults or unfair treatment in schools would undermine the entire exercise.

Sri Lanka young people who question prejudice, reject discrimination and refuse to be manipulated by those seeking political advantage through communal hatred.

Introducing this subject will not erase decades of mistrust overnight. But it offers an opportunity to challenge prejudice before it becomes deeply rooted and to build understanding through shared experiences.

If implemented with commitment, Social Harmony could be an important step towards that goal. The children who learn to stand together today may become the adults who refuse to let racism divide Sri Lanka tomorrow. That would be a victory worth celebrating.
